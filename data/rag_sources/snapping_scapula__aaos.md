# Scapular (Shoulder Blade) Disorders

## Overview
The scapula (shoulder blade) is a bone, shaped like a triangle, on the upper back. It's supported by muscles that enable arm movement. When these muscles weaken or become imbalanced, the scapula's position can shift, potentially causing arm movement difficulties, weakness, and shoulder joint misalignment.

Treatment typically involves physical therapy targeting muscle strengthening and restoration of proper scapular positioning.

## Anatomy
The shoulder functions as a ball-and-socket joint where the head of the humerus (upper arm bone) is the ball, and the scapula (shoulder blade) forms the socket.

Multiple muscle and ligament attachments connect the scapula and arm to the body. The acromion connects to the clavicle through the acromioclavicular joint. During arm movement, the scapula must move correspondingly to maintain the ball and socket in normal alignment.

## Description
Scapular disorders involve changes in either resting position or motion patterns. The medical term is scapular dyskinesis ("dys" = alteration of, "kinesis" = movement).

Alterations typically become visible from behind, where the medial (inner) border of the affected shoulder blade will appear more prominent. This prominence increases during arm movement, commonly called a "winged" scapula.

## Causes
Scapular dyskinesis results from:
- Weakness, imbalance, tightness, or detachment of controlling muscles
- Nerve injuries supplying the muscles
- Bone injuries or shoulder joint injuries

## Symptoms
- Pain/tenderness around the scapula, especially top and medial border
- Arm weakness or fatigue during vigorous use
- Fatigue with repetitive, especially overhead, activities
- Limited range of motion above shoulder height
- Crunching or snapping sounds during movement
- Noticeable scapular protrusion or "winging"
- Drooped or forward-tilted posture on affected side

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
Doctors assess medical history and examine the entire shoulder and scapula for injury, weakness, or tightness.

**Visual observation:** The physician compares the affected scapula to the opposite side from behind, sometimes asking patients to perform arm movements with or without light weights, or wall push-ups to reveal weakness and abnormal patterns.

**Manual muscle testing:** Strength assessment of shoulder and scapular muscles determines if weakness contributes to abnormal motion.

**Corrective maneuvers:**
- **Scapular assistance test (SAT):** The doctor applies gentle upward pressure to the scapula as the patient elevates their arm. If symptoms are relieved and the arc of motion is increased, it is an indication that the muscles are not strong enough to raise the arm.
- **Scapular retraction test (SRT):** The physician tests arm strength with the extended arm, then manually retracts the scapula and retests. In dyskinesis, muscle strength will improve when the shoulder blade is retracted.
- **Special tests:** Additional tests evaluate the shoulder blade based on examination findings.

### Imaging Tests
Imaging isn't always necessary for diagnosis. However, the doctor may order imaging, such as an X-ray, computed tomography (CT) scan, or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan, if bony abnormalities or other shoulder injuries are suspected. For suspected nerve injuries, nerve conduction studies assess nerve function.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
In almost all cases, the symptoms of scapular dyskinesis will improve with nonsurgical treatment.

- **NSAIDs:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs like ibuprofen and naproxen can help relieve pain and swelling.
- **Physical therapy:** Customized exercise programs focus on strengthening scapular stabilizing and moving muscles, and stretching tight muscles limiting motion.
- **Heat therapy:** Hot baths or heating pads may relieve shoulder muscle tightness.
- **Additional measures:**
  - Good posture: pull your shoulder blades back together, and bend your elbows down and back
  - Balanced exercise: match upper body strength sessions appropriately (1 fly and 2 rows per press set)
  - Stretching: include front shoulder and rotation exercises

Consult your doctor if symptoms don't improve.

### Surgical Treatment
Most dyskinesis cases from muscle weakness or tightness don't require surgery. However, if dyskinesis stems from shoulder joint injury, the doctor may repair or reconstruct damaged tissues, followed by rehabilitation.

## Long-Term Outcomes
Once normal scapular position and motion are restored, doctors often recommend ongoing flexibility and strengthening maintenance, particularly for those with vigorous or repetitive shoulder activities. These exercises should be done 3 times a week or as recommended by your doctor.
