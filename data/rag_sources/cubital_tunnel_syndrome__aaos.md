# Cubital Tunnel Syndrome (Ulnar Nerve Entrapment at the Elbow)

## Overview
Ulnar nerve entrapment occurs when the ulnar nerve in the arm becomes compressed or irritated. The ulnar nerve travels from the neck down into the hand and can be constricted in several places. The most common compression location is behind the inside part of the elbow, known as cubital tunnel syndrome.

Common symptoms include numbness and tingling in the pinky and ring fingers. Most cases respond to nonsurgical treatments like activity modification and bracing, though surgery may be necessary if symptoms persist or nerve damage occurs.

## Anatomy
The ulnar nerve travels through the cubital tunnel at the elbow, running under a bump of bone at the inside of the elbow called the medial epicondyle. This "funny bone" location places the nerve close to the skin surface.

Beyond the elbow, the nerve travels under muscles on the inside of the forearm and into the hand on the side of the palm with the pinky finger. It passes through another tunnel called Guyon's canal upon entering the hand.

The ulnar nerve provides sensation to the little finger and half the ring finger. It controls most of the little muscles in the hand that help with fine movements, and some of the bigger muscles in the forearm that help with a strong grip.

## Cause
In many cases, the exact cause remains unknown. However, the ulnar nerve is especially vulnerable at the elbow due to its narrow passage with minimal protective soft tissue. Elbow bending compresses and stretches the nerve while reducing blood supply, which explains why symptoms worsen with bent elbows.

### Common Causes of Compression
- **Repetitive bending:** Sleeping with bent elbows or regularly bending the elbow irritates the nerve and decreases blood supply, causing numbness, particularly upon waking
- **Nerve subluxation:** The nerve may slide out from behind the medial epicondyle when bending; repeated movement irritates it over time
- **Pressure from leaning:** Extended pressure on the elbow compresses the nerve
- **Fluid buildup:** Swelling in the elbow can compress the nerve
- **Direct trauma:** A blow to the inside of the elbow causes pain and electric shock sensations in the ring and pinky fingers ("funny bone" injury)
- **Throwing activities:** Baseball pitching creates significant stress on the elbow's inner side

### Risk Factors
- Prior elbow fracture or dislocation
- Bone spurs or elbow arthritis
- Elbow joint swelling
- Elbow joint cysts
- Repetitive or prolonged elbow bending or flexing activities
- Baseball pitching

## Symptoms
Cubital tunnel syndrome causes aching pain on the elbow's inside; however, most symptoms occur in the hand.
- **Numbness and tingling:** Numbness and tingling in the ring finger and little finger are common symptoms. These symptoms fluctuate and worsen when the elbow bends during activities like driving or phone use. Some patients experience nighttime numbness.
- **Falling asleep sensation:** Fingers feel like they're falling asleep, especially with elbow bending; finger movement may become difficult.
- **Grip weakness:** Weakening grip and reduced finger coordination (typing, playing instruments) indicate more severe nerve compression.
- **Muscle wasting:** Severe or prolonged compression causes muscle wasting in the hand, which cannot be reversed.

Seek medical attention if symptoms are severe or persist beyond six weeks.

## Home Remedies
- Avoid prolonged arm bending activities
- Ensure proper computer workstation setup; keep the chair at appropriate height and avoid resting elbows on armrests
- Avoid leaning on the elbow or resting the arm on open windows while driving
- Keep the elbow straight while sleeping using a towel wrap or backward-worn elbow pad

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
The doctor discusses medical history, general health, work activities, and medications, and examines the elbow and hand to identify nerve compression location, potentially including a neck examination since pinched neck nerves cause similar symptoms.

Physical examination tests may include:
- **Tinel's test:** Tapping over the nerve at the funny bone; if irritated, it causes a shock sensation in the pinky and ring fingers (though this can occur in normal nerves too)
- **Subluxation assessment:** Checking whether the ulnar nerve moves abnormally when bending the elbow
- **Positional testing:** Moving the neck, shoulder, elbow, and wrist to identify position-related symptoms
- **Sensory and strength evaluation:** Testing hand and finger sensation and strength

### Tests
**X-rays:** While most ulnar nerve compression cannot be visualized on X-rays, doctors may use them to detect bone spurs, arthritis, or bony nerve compression.

**Nerve conduction studies:** These tests determine how well the nerve is working and help identify where it is being compressed, by sending electrical current down the arm to detect slowed conduction at compression sites. They help differentiate between elbow, wrist, or neck compression and identify muscle damage through needle electrode examination of muscles the ulnar nerve controls.

## Treatment
Unless nerve compression has caused disability or muscle wasting, nonsurgical treatment is typically attempted first.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
**Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs):** For newly developed symptoms, doctors may recommend anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen or naproxen to reduce nerve swelling.

While steroids are effective anti-inflammatory agents, steroid injections are generally not used to treat cubital tunnel syndrome because there is a risk of damage to the nerve. Occasionally, doctors prescribe oral steroids for short-term inflammation relief.

**Bracing or splinting:** A padded brace or splint worn at night maintains straight elbow positioning.

**Nerve gliding exercises:** Exercises help the ulnar nerve slide through the cubital tunnel and Guyon's canal, potentially improving symptoms while preventing arm and wrist stiffness. Exercises involve wrist and finger curling toward and away from the body, followed by elbow bending, performed with the arm extended.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is recommended when:
- Nonsurgical methods prove ineffective
- Severe nerve compression exists
- Nerve compression has caused muscle weakness or damage

These outpatient procedures include:
- **Cubital tunnel release:** The ligament roof of the cubital tunnel is cut and divided to increase tunnel size and reduce nerve pressure. New tissue growth heals the ligament while maintaining expanded space for nerve movement. This procedure works best for mild to moderate compression when the nerve doesn't subluxate.
- **Ulnar nerve transposition:** The nerve is typically moved from behind the medial epicondyle to a new position in front of it. This relocation keeps it from getting caught on the bony ridge and stretching when the elbow bends, providing a more direct path and removing compression during elbow bending.

### Surgical Recovery
- Depending on surgery type, patients may wear a splint for days to weeks; submuscular transposition typically requires three to six weeks of splinting
- Most patients can move their elbow immediately to prevent nerve scarring
- Heavy lifting is avoided for several weeks despite early motion
- Physical therapy exercises help regain strength and motion; surgeons advise on returning to normal activities

### Surgical Outcome
Surgery generally produces good results with similar success rates among procedures for routine compression cases. However, if the nerve is very badly compressed or if there is muscle wasting, the nerve may not be able to return to normal, potentially leaving residual symptoms. Nerve recovery occurs slowly; full assessment takes considerable time post-surgery.
