# Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a health condition that weakens bones, making them fragile and more likely to break. It develops slowly over several years and is often only diagnosed when a fall or sudden impact causes a bone to break (fracture).

The most common injuries in people with osteoporosis are broken wrist, broken hip, and broken spinal bones (vertebrae). Breaks can also happen in other bones, such as the arm, pelvis and ribs.

## Symptoms

Osteoporosis is not usually painful until a bone is broken, but broken bones in the spine are a common cause of long-term pain. Osteoporosis in the spine can cause the bones that make up the spine to weaken, so they crush or fracture, which can result in a stooped posture in older people as the curvature of the spine increases.

### Osteopenia
Osteopenia is when a bone density scan shows that your bone density is lower than average for your age, but not low enough to be classed as osteoporosis. Osteopenia does not always lead to osteoporosis - whether it progresses depends on a number of factors.

## Causes

Losing bone density is a normal part of ageing, but some people lose bone density much faster than normal, leading to osteoporosis and an increased risk of fractures.

Things that can increase the risk include:
- taking high-dose steroid tablets for more than 3 months
- other medical conditions, such as inflammatory conditions, hormone-related conditions, or malabsorption problems
- a family history of osteoporosis
- long-term use of certain medicines
- having or having had an eating disorder such as anorexia or bulimia
- a low body mass index (BMI)
- not doing much physical activity, or being unable to move around much
- heavy drinking and smoking

Women are also at increased risk after the menopause, when levels of the hormone oestrogen (which helps keep bones healthy) drop, particularly if the menopause happens before age 45.

## Diagnosis

A FRAX or Q-Fracture online assessment tool may be used to estimate your risk of a fracture over the next 10 years. A DEXA scan is a painless scan that measures bone density and usually takes 10 to 20 minutes.
- T score above -1 SD: normal
- T score -1 to -2.5 SD: osteopenia
- T score below -2.5 SD: osteoporosis

## Treatment

Treating osteoporosis involves treating and preventing fractures, and using medicines to strengthen bones. Not everyone needs medicine, but getting enough calcium and vitamin D is important for everyone.

### Bisphosphonates
Slow the rate at which bone is broken down in the body. Options include alendronic acid, ibandronic acid, risedronate and zoledronic acid. They're usually taken on an empty stomach with water, and you need to remain upright for 30 minutes afterwards. Bisphosphonates usually take 6 to 12 months to work, and you may need to take them for 5 years or longer. Side effects can include irritation of the food pipe, difficulty swallowing and stomach pain.

### SERMs
Raloxifene is only recommended for women after the menopause. It's a daily tablet that can help maintain bone density and reduce the risk of fracture, particularly of the spine.

### Parathyroid hormone
Can increase bone density, given as daily injections, and is usually reserved for severe cases where other treatments have not worked.

### Biological medicines
Denosumab and romosozumab are given by injection every month or every few months.

### Supplements
All adults should have 10 micrograms of vitamin D a day, and about 700mg of calcium a day.

## Prevention

Regular exercise, eating a healthy diet rich in calcium and vitamin D, taking a daily vitamin D supplement, stopping smoking, and cutting down on alcohol can all help keep bones strong and reduce your risk of osteoporosis.

## Living with osteoporosis

- Remove hazards at home that could cause a fall
- Have regular vision and hearing checks
- Manage pain with painkillers, heat or cold treatment, and relaxation techniques
- Counselling can help if you're finding it difficult to cope

**Royal Osteoporosis Society** (a UK national charity) runs a helpline on 0300 102 3030, Monday to Friday, 9am-12:30pm and 1:30pm-5pm, and can connect you with local support groups.
