# Calcific Tendinitis of the Shoulder

## Overview
Shoulder pain affects many adults, with various structures potentially contributing to discomfort. Calcific tendinitis (also called calcific tendinopathy) involves the build-up of calcium deposits within the tendons of the rotator cuff. These deposits trigger inflammation and may cause mechanical impingement on the acromion, the bony shoulder roof.

## Anatomy
The shoulder comprises three bones:
- The humerus (upper arm bone)
- The scapula (shoulder blade)
- The clavicle (collarbone)

The rotator cuff tendons attach to the top of the humerus (humeral head) and connect the humerus to the shoulder blade's socket (glenoid), enabling shoulder and arm movement.

## Cause and Risk Factors
- Adults aged 30-60 face increased risk, though anyone can develop it
- Slightly more prevalent in women than men
- Can affect one or both shoulders
- The exact cause remains unknown but may relate to tendon overuse

## Symptoms
- Pain and tenderness in the front or side of the shoulder
- Potentially severe pain occurring without trauma or movement
- Loss of motion or stiffness sensation

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
The doctor will:
- Press different shoulder areas to identify tenderness
- Request arm movements to evaluate motion
- Assess stiffness through gentle arm movement if severe pain prevents self-movement
- Test arm strength
- Check for associated shoulder problems

### Imaging Tests
**X-rays**: Cannot visualize soft tissues but reveal calcium deposits around the rotator cuff.

**MRI**: Provides soft tissue images showing fluid, inflammation, rotator cuff tears, and calcium buildup within tendons.

## Treatment
Treatment aims to reduce pain and restore function, considering age, activity level, and general health.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Many patients experience gradual improvement over weeks to months.
- **Rest**: Avoid activities causing or worsening symptoms.
- **NSAIDs**: Anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen reduce pain and swelling.
- **Physical therapy**: Focus on restoring shoulder motion through stretching, advancing to strengthening exercises as improvement occurs.
- **Corticosteroid injection**: An injection of a local steroid may be helpful if other treatments fail, typically injected into the bursa beneath the acromion, sometimes guided by ultrasound.
- **Shock wave therapy**: Shockwaves can be generated by specialized machines to deliver energy to the deep tissues of the shoulder to break up deposits and promote healing.
- **Saline lavage**: The doctor inserts a needle into the calcium deposits and injects saline with the goal of breaking up the deposits, often under ultrasound guidance, sometimes combined with corticosteroid injection.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery becomes an option when nonsurgical treatment fails. The goal of surgery is to remove the calcium and repair any associated tear of rotator cuff tendons.

**Arthroscopy**: Specialized surgical instruments are inserted into the shoulder through small poke holes with fiberoptic camera guidance. The surgeon removes calcium and addresses other conditions present.

**Rehabilitation**: Post-surgery immobilization in a sling allows healing, followed by early exercises. Recovery typically requires 2-4 months for pain relief, potentially extending to one year.
