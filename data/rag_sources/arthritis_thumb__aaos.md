# Arthritis of the Thumb

## Overview
Arthritis occurs when cartilage inside a joint breaks down or wears over time. In the thumb, the most common site for arthritis to develop is the joint at the base of the thumb — also known as the thumb carpometacarpal (CMC) joint. Osteoarthritis, the degenerative wear-and-tear variety, most frequently affects this location.

## Anatomy of the Thumb
The thumb CMC joint sits near the wrist at the fleshy part of the thumb. It enables swiveling, pivoting, and pinching for gripping. Smooth cartilage covers the ends of the bones. It enables the bones to glide easily in the joint. Without it, bones rub against each other, causing friction and damage. This joint becomes highly vulnerable to arthritis as people age.

## Causes of Thumb Arthritis
Cartilage wears down gradually, causing bones to rub together and produce pain. Risk factors include:

- Being female (women are 2-3 times more likely to develop this condition)
- Age over 50
- Family history of thumb arthritis
- Being overweight
- Previous thumb or wrist injury
- Having loose joints
- Activities involving gripping, pinching, or heavy lifting
- Smoking

## Symptoms of Thumb Arthritis
Thumb CMC arthritis typically develops gradually, with symptoms growing worse over time. Symptoms may include:

- Pain at the base during gripping, pinching, or turning activities
- Pain or aching after prolonged thumb use
- Nighttime pain
- Weakness in gripping or pinching
- Swelling and tenderness at the base
- A bump or bony growth at the base
- Limited thumb motion

## Diagnosing Thumb Arthritis
Your doctor will ask about symptoms, past injuries, and pain-triggering activities. The examination includes checking for tenderness, swelling, and thumb movement. During the test, the doctor puts pressure through your thumb and rotates the thumb at the same time. In a positive test, you may feel crunching in the joint as well as pain.

X-rays help confirm diagnosis, revealing joint damage such as reduced bone spacing or extra bone growth.

## Treatment for Thumb Arthritis

### Nonsurgical Treatment
- **Ice**: Apply for 5-15 minutes several times daily for pain and swelling relief
- **Activity modification**: Avoid aggravating activities and problematic tools
- **Splinting**: A supportive splint reduces pain during activities or overnight
- **Exercises**: Gentle exercises improve range of motion and strengthen thumb muscles
- **Medications**: NSAIDs like ibuprofen or naproxen help with pain; topical NSAID gels offer an alternative with fewer side effects
- **Injections**: Steroid shots into the joint provide temporary relief, though effects diminish over time
- **Awareness**: Some patients are reassured by knowing that this condition is not dangerous.

Symptoms may or may not worsen. Some people have no symptoms despite X-rays that show advanced arthritis.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is available when nonsurgical options fail. The operation can be performed on an outpatient basis, meaning the patient goes home the same day.

Surgical options include:

- Removing a small wrist bone at the thumb base to relieve pain while maintaining mobility — the most common U.S. procedure
- Fusing joint bones together, reducing pain and strengthening pinch but limiting thumb movement
- Thumb CMC arthroplasty: removing damaged joint parts and suspending the thumb using a tendon or medical device to maintain motion
- Joint replacement with metal or metal-plastic implants, though long-term results remain less documented

The patient and doctor will discuss the options and select the one that is best.

## Rehabilitation After Surgery
After surgery, patients will have to wear a cast or splint for 4 to 8 weeks, depending on which procedure was performed.

A hand therapist may assist in restoring movement and strength. Recovery takes several months, with grip strength improving up to 9-12 months post-surgery. Most patients resume normal activities.

## Outcomes
Thumb CMC arthritis is not a dangerous condition and is very common. Many people manage symptoms through nonsurgical treatments. Some patients who do have symptoms find that their symptoms go away over time, with or without treatment.
