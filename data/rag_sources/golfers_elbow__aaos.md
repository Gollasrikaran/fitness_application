# Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer's Elbow)

## Overview
Medial epicondylitis is a painful condition of the elbow that can be caused by overuse or through overexertion. While commonly affecting golfers, it can result from overhead throwing, tennis, and weightlifting, or develop without specific injury. The condition involves tendons in the forearm that partially tear and wear down over time due to repetitive motions. Most cases respond well to nonsurgical treatment, with surgery considered only after several months without improvement.

## Anatomy
The elbow comprises three bones: the humerus (upper arm), radius (outer forearm), and ulna (inner forearm). Muscles controlling forearm rotation and wrist/finger flexion attach via tendons to the medial epicondyle, a bony prominence on the elbow's inner side.

Key muscles involved include:
- Pronator teres (most commonly injured)
- Flexor carpi radialis
- Palmaris longus
- Flexor digitorum superficialis
- Flexor carpi ulnaris

The ulnar nerve, positioned behind the medial epicondyle, can become irritated in some cases.

## Cause
Repetitive motions of these muscles lead to tendon injuries at the attachment point. Risk factors include improper golf swing technique, inadequate equipment, age (typically 40-60), and repetitive activities of daily living, like doing certain household chores.

## Symptoms
Symptoms typically develop gradually over weeks to months and include:
- Pain or burning in the inner part of the elbow, with occasional shooting pain
- Weak grip strength
- Decreased wrist and elbow range of motion

Some patients develop ulnar neuritis, causing numbness and tingling in ring and pinky fingers.

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
Physicians ask about injury specifics, pain-causing activities, and symptom duration. Physical examination includes:
- **Palpation**: Pressing on bony prominences to locate pain
- **Strength testing**: Resistance exercises for wrist, forearm, and elbow
- **Ulnar nerve testing**: Tinel test and sensation/strength assessment

### Imaging Tests
- **X-rays**: Rule out fractures or arthritis
- **Ultrasound or MRI**: Confirm tendon injury severity; typically used after prolonged symptoms unresponsive to treatment
- **EMG studies**: Rule out nerve compression when ulnar nerve symptoms present

## Treatment

### Relieving Acute Pain
- **Activity modification**: Avoid pain-causing activities
- **Icing**: Apply 20 minutes several times daily (not directly to skin)
- **NSAIDs**: Take a short course of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications
- **Bracing**: Counterforce or wrist braces limit force on injured tendons
- **Steroid injections**: May provide initial relief but may lengthen the rehabilitation process

### Rehabilitation and Prevention
**Stretching and Strengthening:** Early treatment focuses on stretching to regain full, pain-free movement of the wrist. Progress to strengthening exercises emphasizing wrist flexion and pronation. Eccentric strengthening exercises are thought to be particularly beneficial.

**Other Nonsurgical Treatments:**
- Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections
- Dry needling
- Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (EWST)

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is considered for patients whose symptoms fail to improve after trying recommended nonoperative treatments for at least several months. The procedure involves removing damaged tendon tissue (debridement):
- Small damage: side-to-side repair
- Large damage: anchors reattach healthy tendon to bone

Ulnar nerve decompression may occur simultaneously when nerve symptoms present.

#### Post-Surgical Care
Patients typically wear a splint or sling for 1-2 weeks. Strengthening exercises begin 6-8 weeks post-surgery, with normal activities resumable within 3-6 months.
