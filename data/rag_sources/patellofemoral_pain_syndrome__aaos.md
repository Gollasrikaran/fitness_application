# Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome

## Description
Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS) is a broad term used to describe pain in the front of the knee and around the patella, or kneecap. Also known as "runner's knee," it affects athletes and non-athletes alike, with particular prevalence among females and young adults. The condition can impair everyday activities like stair climbing and kneeling.

## Anatomy of the Knee
The knee comprises the femur's lower end, tibia's upper end, and patella. Ligaments and tendons connect these bones. Key structures include:

- **Quadriceps tendon**: Connects thigh muscles to the patella
- **Patellar retinacula**: Stabilize the patella by attaching to the tibia
- **Patellar tendon**: Stretches from patella to tibia
- **Trochlear groove**: A groove where the patella rests and moves
- **Articular cartilage**: Enables smooth bone gliding
- **Menisci**: Rubbery, C-shaped shock-absorbing disks
- **Synovium**: Tissue lining that produces lubricating fluid
- **Fat pad**: Cushions the kneecap

## Why PFPS Occurs
PFPS develops when nerves sense pain in the soft tissues and bone around the kneecap. Chondromalacia patella — softening of undersurface cartilage — may accompany this condition, causing inflammation and bone pain despite cartilage lacking nerves.

## Cause

**Overuse**: Repetitive stress from jogging, squatting, or stair climbing; sudden activity increases in frequency, duration, or intensity; improper training techniques; equipment changes; footwear or playing surface modifications.

**Patellar Malalignment**: Abnormal kneecap tracking causes it to shift within the trochlear groove. Contributing factors include:
- Leg alignment problems (hip to ankle)
- Patella alta (riding too high)
- Muscular imbalances or weakness in quadriceps and hip muscles

## Symptoms
The most common symptom of PFPS is a dull, aching pain in the front of the knee. Additional symptoms include:

- Pain during knee-bending activities
- Pain after prolonged sitting with bent knees
- Pain related to activity changes
- Popping or crackling sounds

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
Doctor assesses pain location, activity triggers, knee/core strength through squatting and lunging. Evaluation includes:
- Lower leg and kneecap alignment
- Knee stability and hip rotation
- Range of motion
- Kneecap tenderness
- Muscle strength, flexibility, and tone
- Heel cord tightness
- Gait assessment

### Imaging Tests
- **X-rays**: Rule out bone damage
- **MRI scans**: Visualize soft tissues; ordered when symptoms persist despite physical therapy

## Treatment

### Home Remedies

**Activity Changes**: Stop aggravating activities; switch to low-impact options (stationary biking, elliptical, swimming); lose weight if overweight.

**RICE Method**:
- Rest: Avoid weight-bearing on the painful knee
- Ice: Apply cold packs 20 minutes, multiple times daily (not directly on skin)
- Compression: Wrap knee lightly with elastic bandage
- Elevation: Keep knee raised above heart level

**Medication**: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and naproxen can help reduce swelling and relieve pain.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Treatment aims to relieve pain and restore range of motion and strength. Most cases respond to:

**Physical Therapy**: Exercises improving range of motion, strength, and endurance, emphasizing quadriceps and hip strengthening. Core exercises strengthen abdominal and lower back muscles.

**Orthotics**: Shoe inserts align and stabilize the foot and ankle, reducing lower leg stress.

### Surgical Treatment
Reserved for severe cases unresponsive to conservative measures:

**Arthroscopy**: Camera-guided minimally invasive surgery including:
- **Debridement**: Removing damaged cartilage
- **Lateral release**: Loosening tight lateral retinaculum to correct malalignment

**Tibial Tubercle Transfer**: Realigns the kneecap by moving the patellar tendon and tibial tubercle portion toward the inner knee, reattaching with screws for improved tracking.

## Prevention
Patellofemoral pain syndrome is usually fully relieved with simple measures or physical therapy. Prevention strategies include:

- Wearing appropriate athletic shoes
- Thorough warm-ups
- Stretching quadriceps and hamstrings
- Gradual training progression
- Avoiding previously problematic activities
- Maintaining healthy body weight
