# NICE NG59: Low back pain and sciatica in over 16s — assessment and management

Sciatica (nerve root pain caused by irritation or compression of a lumbosacral nerve root) is very often caused by a herniated (prolapsed/slipped) disc pressing on a nerve root, and is one of the two conditions this NICE guideline explicitly covers. The full guideline is published by NICE at nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59; this content reflects that guidance as reproduced on the NCBI Bookshelf.

## Self-Management

Healthcare professionals should provide people with advice and information, tailored to their needs and capabilities, to help them self-manage their sciatica (with or without accompanying low back pain), at all steps of the treatment pathway. This includes information about the nature of the condition and encouragement to maintain normal activities.

## Risk Stratification

Consider risk stratification tools (such as the STarT Back tool) at first contact for each new episode, to inform shared decision-making. Simpler support (reassurance, advice to keep active, self-management guidance) is offered to people likely to improve quickly; more complex and intensive support (exercise programmes, with or without manual therapy or a psychological approach) is offered to people at higher risk of a poor outcome.

## Imaging

Do not routinely offer imaging in a non-specialist setting. Imaging should only be considered in specialist settings of care if the result is likely to change management — for example, before a decision on injection therapy or surgery for sciatica caused by a disc herniation. Patients should be told they may not need imaging if referred for specialist opinion.

## Exercise

Consider a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body, or a combination of approaches) for people with a specific episode or flare-up of low back pain with or without sciatica, taking their needs, preferences and capabilities into account.

## Manual and Psychological Therapy

Manual therapy (manipulation, mobilisation, soft tissue techniques) and cognitive-behavioural psychological therapy should only be considered as part of a treatment package that also includes exercise.

## Pharmacological Management (for sciatica)

- Do not offer gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids or benzodiazepines for sciatica.
- Do not offer opioids for managing chronic sciatica.
- Be aware there is only limited evidence of benefit from NSAIDs for sciatica.

## Invasive Treatments

- Consider epidural injections for people with acute and severe sciatica (which is frequently caused by a herniated disc compressing a nerve root).
- Consider referral for spinal decompression surgery when non-surgical treatment has not worked and imaging findings (such as a confirmed disc herniation) are consistent with the person's symptoms.
