# Hamstring Muscle Injuries

## Overview
Hamstring injuries, commonly called "pulled hamstrings," frequently affect athletes participating in sprinting sports like track, soccer, basketball, and waterskiing. These muscle strains typically respond well to nonsurgical treatment.

## Anatomy
Three hamstring muscles run down the back of the thigh:
- Semitendinosus
- Semimembranosus
- Biceps femoris

These muscles originate at the ischial tuberosity (bottom of the pelvis), cross the knee joint, and attach near the top of the lower leg. They enable leg extension and knee bending.

## Description
Hamstring strains range from mild pulls to complete tears, graded by severity (grade 1 being mild, grade 3 being complete tears requiring months to heal). Most injuries occur in the muscle belly or where muscle fibers connect to tendons. Severe injuries may cause avulsion — where the tendon completely detaches from bone, sometimes pulling bone fragments away.

## Cause

### Muscle Overload
The primary cause involves stretching muscles beyond capacity or subjecting them to sudden loads. During sprinting, hamstrings lengthen while contracting under body weight and forward propulsion force (eccentric contraction).

### Risk Factors
- Muscle tightness reduces flexibility
- Muscle imbalance (quadriceps often stronger than hamstrings)
- Poor conditioning weakens injury resistance
- Muscle fatigue decreases shock absorption
- Specific sports (football, soccer, basketball, waterskiing)
- Running, dancing, and walking-focused exercise programs
- Adolescent growth spurts (bones growing faster than muscles)

## Symptoms
A hamstring strain causes sudden, sharp pain in the back of your thigh during sprinting, often causing immediate stoppage or falling. Additional symptoms include:
- Swelling within hours post-injury
- Bruising below the knee within days
- Persistent hamstring weakness for weeks

## Doctor Examination

**Patient History and Physical Examination**: Doctors assess injury circumstances and examine the thigh for tenderness, bruising, and muscle damage severity through palpation.

**Imaging Tests**:
- **X-rays**: Detect tendon avulsions with bone fragments
- **MRI scans**: Create soft tissue images showing injury severity

## Treatment
Treatment depends on injury type, severity, and patient goals. The objective is enabling return to normal activities while preventing future problems.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Most hamstring strains heal effectively without surgery.

**RICE Protocol:**
- **Rest**: Avoid the causative activity; crutches may be recommended
- **Ice**: Apply cold packs 20 minutes, multiple times daily (not directly to skin)
- **Compression**: Wear elastic bandages preventing additional swelling
- **Elevation**: Recline with leg raised above heart level

**Immobilization**: Knee braces maintain neutral leg positioning.

**Physical Therapy**: After initial pain and swelling subside, therapy restores range of motion through gentle stretching, then gradually adds strengthening exercises as healing progresses.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery addresses tendon avulsions where tendons completely detach from bone. Proximal avulsions (at the pelvis) are more common than distal avulsions (at the shinbone). Surgery isn't typically performed for muscle belly tears.

**Procedure**: Surgeons reposition the hamstring tendon, remove scar tissue, and reattach it using small anchors.

**Rehabilitation**:
- Proximal repairs require approximately 6 months rehabilitation
- Distal repairs require approximately 3 months before returning to athletics
- Crutches and braces may be needed initially
- Therapy progresses from gentle stretching to strengthening exercises

## Recovery
Most people achieve full functional recovery after completing rehabilitation. Early RICE protocol and physical therapy interventions produce better outcomes and faster return to sports. Preventing reinjury requires following medical guidance completely, as reinjury increases permanent damage and chronic condition risk.

## New Developments
Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections — concentrated blood preparations containing growth factor proteins — are being investigated for accelerating hamstring healing. Many treatment centers incorporate PRP into nonsurgical regimens, though effectiveness requires further research confirmation.
