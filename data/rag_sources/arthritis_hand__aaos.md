# Arthritis of the Hand

## Overview
The hand and wrist contain multiple small joints that work together to enable motion, from fine movements like threading a needle to everyday activities. When arthritis affects these joints, daily living becomes challenging. Over time, arthritic joints can lose their normal shape, causing increased pain and limited motion, potentially making some activities impossible.

## Description
Arthritis is defined as "inflammation of one or more of your joints." The most prevalent types are osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, though more than 100 different forms exist.

Healthy joints function smoothly due to articular cartilage — a smooth, slippery tissue covering bone ends. This surface is lubricated by synovial fluid produced by the joint lining (synovium).

### Disease
When arthritis develops without injury, symptoms typically emerge gradually as cartilage breaks down over extended periods.

**Osteoarthritis:**
- Much more common than rheumatoid arthritis
- Generally affects older people
- Known as "wear-and-tear arthritis" because cartilage wears away over prolonged use
- Appears in predictable patterns in certain joints

**Rheumatoid Arthritis:**
- A chronic autoimmune disease affecting multiple body areas
- Causes synovium swelling, producing joint pain and stiffness
- Most often starts in small hand and feet joints
- Usually affects matching joints on both body sides
- Causes progressive cartilage damage

### Trauma
Fractures — especially those damaging joint surfaces — and dislocations commonly lead to post-traumatic arthritis. Even with proper treatment, injured joints are more likely to develop arthritis than uninjured joints. Trauma may directly damage cartilage at injury time, or fractures may heal improperly, creating uneven joint surfaces or abnormal angles that gradually cause arthritis.

## Symptoms

### Pain
Early-stage hand arthritis presents with:
- Dull or burning joint pain
- Pain occurring after increased joint use, such as heavy gripping
- Delayed pain onset (hours afterward or the next day)
- Typical morning pain and stiffness

As cartilage wears away, symptoms increase in frequency. Advanced disease may cause nighttime pain awakening patients.

- Pain typically worsens with use and improves with rest
- Many arthritis sufferers report increased pain during rainy weather
- Previously simple activities like opening jars or starting cars become painful
- Patients may alter hand use patterns to prevent arthritic joint pain

### Swelling
When affected joints experience stress exceeding their capacity, they may swell during or several hours after heavy use.

### Changes in Surrounding Joints
Advanced thumb base arthritis causes neighboring joints to become excessively mobile, compensating for arthritic stiffness. However, joints moving beyond normal limits create additional problems.

### Warmth
Arthritic joints may feel warm due to the body's inflammatory response.

### Crepitus and Looseness
- A grating or grinding sensation or sound (crepitus) may occur from damaged cartilage surfaces rubbing together
- If arthritis involves damaged ligaments, joint support structures may become unstable or loose
- Advanced cases show enlarged joints (hypertrophic) from bone changes, cartilage loss, and swelling

### Cysts
When arthritis affects end finger joints (DIP joints), small mucous cysts may develop. These cysts can cause nail plate ridging or dents, may rupture leaking gelatinous fluid, and ruptured cysts risk infection.

## Doctor Examination
Doctors diagnose hand arthritis through hand examination and X-rays. Specialized imaging like MRI is typically unnecessary except when suspecting Kienböck's disease (interrupted blood supply to the wrist's lunate bone).

## Treatment
Arthritis need not result in a painful or sedentary life. Early intervention helps patients resume important activities.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
**Medications:** Medications address symptoms but cannot restore cartilage or reverse damage. The most common medications for arthritis are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including naproxen and ibuprofen. Glucosamine and chondroitin sulfate are advertised supplements but lack study for hand and wrist arthritis specifically. Rheumatoid arthritis requires specialized autoimmune-targeting drugs prescribed by rheumatologists.

**Injections:** When anti-inflammatory medication proves inappropriate, injections containing anesthetic and steroid provide weeks-to-months pain relief. Limited repetitions are possible due to potential side effects including skin lightening, tendon/ligament weakening, cartilage damage, and infection.

**Splinting:** Splints limit joint motion, reducing pain and supporting affected joints during stress. Worn during painful periods, splints must remain small enough for functional hand use, as extended wearing causes muscle deterioration (atrophy).

### Surgical Treatment
When nonsurgical treatment fails, surgery is considered. Surgical choice should offer reasonable long-term pain relief and functional return, tailored to individual needs. Joint preservation or reconstruction is preferred when possible.

**Joint Fusion:** Fusion surgeries provide pain relief by eliminating joint motion. Damaged surfaces cannot cause pain or symptoms, but lost motion reduces function. Some joints show insignificant functional loss; others experience obvious, challenging limitations affecting quality of life.

**Joint Replacement:** The goal of joint replacement is to provide pain relief and maintain function. Modern replacements use ceramics or long-wearing metal and plastic similar to hip and knee replacements. Many hand and wrist joints can be replaced. Long-term results remain unknown, though early results show promise.

## After Surgery
Recovery follows any joint surgery. Hand therapists often help maximize recovery. Postoperative splints or casts protect healing hands during recovery. Activity modification during healing is necessary, with oral pain medication addressing discomfort.

Recovery duration varies widely depending on surgery extent and individual factors. People usually can return to most if not all of their desired activities in about 3 months.

## New Developments
Doctors increasingly focus on damaged joint preservation, including earlier diagnosis confirmation and component repair before complete surface damage occurs.

Arthroscopy of the small joints of the hand and wrist is now possible because the equipment has been made much smaller.

Encouraging cartilage repair and replacement results in larger joints like knees have been applied to smaller hand and arm joints. Stem cell research may offer options for regenerating damaged joint surfaces.
