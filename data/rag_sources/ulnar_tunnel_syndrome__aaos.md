# Ulnar Tunnel Syndrome of the Wrist

## Overview
The ulnar nerve is one of three primary nerves supplying sensation and function to the hand, originating from the spinal cord and traveling from the neck through the arm into the hand. Ulnar tunnel syndrome occurs when the ulnar nerve is compressed at the wrist. This compression reduces blood flow to the nerve and impairs its function.

When compressed at the wrist, the condition causes numbness and tingling in the little finger (pinky) and the outside of the ring finger, along with weakness in hand pinch and grip strength.

## Cause
The most common cause involves soft tissue tumors pressing on the nerve. The tumor is typically a benign (noncancerous) cyst called a ganglion which originates from the wrist joint. Ganglion cysts contain thick gel and vary in size.

Anatomical factors can also contribute — some individuals have a naturally narrow tunnel (Guyon's canal) where the ulnar nerve passes from wrist to hand, predisposing them to compression.

Repetitive trauma or chronic pressure on the palm's ulnar side causes additional cases. Examples include:

- Using jackhammers or power tools (repetitive trauma)
- Long-distance cycling with weight resting on handlebars (chronic pressure)

People experiencing repetitive or chronic trauma may develop hypothenar hammer syndrome, characterized by poor blood flow resulting in blue or white fingers alongside numbness and tingling.

## Symptoms
Symptoms typically develop gradually:

- People commonly experience numbness and tingling in the little and ring fingers with associated hand weakness, poor grip, weak pinch, and difficulty separating or closing fingers
- The severity depends on pressure point location and treatment delay
- As the condition progresses, tasks like opening jars, holding objects, or coordinating fingers during typing or musical instrument playing become more difficult

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
Doctors examine hands for characteristic signs:

- They test sensation in fingers, particularly the little and ring fingers, using two-point discrimination testing
- They assess muscle weakness indicators, including reduced bulk (muscle atrophy) and poor strength
  - Wartenberg's sign: inability to pull the small finger inward to touch the ring finger
  - Froment's sign: inability to effectively pinch paper between thumb and index finger without flexing the last joint
- They apply pressure over the ulnar nerve at the wrist to determine if numbness and tingling worsen
- They tap the ulnar nerve at the wrist to check for tingling (Tinel sign), indicating nerve compression
- They may examine the elbow, since the ulnar nerve also travels through a narrow tunnel there

### Tests
- Nerve conduction study/electromyography (NCS/EMG) determines proper nerve function
- Computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or ultrasound identifies objects compressing the nerve, such as cysts
- X-rays identify bone fragments from fractures pressing on the nerve

## Nonsurgical Treatment
Treatment approaches depend on the cause:

- For wrist positioning issues during typing, position changes or padding may help
- Jackhammer operators must eliminate repetitive trauma through technique modification, protective padding, or job changes
- Cyclists can relieve chronic pressure by frequently changing hand position on handlebars or adding protective padding

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as naproxen or ibuprofen, may alleviate symptoms in many cases, and short-term wrist splinting may provide additional relief.

## Surgical Treatment
Many cases result from growths at the wrist or narrow ulnar tunnels. Surgery becomes necessary to remove growths or widen the tunnel. An experienced hand surgeon can remove cysts, scar tissue, and other causes of compression and open up the ulnar tunnel on an outpatient basis.

### Complications
Though rare, complications include infection, wound issues, or damage to nearby nerves, vessels, and tendons.

### Surgical Outcome
After pressure relief, normal sensation may return and the tingling may decrease, though recovery takes several months to a year. Older patients with prolonged compression may experience incomplete recovery and irreversible muscle wasting. Surgeons typically prescribe postoperative rehabilitation and home exercises to support recovery.
