# Sesamoiditis and Sesamoid Fracture

## Overview
The human body contains a few bones that differ structurally from typical bones. Rather than connecting to other bones through a joint, these specialized bones attach only to a tendon or sit embedded within muscle tissue — these are called sesamoid bones. The largest sesamoid in the body is the kneecap. Two additional tiny sesamoids, about the size of a kernel of corn, are located on the underside of the forefoot near the big toe, one on the outer side and one closer to the center of the foot.

This article focuses specifically on sesamoid conditions of the foot. For information about kneecap-related conditions, see separate resources on patellar fractures, patellofemoral arthritis, or patellar tendon injuries.

## Anatomy
The pair of sesamoid bones sits beneath the joint of the big toe on the sole of the foot. The tendons responsible for flexing the big toe connect to both sesamoids. The bones are held together by ligaments and a particularly strong plantar plate structure.

## Description and Function
Sesamoids work mechanically like pulleys. They provide a smooth gliding surface that lets tendons move easily, increasing the effectiveness with which tendons transmit force generated by muscles. In the forefoot, these bones help distribute weight and assist with lifting the bones of the big toe.

Like any bone, sesamoids can break through either a sudden injury or accumulated stress from repetitive use. In addition, the tendons near the sesamoids can become irritated or inflamed — a condition called sesamoiditis, which is a type of tendon inflammation. This condition is seen regularly in ballet dancers, competitive runners, and baseball catchers.

## Symptoms
Discomfort is concentrated under the big toe, in the ball of the foot. The onset differs depending on the specific condition: sesamoiditis typically causes pain that develops gradually, while a sesamoid fracture causes sudden pain following a hyperextension injury (when the toe is bent backward). Affected individuals may or may not develop visible swelling and bruising. Difficulty bending and straightening the big toe is common, and pain often accompanies these movements.

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
The doctor will feel the foot and big toe to identify tenderness specifically localized to the sesamoid area, assess active movement of the joints around the big toe and sesamoids, and may gently bend the big toe upward toward the top of the foot to see whether this movement increases pain.

### Imaging
**X-rays.** X-rays of the forefoot help establish the diagnosis. One notable consideration is that the medial sesamoid (the one closer to the center of the foot) sometimes naturally exists in two separate pieces — a "bipartite" configuration. The way to distinguish this normal variation from an actual fracture is by the appearance of the bone edges: a bipartite sesamoid has smooth borders, while a fractured sesamoid has irregular, jagged edges. Comparison X-rays of the uninjured foot can help with accuracy.

**Bone scan.** When initial X-rays appear normal, a bone scan can reveal areas of bone irritation even without a visible fracture line.

**MRI.** An MRI is typically not needed for the initial diagnosis. However, when X-rays do not reveal the underlying issue and conservative treatment has not helped, an MRI becomes useful for visualizing soft tissue inflammation near the sesamoids or identifying a stress fracture too subtle to see on X-ray.

## Treatment
Management typically starts with conservative methods. Only when nonsurgical approaches fail to relieve symptoms does surgical removal of the sesamoid become a consideration.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
**For sesamoiditis:**
- Stop the activity causing pain and rest the foot; doctors may recommend either avoiding weight-bearing entirely or limiting it to pain-free positions
- Take over-the-counter pain medication such as aspirin or ibuprofen
- Rest and apply cold therapy to the sole of the foot (avoiding direct ice contact with skin by using a protective wrap)
- Choose footwear with soft soles and minimal heel height; rigid-soled shoes like clogs can also be comfortable
- Apply felt-based cushioning to reduce stress on the affected area
- Gradually resume activities while continuing to use dense foam padding under the sesamoids for support
- Avoid activities that put weight directly on the forefoot
- Tape the big toe in a slightly downward-bent position
- Consider a doctor-administered steroid injection to reduce inflammation
- Use a removable short leg brace for 4 to 6 weeks if symptoms persist

**For sesamoid fractures:**
- Use rigid-soled shoes or a short leg brace
- Tape the toe to restrict movement
- Use J-shaped padding around the sesamoid area to reduce pressure during healing
- Take pain medication such as aspirin or ibuprofen as needed
- Allow several months for discomfort to fully resolve
- Continue using cushioning and orthotic support throughout the healing process

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is considered for persistent sesamoiditis that has not responded to conservative treatment, for displaced sesamoid fractures where the broken pieces are out of alignment, and for fractures that fail to heal. Surgical repair of a displaced fracture may involve reconstruction with heavy sutures. Surgery for sesamoid pain often involves partial or total removal of the affected sesamoid bone.

## Recovery
Recovery time varies depending on the severity of the condition and the intensity of symptoms. An extended period — spanning weeks to months — of protective casting combined with limited weight-bearing is often necessary, since normal walking places substantial force on the forefoot ligaments and sesamoids.

The reassuring news is that most sesamoid conditions resolve on their own without surgery. When surgery is needed, it typically succeeds in eliminating the pain caused by the problematic sesamoid.
