# Ganglion Cyst of the Wrist and Hand

## Overview
Ganglion cysts represent the most prevalent mass or lump in the hand. They are benign and typically harmless. These fluid-filled cysts can develop in various locations, though they most frequently occur on the back of the wrist. They may appear and disappear rapidly while changing in size. Many require no intervention, but treatment options exist if the cyst causes discomfort, functional limitations, or aesthetic concerns.

## Description
A ganglion emerges from a joint like a balloon attached to a stalk. It develops from tissues surrounding joints, including ligaments, tendon sheaths, and joint linings. The interior contains thick, slippery fluid similar to joint lubricant.

The back of the wrist is the most common location for ganglion cysts.

Ganglion cysts can develop in several hand and wrist joints:

- Both the top and underside of the wrist
- The end joint of a finger
- The base of a finger

These vary in size and typically enlarge with increased activity. Rest usually causes the lump to reduce in size.

## Cause
The trigger for ganglion formation remains unknown.

- They occur most frequently in younger individuals between ages 15 and 40
- Women experience them more often than men
- They are prevalent among gymnasts who repeatedly stress the wrist
- Mucous cysts, developing at the end joint on the nail side of a finger, typically associate with finger joint arthritis and occur more commonly in women aged 40 to 70

## Symptoms
Most ganglions present as a visible lump; smaller ganglions may remain hidden beneath the skin (occult ganglions). While many produce no symptoms beyond the mass appearance, pressure on nerves crossing the joint can cause pain, tingling, and muscle weakness.

Large cysts may generate anxiety or dissatisfaction regarding appearance, even without pain.

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
Your doctor will review your medical history and symptoms, potentially asking:

- Duration of ganglion presence
- Whether it changes in size
- Whether it causes pain

Your doctor may apply pressure to test tenderness. Since ganglions contain fluid, they are translucent. A penlight may be held against the cyst to verify light passes through, confirming it is a cyst rather than a solid tumor.

### Imaging Tests
**X-rays.** X-rays create clear pictures of dense structures like bone. Although X-rays won't show ganglion cysts, they eliminate other conditions such as hand arthritis, wrist arthritis, or bone tumors.

**Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans or ultrasounds.** These better visualize soft tissues like ganglions compared to X-rays. MRI or ultrasound may locate occult ganglions not visible externally or distinguish cysts from other tumors. However, further imaging often isn't necessary before treatment.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial ganglion cyst treatment is nonsurgical.

**Observation.** Without pain or other symptoms, monitoring may be recommended to ensure no unusual changes occur. This remains safe because ganglions are benign and may self-resolve over time.

**Immobilization.** Activity often causes ganglion enlargement, increasing pressure on surrounding nerves and causing pain. A wrist brace or splint may relieve symptoms and reduce ganglion size. As pain decreases, strengthening exercises and range-of-motion work may be prescribed.

**Aspiration.** If the ganglion causes significant pain or substantially limits activities, fluid may be drained through aspiration.

The area surrounding the ganglion is numbed and punctured with a needle to withdraw fluid.

Unfortunately, aspiration frequently fails to eliminate the ganglion permanently because the root or connection to the joint or tendon sheath remains. Like a weed, the ganglion may regrow if the root persists. Therefore, ganglion cysts often return after aspiration.

Aspirations are most often performed on ganglions on the wrist's top. Palm-side wrist ganglions can be problematic to aspirate due to proximity to major vessels and nerves.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery may be recommended if nonsurgical methods don't relieve symptoms or if the ganglion returns after aspiration. The removal procedure is called excision.

Surgery removes the cyst and addresses the stalk from which it arises. This may involve removing part of the involved joint capsule or tendon sheath to ensure complete root removal. Even after excision, a small possibility exists that the ganglion will return.

Excision is typically an outpatient procedure with patients discharged the same day following recovery observation. Some tenderness, discomfort, and swelling may occur post-surgery. Normal activities can usually resume 2 to 6 weeks after the procedure.

## Outcomes
Ganglion cysts rarely cause permanent disability. Cysts that don't self-resolve and produce symptoms typically respond well to surgery. In rare cases where ganglions return post-surgery, revision surgery can be performed. Following removal, most patients experience symptom relief.
