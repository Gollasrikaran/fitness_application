# Sprained Ankle

## Overview
Ankle sprains are the leading cause of missed participation in sports and occur frequently across all age groups and activity levels. These injuries happen when the strong ligaments that support the ankle are stretched beyond their normal range and tear. The ligaments on the outer side of the ankle are most commonly affected. The severity of a sprain varies considerably depending on how many ligaments are involved and how much they have torn.

Most ankle sprains respond well to basic care including ice, elevation, over-the-counter pain relievers, and simple rehabilitation exercises. However, medical evaluation is necessary if the ankle continues to swell or hurt for several weeks despite home care, or if putting weight on it becomes difficult. When conservative treatment fails, surgery to repair or reconstruct the damaged ligaments may be appropriate.

Untreated or inadequately rehabilitated severe sprains create chronic weakness in the ankle, substantially increasing the risk of re-injury. Repeated sprains can lead to persistent pain, chronic ankle pain, arthritis, and instability.

## What Is an Ankle Sprain?
Ligaments are fibrous connective tissues that link bones throughout the body. The ankle has numerous ligaments that keep the bones properly aligned and provide the joint stability needed for standing, walking, and running.

A sprain occurs when one or more of the ankle-stabilizing ligaments are damaged. About 90% of ankle sprains involve an inversion injury (the foot turns inward) to the anterior talofibular (ATFL) and calcaneofibular (CFL) ligaments, located on the outer side of the ankle. Less often, medial ankle sprains result from an eversion injury (the foot turns out) to the deltoid ligament on the inner side of the ankle.

Tearing severity ranges from microscopic fiber damage to a complete tear. When a ligament tears completely, the ankle may become unstable once the initial swelling goes down. Progressive instability can damage the underlying bone and cartilage surfaces over time.

## Causes of Ankle Sprains
A typical injury involves twisting the foot or ankle, sometimes accompanied by an audible or felt "pop" when the ligament tears severely. These injuries can happen unexpectedly during activities including:

- Walking, working, or exercising on uneven ground
- Falls or trips
- High-demand sports involving cutting movements or jumping (trail running, basketball, tennis, football, soccer)

Risk factors include:

- Poor balance
- Naturally loose or hypermobile ligaments
- Misaligned ankle structure
- Footwear that lacks adequate foot and ankle support
- Shoes with insufficient traction
- A history of previous ankle sprain, which increases the chance of spraining it again

## Symptoms of an Ankle Sprain
The presentation and intensity of symptoms depend on how severe the injury is. Possible symptoms include:

- Pain both at rest and with weight-bearing or movement
- Swelling
- Bruising from bleeding under the skin
- Tenderness to the touch
- A feeling of instability or that the joint is giving way

Symptoms of a severe sprain are similar to those of a broken bone and require prompt medical evaluation.

## Diagnosing an Ankle Sprain

### Physical Examination
Doctors diagnose ankle sprains by asking about how the injury happened and performing a detailed examination of the foot and ankle. Swelling and inflammation often make this exam uncomfortable, and it typically involves:

**Observation.** The doctor visually compares the injured ankle to the uninjured side. Sprains typically show swelling and bruising directly over the damaged ligaments.

**Palpation.** Tenderness is localized specifically over the injured ligaments. The doctor gently presses around the ankle to identify which ligaments are damaged.

**Range of motion testing.** The doctor moves the ankle in various directions, though stiffness and swelling may limit movement.

**Stability testing.** The doctor applies controlled pressure and pulling motions in different directions to assess joint stability.

Doctors can often estimate the severity of a sprain based on the amount of swelling, pain intensity, degree of bruising, and results of stability testing. Difficulty bearing weight or tenderness over bone calls for additional testing to rule out a fracture.

### Imaging Tests
Ankle sprains are primarily diagnosed clinically, based on how the injury happened, reported symptoms, and physical exam findings. Doctors sometimes order imaging to rule out a fracture or injury to nearby tissue.

**X-rays** show dense structures like bone well. Depending on symptoms and exam findings, doctors may order X-rays of the ankle and foot bones. Severe ankle sprains can have a similar degree of pain, bruising, and swelling as a fracture, which makes it hard to tell the two injuries apart from symptoms alone.

**Stress X-rays** are specialized images taken while the doctor pushes on the ankle in different directions using controlled force. They reveal whether ligament damage has compromised the ankle's stability.

**MRI** is not required to diagnose a standard ankle sprain but may be ordered to:

- Evaluate soft tissues like ligaments, cartilage, and tendons that plain X-rays cannot show
- Assess for a "high ankle sprain" involving the connection between the lower leg bones
- Investigate symptoms that persist beyond 6 to 8 weeks despite conservative treatment

## Classification of Ankle Sprains
After the examination, doctors assign a grade to the sprain to guide treatment, based on the extent of ligament damage.

**Grade 1.** Minor stretching of the fibers with microscopic tearing, mild localized pain, bruising, and swelling. Weight-bearing typically causes no pain, and stability testing shows no instability.

**Grade 2.** Partial ligament tearing, causing moderate localized pain, bruising, and swelling. Weight-bearing causes mild discomfort, and the exam shows slight looseness compared to the uninjured ankle.

**Grade 3.** Complete tearing of the ligament, with significant localized pain, bruising, and swelling. Weight-bearing produces substantial pain, and the exam shows pronounced instability.

## Treatment for Ankle Sprains

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Nearly all isolated ankle sprains heal without surgery. Even complete ligament tears (Grade 3) heal through proper immobilization and rehabilitation without needing operative repair.

Treatment follows a three-phase progression that applies to all sprains:

**Phase 1** includes brief immobilization, activity restriction, and ice for swelling. Early weight-bearing as comfort allows is the standard approach. Grade 2 sprains benefit from a removable supportive device like a walking boot or inflatable brace. Grade 3 injuries may require a short leg cast or a protective cast-brace for 10 to 14 days. Swelling and discomfort typically last 2 to 3 days, and crutches may be recommended if walking is difficult.

**Phase 2** typically starts early and focuses on functional rehabilitation, including:

- Motion exercises to improve ankle movement through its available range
- Isometric strengthening — contracting muscles without moving the joint
- Balance retraining to restore proprioception

Immobilization should end during this phase to prevent stiffness from developing.

**Phase 3** includes progressively more challenging strengthening and balance exercises alongside gradual return to activity. Initially, activities that avoid turning or twisting the ankle are emphasized, progressing later to quick cutting movements typical of sports like tennis, basketball, and football. Early return to sports or work often requires ankle taping or bracing for support.

This three-phase progression may take as little as 2 weeks for minor injuries but can extend to 6 to 12 weeks for more serious injuries.

### Home Treatment for Ankle Sprains
Milder cases may respond to home care recommended by a doctor.

**RICE protocol.** Apply this approach right after the injury:

- **Rest.** Avoid walking on the injured ankle and stop participating in sports.
- **Ice.** Apply cold therapy immediately to control swelling, using 20- to 30-minute applications three to four times a day. Never apply ice directly to the skin; use an ice pack or a towel wrap and monitor the skin.
- **Compression.** Compression bandages, wraps, or elastic supports help immobilize and stabilize the ankle and may reduce swelling. Continue wearing compression as long as it's comfortable.
- **Elevation.** Keep the ankle above heart level as much as possible during the first 48 hours to manage and minimize swelling.

**Medication.** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and naproxen can help control pain and swelling. They offer greater benefit for mild sprains than narcotic pain medications since they reduce both swelling and discomfort, improving function. Those who cannot tolerate NSAIDs may use acetaminophen for pain relief instead.

**Physical therapy.** Rehabilitation activities in phases two and three improve flexibility, muscle strength, and balance.

- **Early movement.** Exercises involving unrestricted ankle movement without resistance help prevent stiffness. Stretching bands may assist with range-of-motion work.
- **Strengthening exercises.** Once swelling and discomfort decrease, exercises targeting the muscles and tendons in the front and back of the lower leg and ankle begin. Water-based exercise may substitute if weight-bearing is too painful. Progressive resistance training is introduced as tolerated.
- **Proprioception (balance) training.** Poor balance often precedes repeat injuries and chronic instability. Example exercises include standing on the injured foot with the other foot raised and eyes closed. Balance boards are commonly used at this stage.
- **Endurance and agility exercises.** Once pain resolves, agility drills are progressively added. Running progressively tighter figure-8 patterns improves both agility and lower leg strength as balance improves.

### Surgery for Ankle Sprains
Surgery for ankle sprains is uncommon.

- Surgery is considered for cases that fail nonsurgical treatment, when patients have persistent instability and pain despite months of rehabilitation and conservative care
- High ankle sprains with instability affecting the connecting structures between the lower leg bones may need surgery
- Surgery may be appropriate when a severe sprain is accompanied by additional injuries such as cartilage damage or tendon rupture

**Surgical options:**

**Arthroscopy.** Surgeons use a small camera (arthroscope) to see inside the ankle. Tiny instruments remove loose bone or cartilage fragments and pieces of ligament caught in the joint. Arthroscopic approaches can sometimes allow ligament repair with stitches through small incisions.

**Open repair/reconstruction.** Surgeons may repair torn ligaments with stitches while directly viewing the bone and tissues (an open surgical approach). Alternatively, surgeons can reconstruct damaged ligaments using tissue grafted from other ligaments or tendons in the ankle area.

**Recovery following surgery:**

- **Immobilization.** Post-operative care includes a period of immobilization. A protective cast or boot protects the repair. Following the doctor's instructions about how long to use the protective device is important to prevent premature stress that could re-tear the repair or reconstruction.
- **Rehabilitation.** Post-operative rehabilitation aims to restore strength, movement, and balance to return to prior activity levels. Recovery time depends on the severity of the injury and the surgical approach, and can range from weeks to months.

## Outcomes
Overall outcomes for ankle sprains are generally favorable. With appropriate treatment and adherence to rehabilitation, most people return to their usual daily activities after recovering. Success and return to activity depend on:

- The severity grade of the sprain
- The presence of other injuries that affect healing time
- The patient's commitment to rehabilitation exercises (incomplete rehabilitation is the most common cause of chronic instability after a sprain; skipping strengthening work leaves ligaments weak and raises the risk of future sprains)

Discussing the expected outcome for your specific injury with your doctor is important.

### Chronic Ankle Sprains
Repeated re-spraining can occur if ligaments don't have enough time to heal before returning to demanding activity. It can be difficult to know exactly when healing is complete, though pain and swelling levels typically guide the pace of physical therapy and functional recovery.

Pain that persists beyond 4 to 6 weeks suggests a chronic ankle sprain. Circumstances that can worsen an already-sprained ankle include:

- Walking on uneven surfaces
- Sports that require cutting movements or twisting and rolling of the foot

Abnormal proprioception is a common complication of a sprain that leads to repeat injuries. Subsequent injuries can produce chronic instability, pain, and progressive damage to the underlying cartilage and bone.

## Preventing Ankle Sprains
The best prevention centers on maintaining adequate muscle strength, balance, and flexibility. These precautions help prevent sprains:

- Perform a thorough warm-up before exercise and physical activity
- Do strengthening exercises to improve ankle stability
- Pay close attention when walking, running, or working on uneven surfaces
- Choose footwear that provides appropriate support for the specific activity
