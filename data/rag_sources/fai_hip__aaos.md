# Femoroacetabular Impingement

## Overview
Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is a condition where extra bone grows along one or both hip joint bones, creating an irregular shape. This abnormal structure causes the bones to rub together during movement, potentially damaging the joint over time and causing pain that limits activity.

## Anatomy
The hip is a ball-and-socket joint consisting of:
- The socket (acetabulum), part of the pelvis bone
- The ball (femoral head), the upper end of the thighbone

A slippery tissue called articular cartilage covers both surfaces, enabling smooth, low-friction movement. The acetabulum is ringed by fibrocartilage called the labrum, which forms a gasket-like seal that stabilizes the joint.

## Description
In FAI, bony prominences develop on the femoral head and/or acetabulum either from how the hips formed at birth or from bone overgrowth (bone spurs). This extra bone prevents smooth movement and can eventually tear the labrum and break down articular cartilage, leading to osteoarthritis.

### Types of FAI
- **Pincer**: Extra bone extends over the acetabulum's normal rim, potentially crushing the labrum underneath.
- **Cam**: The femoral head lacks proper roundness, with a bump grinding against cartilage inside the socket.
- **Combined**: Both pincer and cam types are present simultaneously.

## Cause
FAI develops because hip bones don't form normally during childhood growth. When the hip bones are shaped abnormally, there is little that can be done to prevent FAI. Some people never experience problems despite having the condition, though symptoms typically indicate cartilage or labrum damage that may worsen. Athletic individuals may experience pain earlier due to more vigorous hip joint use, though exercise doesn't cause FAI.

## Symptoms
Common FAI symptoms include:
- Pain (often in the groin or outer hip area)
- Stiffness
- Limping

Turning, twisting, and squatting may trigger sharp pain, though symptoms sometimes appear as a dull ache.

## Home Remedies
Initially, identify activities that may have caused pain and modify them while allowing the hip to rest. Over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen and naproxen may help. However, persistent symptoms require medical evaluation, as the longer painful symptoms go untreated, the more damage FAI can cause in the hip.

## Doctor Examination

### Impingement Test
During physical examination, doctors typically perform an impingement test by bringing the knee toward the chest and rotating it inward. A positive result reproduces the hip pain.

### Imaging Tests
- **X-rays**: Show abnormally shaped bones and signs of arthritis.
- **CT scans**: Provide more detailed images of the hip's abnormal shape.
- **MRI scans**: Better visualize soft tissue damage to the labrum and cartilage, especially with injected dye.
- **Local anesthetic**: A numbing injection into the hip joint can confirm FAI if it provides temporary relief.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
- **Activity changes**: Avoid activities causing symptoms.
- **NSAIDs**: Prescription-strength anti-inflammatory medications reduce pain and inflammation.
- **Physical therapy**: Specific exercises improve hip range of motion and strengthen supporting muscles, relieving stress on injured structures.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is recommended when tests show joint damage and nonsurgical treatment fails. Arthroscopic surgery using small incisions allows surgeons to repair labrum and cartilage damage while trimming the acetabulum's bony rim and shaving the femoral head bump. Severe cases may require open surgery with larger incisions.

## Long-Term Outcomes
Surgery can successfully reduce impingement symptoms and prevent future joint damage. However, not all of the damage can be completely fixed by surgery, especially if treatment has been put off and the damage is severe. Although surgery offers no guarantee of success, it currently represents the best treatment approach for painful FAI.

## Future Developments
Improving surgical results suggest doctors will recommend earlier intervention. Computer-based navigation systems are advancing surgical techniques for bone removal procedures.
