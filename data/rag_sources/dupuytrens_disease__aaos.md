# Dupuytren's Disease

## Overview
Dupuytren's disease affects the palmar fascia, which is the fibrous layer of tissue that lies underneath the skin in the palm and fingers. The condition causes this tissue to thicken and contract over time, potentially pulling fingers inward toward the palm. This contraction may result in difficulty straightening fingers, known as a Dupuytren's contracture. In some cases, the worsening contracture interferes with hand function and daily activities, prompting treatment through nonsurgical or surgical interventions.

## Description
The palmar fascia normally anchors and stabilizes skin on the palm side of the hand. Without it, palm skin would be as loose as skin on the back of the hand. In Dupuytren's disease, this fascia gradually thickens and shortens.

The condition typically begins with lumps of tissue (nodules) forming under the palm skin, potentially followed by pitting — deep dents on the palm surface as diseased tissue pulls on overlying skin.

As the disease progresses, fascia bands in the palm develop into thick cords that can tether one or more fingers and the thumb into bent positions, creating a contracture. Although these cords may resemble tendons, tendons are not involved in Dupuytren's.

Many contractures progress very slowly over years and may remain mild enough to require no treatment. However, moderate or severe cases make straightening fingers difficult, necessitating treatment to reduce contracture and improve motion. Typically, as contractures worsen, fascial involvement becomes more severe, and treatment success decreases.

## Cause
The complete cause remains unknown, but evidence strongly suggests genetics plays the most important role.

Anecdotal reports describe Dupuytren's developing or worsening following hand injury or surgery, though no solid evidence supports this connection. Similarly, no convincing evidence indicates overuse causes the condition.

### Risk Factors
Multiple factors contribute to Dupuytren's development or progression:

- **Gender:** Biologic men develop the condition more frequently than biologic women
- **Ancestry:** People of northern European (English, Irish, Scottish, French, Dutch) and Scandinavian (Swedish, Norwegian, Finnish) descent show higher incidence
- **Heredity:** The condition frequently runs in families
- **Alcohol use:** Drinking alcohol may be associated with Dupuytren's
- **Certain medical conditions:** People with diabetes and seizure disorders have higher rates
- **Age:** Incidence increases with advancing age
- **Trauma:** The disease may appear or worsen following hand trauma, including surgery

## Symptoms
Dupuytren's contracture typically progresses very slowly over years. Signs and symptoms include:

**Nodules:** One or more small lumps may develop in the palm. These nodules typically attach to overlying skin and initially feel tender, though tenderness usually diminishes over time. Pitting, or deep dents, may appear in skin near nodules.

**Cords:** Nodules may thicken and contract, forming dense, tough tissue cords underneath the skin. These cords can limit or prevent finger and thumb straightening or spreading.

**Contractures:** As underlying tissue tightens, one or more fingers may pull toward the palm, with difficulty spreading them apart. The ring and little fingers are most commonly affected, though any finger or the thumb can be involved.

When fingers are affected:
- The metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint — where the finger meets the hand (knuckle) — is most commonly involved
- The proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint — the middle finger joint after the MCP — is the next most common. PIP contractures are harder to treat and less likely to completely recover than MCP contractures
- The distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint — the last finger joint near the fingernail — is less likely to be affected

The disease can be progressive, with increasing finger bending making full straightening difficult. Grasping large objects, placing hands in pockets, or performing simple activities may become challenging.

Some patients develop thickened tissue on feet (Ledderhose disease) or penis (Peyronie's disease). Symptoms typically do not occur elsewhere on the body.

## Doctor Examination
Your doctor will likely discuss your general health, medical history, and symptoms. Since Dupuytren's can run in families, the doctor may ask about relatives with the disease.

During the hand examination, your doctor will typically:

- Record nodule and cord locations on your palm
- Measure finger and thumb range of motion
- Test finger and thumb sensation
- In some cases, document hand appearance with clinical photographs

These measurements may be compared with future measurements to determine if the condition is progressing over time.

## Treatment
Currently, no cure exists for Dupuytren's; however, it is not life- or limb-threatening and is not cancer. The condition does not spread to vital organs.

Although effects vary by patient, Dupuytren's usually progresses very slowly and may not become problematic for many years. For some patients, the condition may never progress beyond palm lumps. If it does progress, doctors typically recommend nonsurgical treatment first to slow disease progression.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
**Steroid injection:** Corticosteroids are powerful anti-inflammatory medications that can be injected into painful nodules to reduce symptoms. Effectiveness varies by patient. While corticosteroids may help with symptoms, they do not prevent disease progression and thus represent treatment, not cure.

**Splinting:** Although patients often request splints to prevent contracture progression, splinting is not proven to prevent finger contracture progression. Forceful finger stretching is unhelpful and potentially injurious.

Splinting may protect surgical sites after Dupuytren's surgery; however, it remains unclear whether splinting reduces recurrent contracture risk or wound tightening during healing.

**Collagenase injection:** The collagen comprising Dupuytren's cords differs from collagen in other hand structures like tendons, nerves, blood vessels, and bones. An injectable medication exploits these differences.

Collagenases are enzymes breaking down collagen. A targeted collagenase specifically breaks down collagen in Dupuytren's cords without damaging other hand structures. When properly injected into a cord, collagenase can rupture the cord, allowing improved finger motion. While collagenase does not completely remove the cord, symptoms improve following treatment.

Collagenase treatment involves two steps:
- First, the cord receives medication injection, allowed to work for two to three days
- Then, at a separate visit, the doctor manipulates the finger until the cord ruptures

This non-operative alternative to surgery offers less pain and swelling than major surgery and may suit patients with multiple medical issues unsuitable for surgery. However, disease recurrence is higher with collagenase than surgery.

Although rare, injection may cause allergic reaction or flexor tendon rupture. Other potential complications match surgery risks: infection, nerve or blood vessel injury, pain, and stiffness.

**Needle aponeurotomy:** This office procedure with local anesthesia breaks up the Dupuytren's cord using a small needle placed into the cord and moved precisely until completely cutting through it. While not removing the cord, the break allows improved finger motion. Like collagenase injection, disease recurrence may be more likely than with surgery.

Rare complications include infection, nerve or blood vessel injury, pain, and stiffness.

### Surgical Treatment
If contracture interferes with hand function, your doctor may recommend surgery. The goal is reducing contracture and improving affected finger motion.

Although no known cure exists, surgery aims to "set back the clock" by reducing the cord's restricting effects through disruption or removal. Unfortunately, healing tissues form with similar cord development potential — but hand function gains remain significant.

The most commonly performed procedures are:
- Fasciotomy
- Partial palmar fasciectomy

**Fasciotomy:** Your doctor makes a palm incision, then divides thickened cord tissue. Although the cord itself remains, division decreases contracture and increases affected finger movement. Fasciotomy typically uses local anesthetic only, without sleep. After the procedure, the wound often remains open and heals gradually, and a splint is typically worn during recovery. Because diseased tissue remains unremoved, contracture recurrence may be more likely with fasciotomy than partial palmar fasciectomy.

**Partial palmar fasciectomy:** Your doctor makes an incision, then removes as much abnormal tissue and cords as possible to straighten fingers. Multiple incision types exist; however, "zig-zag" incisions are typically used, allowing skin healing without additional contracture. Sometimes the wound heals open gradually on its own after fasciectomy; other cases require skin grafts. A splint may be worn during recovery. Partial palmar fasciectomy is more extensive than fasciotomy, generally requiring more wound care, physical therapy, longer healing time, and greater patient recovery effort, but removes maximum diseased tissue, helping prevent disease recurrence.

In some very severe cases, doctors may apply a device to the affected finger before surgery that gradually stretches the finger and cords over about six weeks, potentially making subsequent surgery easier, safer, and more effective. The device is removed during the partial palmar fasciectomy.

**Complications:** Surgery for Dupuytren's contracture carries risks increasing with contracture severity, number of contractures addressed in a single procedure, and presence of other medical conditions. Possible risks include pain, scarring, nerve and/or blood vessel injury, wound infection, stiffness, loss of sensation (temporary loss may result from stretched contracted nerves), and loss of finger viability or finger loss (rare).

**Recovery:** Severe post-surgery problems are uncommon, but expect some pain, swelling, and stiffness. Although surgery aims to improve finger straightening ability, sometimes patients lose the ability to bend involved digits due to stiffness. Elevating the hand above the heart and gently moving fingers decrease swelling and improve stiffness. Physical therapy strengthens finger and hand function, reduces swelling, and aids wound care; hand therapists often create splints for recovery wear.

**Outcomes:** Most patients show improved finger movement after surgery. However, because surgery does not "cure" the condition, approximately 20% experience meaningful contracture recurrence. Some patients may require additional surgery.

## Summary
In general, Dupuytren's disease treatment can be quite successful. While many patients do not achieve 100% pre-disease digit motion return, symptoms often greatly improve following treatment, restoring good hand function.

Importantly, as Dupuytren's progresses, it becomes harder to treat and outcomes worsen. Therefore, if you have Dupuytren's or suspect you may have it, consulting a hand surgeon sooner rather than later ensures the best possible outcome.
