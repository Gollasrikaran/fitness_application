# Arthritis of the Knee

## Overview
Arthritis is inflammation of one or more of your joints. Pain, swelling, and stiffness are the primary symptoms of arthritis. The condition frequently affects the knee and can make everyday activities like walking or climbing stairs difficult. While there's no cure, many treatment options exist to manage pain and maintain activity levels.

## Anatomy
The knee comprises three bones: the femur (thighbone), tibia (shinbone), and patella (kneecap). The ends of the three bones that form the knee joint are covered with articular cartilage, a smooth, slippery substance that protects and cushions the bones. Two wedge-shaped cartilage pieces called menisci function as shock absorbers, while the synovial membrane releases lubricating fluid to reduce friction.

## Description

### Osteoarthritis
Osteoarthritis is the most common form of arthritis in the knee. It is a degenerative, wear-and-tear type of arthritis that occurs most often in people 50 years of age and older. The protective cartilage gradually wears away, becoming frayed and rough. This decreases the space between bones, potentially causing bone-on-bone contact and painful bone spurs.

### Rheumatoid Arthritis
Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic disease that attacks multiple joints throughout the body, including the knee joint. This autoimmune condition causes the synovial membrane to swell, resulting in pain and stiffness as the immune system damages cartilage and ligaments.

### Posttraumatic Arthritis
This form develops following knee injuries. Fractures, meniscal tears, and ligament injuries can cause instability and accelerated wear on the joint surface.

## Symptoms
Individuals with knee arthritis experience:

- Gradual or sudden pain development
- Stiffness and swelling, particularly in the morning or after rest
- Pain flare-ups with vigorous activity
- Joint locking or sticking from loose cartilage fragments
- Crepitus (creaking, clicking, snapping, or grinding noises)
- Weakness or buckling sensations
- Increased pain with weather changes

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
The physician evaluates:

- Joint swelling, warmth, or redness
- Knee tenderness
- Range of motion (passive and active)
- Joint instability
- Crepitus with movement
- Pain with weight-bearing
- Gait abnormalities
- Surrounding muscle, tendon, and ligament condition
- Involvement of other joints

### Imaging Tests
X-rays provide detailed pictures of dense structures, such as bone. They can help distinguish between various forms of arthritis. X-rays may reveal joint space narrowing, bone changes, and bone spur formation. MRI and CT scans occasionally assess bone and soft tissue conditions.

### Laboratory Tests
Blood tests help determine arthritis type, particularly for diagnosing rheumatoid arthritis.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment

**Lifestyle Modifications:**
- Minimize activities that aggravate symptoms
- Exercise is recommended for osteoarthritis to improve pain and function. Switching from high-impact activities (like jogging or tennis) to lower impact activities (like swimming or cycling) reduces knee stress
- Weight loss decreases joint stress

**Physical Therapy:**
Specific exercises improve range of motion, flexibility, and leg muscle strength through individualized programs.

**Assistive Devices:**
Canes, braces, or knee sleeves provide stability and support. "Unloader" braces shift weight from affected areas, while "support" braces stabilize the entire knee.

**Other Remedies:**
Heat, ice, or elastic bandages provide pain relief.

**Medications:**
- Over-the-counter pain relievers and anti-inflammatory drugs are typically first-line therapy
- NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) improve pain and function but require caution with certain health conditions
- Acetaminophen offers an alternative for NSAID-intolerant patients
- COX-2 inhibitors (Celebrex, Mobic) may reduce gastrointestinal side effects
- Topical NSAIDs are recommended for those unable to tolerate oral versions
- Oral narcotic medications are ineffective and should be avoided
- Corticosteroids (also known as cortisone) are powerful anti-inflammatory agents that can be injected into the joint. Effects are temporary; doctors typically limit injections to three or four yearly per joint
- DMARDs (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine) slow rheumatoid arthritis progression
- Glucosamine and chondroitin sulfate, substances found naturally in joint cartilage, can be taken as dietary supplements, though evidence doesn't support their effectiveness in reversing arthritis

**Alternative Therapies:**
These unproven approaches include acupuncture, magnetic pulse therapy, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), and stem cell injections. PRP uses a component of your own blood, platelets, that have been separated from your blood, concentrated, and injected into your knee. Stem cells may potentially grow into new tissue to heal damaged surfaces.

### Surgical Treatment

**Arthroscopy:**
Arthroscopic surgery is not often used to treat arthritis of the knee. In cases where osteoarthritis is accompanied by a degenerative meniscal tear, arthroscopic surgery may be recommended.

**Cartilage Grafting:**
Healthy cartilage from other knee areas or tissue banks fills cartilage defects, typically for younger patients with limited damage.

**Synovectomy:**
The damaged joint lining from rheumatoid arthritis is removed to reduce pain and swelling.

**Osteotomy:**
The tibia or femur is cut and reshaped to relieve joint pressure, suitable for early-stage osteoarthritis affecting one knee side.

**Total or Partial Knee Replacement:**
Damaged cartilage and bone are removed and replaced with metal or plastic joint surfaces.

## Recovery
Post-surgical recovery includes physical therapy to restore strength and range of motion. Patients may temporarily use knee braces, crutches, or canes. In most cases, surgery relieves pain and makes it possible to perform daily activities more easily.
