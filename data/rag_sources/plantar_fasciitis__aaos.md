# Plantar Fasciitis and Bone Spurs

## Overview
Plantar fasciitis is one of the most common causes of pain on the bottom of the foot at the heel. Approximately 2 million patients are treated for this condition every year. Heel pain commonly affects athletes due to overuse, overweight individuals due to overload, and people whose work requires extended periods of standing.

## Anatomy
The plantar fascia is a long, thin ligament beneath the skin on the bottom of the foot. It connects the heel to the front of the foot and supports the arch.

## Cause
The plantar fascia absorbs high stresses from walking, running, and sports activities. Excessive pressure can damage or tear the tissue, triggering the body's inflammatory response, which produces heel pain and soreness.

Risk factors include:
- New or increased activity
- Repetitive high-impact activity (running, dancing, sports)
- Prolonged standing on hard surfaces
- Foot anatomy — flat feet or a high arch
- Tight calf muscles
- Obesity
- Age — most common between 40 and 60

## Symptoms
- Pain on the bottom of the foot near the heel
- Pain during the first steps after waking or after periods of rest, which typically subsides within minutes
- Increased pain after (not during) exercise or activity

## Doctor Examination
The doctor checks for a high arch or flat foot, the area of maximum tenderness on the bottom of the foot just forward of the heel bone, limited upward motion of the ankle, and rules out other conditions such as Achilles tendinitis, a heel stress fracture, or nerve entrapment.

X-rays help rule out other causes of heel pain such as fractures or arthritis, and can show a heel spur, which can result from longstanding tension on the plantar fascia where it attaches to the heel. Notably, most people who have bone spurs on their heels do not have heel pain, so heel spurs are not the cause of plantar fasciitis pain and it can be treated without removing the spur. MRI and ultrasound are not routinely used to diagnose plantar fasciitis and are rarely ordered.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
More than 90% of patients with plantar fasciitis improve within 10 months of starting simple treatment.

- **Rest**: Reducing or stopping activities that worsen pain; a walking boot and crutches may be used temporarily.
- **Changing activity**: Substituting low-impact exercise such as cycling or swimming.
- **Stretching**: Plantar fasciitis is aggravated by tight muscles in the feet and calves, and stretching the calves and plantar fascia is the most effective way to relieve pain.
  - **Calf stretch**: Lean forward against a wall with one knee straight and heel on the ground, the other leg forward with a bent knee, pushing the hips toward the wall; hold 10 seconds, repeat 20 times per foot.
  - **Plantar fascia stretch**: Seated, cross the affected foot over the opposite knee, grasp the toes and slowly pull them toward you (using a towel if needed), holding 10 seconds and repeating 20 times per foot — best done in the morning before standing or walking.
- **Ice**: Rolling the foot over cold water or ice for 20 minutes, three to four times daily.
- **Physical therapy**: Stretching exercises for the calf muscles and plantar fascia, sometimes with specialized ice treatments, massage, and other inflammation-reducing therapies.
- **Supportive shoes and orthotics**: Shoes with thick soles and extra cushioning reduce pain with standing and walking; soft silicone heel pads, and pre-made or custom orthotics, can help. Worn athletic shoes should be replaced.
- **Night splints**: Most people sleep with their feet pointed down, which relaxes the plantar fascia and contributes to morning heel pain; a night splint stretches the fascia during sleep.

Other nonsurgical options if symptoms persist after several months:
- **Cortisone injections** into the plantar fascia to reduce inflammation and pain, though doctors may limit use since repeated steroid injections can weaken the fascia and risk rupture, foot flattening, and chronic pain.
- **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections** to promote healing without rupture risk, though costly and needing more research.
- **Botulinum injections** into the calf to relax the muscle and reduce tension, alongside continued stretching.
- **Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT)**: High-energy shockwave impulses meant to stimulate healing; results are mixed and it is uncommon, but is noninvasive and low risk, so it is sometimes tried before surgery.
- **Ultrasonic tissue repair**: A minimally invasive, ultrasound-guided probe used to break up and remove damaged fascia tissue.

### Surgical Treatment
Because more than 90% of patients recover with nonsurgical treatment, surgery is generally reserved for people who have not improved after 12 months of aggressive nonsurgical treatment.

- **Partial plantar fascia release**: Performed via an open approach (larger incision) or endoscopic approach (small incision with a camera) to partially cut the fascia at its heel attachment and relieve tension. Large bone spurs are rarely removed since spurs don't cause the pain. Protected weightbearing is recommended afterward.
- **Gastrocnemius recession**: Lengthens a calf muscle for patients who still have difficulty flexing the foot despite extensive calf stretching, since tight calf muscles place added stress on the plantar fascia. Performed via traditional open incision or a smaller incision with an endoscope. Complication rates are low but may include sural nerve damage or calf weakness.

Most patients have good results with surgery, but because it can result in chronic pain and dissatisfaction, it is recommended only after trying all nonsurgical treatments.
