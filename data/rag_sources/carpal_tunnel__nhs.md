# Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

## Overview
Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is pressure on a nerve in your wrist. It causes tingling, numbness and pain in your hand and fingers.

## Symptoms
Symptoms include an ache or pain in your fingers, hand or arm, a numb hand, tingling or "pins and needles", and a weak thumb or difficulty gripping. These symptoms typically develop gradually and are often worse at night, sometimes waking you up.

## Self-Treatment
Things you can try yourself to help with carpal tunnel syndrome:
- Wear a wrist splint at night for up to 6 weeks to hold the wrist straight and relieve pressure on the nerve
- Avoid activities that make symptoms worse, such as repetitive wrist bending or gripping
- Take over-the-counter painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen for short-term relief (these do not treat the underlying cause)
- Try hand exercises, which may help relieve symptoms for some people

## Seeing a GP
See a GP if your symptoms are not improving or keep coming back despite self-treatment. A GP will usually be able to diagnose carpal tunnel syndrome from your symptoms, though they may sometimes refer you for nerve conduction tests or other investigations.

## Medical Treatment
A GP may offer a steroid injection into the wrist to reduce swelling and pressure on the nerve. If symptoms are severe or do not improve with other treatments, surgery may be recommended. During surgery, a small cut is made in your hand and the carpal tunnel inside your wrist is cut so it no longer puts pressure on the nerve.

## Causes and Risk Factors
Carpal tunnel syndrome happens when one of the main nerves in the wrist (the median nerve) is squeezed or compressed. You're more likely to get carpal tunnel syndrome if you:
- are overweight
- are pregnant
- do repetitive activities with your hands or wrists, such as typing or using vibrating tools
- have a health condition such as rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, or an underactive thyroid
- have a family history of carpal tunnel syndrome
- have previously injured your wrist
