# NICE NG59: Low back pain and sciatica in over 16s — assessment and management

The NICE guideline NG59 covers the assessment and management (including self-management) of low back pain and sciatica in people aged 16 and over. The full guideline is published by NICE at nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59; this content reflects that guidance as reproduced on the NCBI Bookshelf.

## Self-Management

Healthcare professionals should provide people with advice and information, tailored to their needs and capabilities, to help them self-manage their low back pain (with or without sciatica), at all steps of the treatment pathway. This includes information about the nature of the pain and encouragement to maintain normal activities.

## Risk Stratification

The guideline recommends considering risk stratification tools (such as the STarT Back tool) at first contact with a healthcare professional for each new episode of low back pain, to inform shared decision-making about stratified management:
- Simpler and less intensive support (such as reassurance, advice to keep active, and guidance on self-management) for people likely to improve quickly and have a good outcome.
- More complex and intensive support (such as exercise programmes, with or without manual therapy or a psychological approach) for people at higher risk of a poor outcome.

## Imaging

Do not routinely offer imaging in a non-specialist setting for people with low back pain with or without sciatica. Imaging should only be considered in specialist settings of care (for example, spinal surgery or specialist spinal assessment services) if the result is likely to change management. Patients should be told that they may not need imaging if they are referred for specialist opinion.

## Exercise

Consider a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body, or a combination of approaches) within the NHS for people with a specific episode or flare-up of low back pain with or without sciatica. Take people's specific needs, preferences and capabilities into account when choosing the type of exercise.

## Manual Therapy

Manual therapy (spinal manipulation, mobilisation or soft tissue techniques such as massage) should only be considered as part of a treatment package that also includes exercise, with or without psychological therapy.

## Psychological Therapy

Psychological therapy using a cognitive behavioural approach should only be considered as part of a treatment package that includes exercise, with or without manual therapy.

## Pharmacological Management (for low back pain)

- Consider oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible period of time.
- Consider a weak opioid (with or without paracetamol) only if an NSAID is contraindicated, not tolerated, or has not been effective.
- Do not offer paracetamol alone.
- Do not offer opioids for managing chronic low back pain.

## Pharmacological Management (for sciatica)

- Do not offer gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids or benzodiazepines.
- Do not offer opioids for managing chronic sciatica.
- Be aware there is only limited evidence of benefit from NSAIDs for sciatica.

## Invasive Treatments

- Do not offer spinal injections for managing low back pain.
- Consider epidural injections for people with acute and severe sciatica.
- Consider referral for spinal decompression when non-surgical treatment has not worked and imaging findings are consistent with the person's symptoms.
- Do not offer spinal fusion for people with low back pain (unless as part of a randomised controlled trial).
