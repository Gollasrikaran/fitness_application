# NICE Guideline NG100: Rheumatoid Arthritis in Adults - Management (Recommendations)

Published: 11 July 2018. Last updated: 12 October 2020 (with further amendments dated 2024).

## 1.1 Referral, Diagnosis and Investigations

**Referral from primary care**: "Refer for specialist opinion any adult with suspected persistent synovitis of undetermined cause." Refer urgently (even with a normal acute-phase response, negative anti-CCP antibodies or rheumatoid factor) if the small joints of the hands or feet are affected, more than one joint is affected, or there has been a delay of 3 months or longer between symptom onset and seeking medical advice.

**Investigations for diagnosis**: Offer a blood test for rheumatoid factor in adults with suspected RA who have synovitis on clinical examination. Consider measuring anti-CCP antibodies if rheumatoid factor is negative. X-ray the hands and feet in adults with suspected RA and persistent synovitis.

**Investigations following diagnosis**: As soon as possible after diagnosis, measure anti-CCP antibodies (if not already done), X-ray the hands and feet for erosions (if not already done), and measure functional ability (for example using the Health Assessment Questionnaire, HAQ) as a baseline.

## 1.2 Treat-to-Target Strategy

"Treat active RA in adults with the aim of achieving a target of remission or low disease activity if remission cannot be achieved (treat-to-target)." Achieving the target may involve trying multiple conventional DMARDs (cDMARDs) and biological or targeted synthetic DMARDs with different mechanisms of action, one after the other. Consider a target of remission rather than low disease activity for people at increased risk of radiological progression. Measure CRP and disease activity (for example DAS28) monthly in specialist care until the target is achieved.

## 1.3 Communication and Education

Explain the risks and benefits of treatment options in ways that can be easily understood, and offer people the opportunity to talk about and agree all aspects of their care. Offer verbal and written information to improve understanding of the condition and counter misconceptions. Offer the opportunity to take part in educational activities, including self-management programmes.

## 1.4 Initial Pharmacological Management

For adults with newly diagnosed active RA: offer first-line treatment with cDMARD monotherapy (oral methotrexate, leflunomide or sulfasalazine) as soon as possible, ideally within 3 months of onset of persistent symptoms, escalating dose as tolerated. Consider hydroxychloroquine as an alternative first-line option for mild or palindromic disease. Consider short-term bridging treatment with glucocorticoids when starting a new cDMARD. Offer additional cDMARDs in combination (step-up strategy) when the treatment target has not been achieved despite dose escalation.

## 1.5 Further Pharmacological Management

For medicines recommended in NICE technology appraisal guidance for moderate RA (DAS28 3.2-5.1) or severe RA (DAS28 more than 5.1) that has inadequately responded to conventional DMARD combination therapy, refer to the relevant technology appraisals (for example upadacitinib TA744, adalimumab TA715, etanercept TA715, infliximab TA715, filgotinib TA676, sarilumab TA485, tofacitinib TA480, baricitinib TA466, abatacept TA375, tocilizumab TA247, and others). Biological or targeted synthetic DMARDs should generally be given in combination with methotrexate unless contraindicated or not tolerated. "Anakinra is not recommended for the treatment of RA, except in the context of a controlled, long-term clinical study," and the combination of TNF inhibitor therapy with anakinra should not be offered.

**Glucocorticoids**: Offer short-term glucocorticoid treatment for managing flares to rapidly decrease inflammation. In established RA, only continue long-term glucocorticoid treatment when the long-term complications have been fully discussed and all other treatment options have been offered.

## 1.6 Symptom Control

Consider oral NSAIDs (including cox-II selective inhibitors) when control of pain or stiffness is inadequate, taking account of gastrointestinal, liver and cardio-renal toxicity and individual risk factors. When treating symptoms with oral NSAIDs: "offer the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time," offer a proton pump inhibitor, and review risk factors for adverse events regularly.

## 1.7 The Multidisciplinary Team

Adults with RA should have ongoing access to a multidisciplinary team, providing periodic assessment of the effect of the disease on their lives (pain, fatigue, everyday activities, mobility, work, social/leisure activities, quality of life, mood, sexual relationships), and access to a named coordinating team member such as a specialist nurse.

## 1.8 Non-Pharmacological Management

**Physiotherapy**: Adults with RA should have access to specialist physiotherapy, with periodic review, to improve general fitness and encourage regular exercise, learn exercises for joint flexibility and muscle strength, and learn about short-term pain relief methods such as TENS and wax baths.

**Occupational therapy**: Access to specialist occupational therapy if there are difficulties with everyday activities or problems with hand function.

**Hand exercise programmes**: Consider a tailored strengthening and stretching hand exercise programme for adults with RA who have pain and dysfunction of the hands or wrists, delivered by a practitioner with training and skills in this area.

**Podiatry**: All adults with RA and foot problems should have access to a podiatrist, and functional insoles/therapeutic footwear should be available if indicated.

**Psychological interventions**: Offer psychological interventions (for example relaxation, stress management, cognitive coping skills) to help adults adjust to living with RA.

**Diet and complementary therapies**: There is no strong evidence that dietary changes benefit arthritis, though a Mediterranean diet can be encouraged. Complementary therapies may provide short-term symptomatic benefit for some but there is little evidence for long-term efficacy; they should not replace conventional treatment.

## 1.9 Monitoring

Ensure all adults with RA have rapid access to specialist care for flares, information about accessing specialist care, and ongoing drug monitoring. Consider a review appointment 6 months after achieving the treatment target to confirm it has been maintained. Offer all adults with RA, including those who have achieved the treatment target, an annual review to assess disease activity and damage, measure functional ability, check for comorbidities (including hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, osteoporosis and depression), assess complications, and assess the need for surgical referral. For adults who have maintained the treatment target for at least 1 year without glucocorticoids, consider cautiously reducing drug doses or stopping drugs in a step-down strategy. "Do not use ultrasound for routine monitoring of disease activity in adults with RA."

## 1.10 Timing and Referral for Surgery

Offer early specialist surgical opinion if persistent pain due to joint damage, worsening joint function, progressive deformity, or persistent localised synovitis do not respond to optimal non-surgical management. Refer urgently, before damage or deformity becomes irreversible, for imminent or actual tendon rupture, nerve compression (for example carpal tunnel syndrome), or stress fracture. Offer urgent combined medical and surgical management for suspected or proven septic arthritis. If cervical myelopathy is suspected, request an urgent MRI scan and refer for specialist surgical opinion. "Do not let concerns about the long-term durability of prosthetic joints influence decisions to offer joint replacements to younger adults with RA."
