# Cervical Radiculopathy (Pinched Nerve)

## Overview
Cervical radiculopathy, commonly called a "pinched nerve," occurs when a nerve in the neck is compressed or irritated where it branches from the spinal cord. This condition may cause pain radiating into the shoulder and/or arm, along with muscle weakness and numbness.

The condition typically stems from degenerative changes in the spine as people age, or from sudden injuries causing herniated disks. Most cases respond well to conservative treatment with medication and physical therapy, avoiding the need for surgery.

## Anatomy
The cervical spine consists of seven small vertebrae forming the neck, part of the spine's 24 stacked bones that create a protective canal for the spinal cord.

Key structural components include:
- **Spinal cord and nerves**: Electrical pathways carrying signals between brain and muscles, with nerve roots branching through openings (foramen) in vertebrae
- **Intervertebral disks**: Flexible shock-absorbing structures between vertebrae, approximately half an inch thick, composed of a tough outer ring (annulus fibrosus) and soft, jelly-like center (nucleus pulposus)

## Cause

### Degenerative Changes
As spinal disks age, they lose height and water content, becoming stiffer. This causes vertebrae to move closer together. The body responds by forming bone spurs around the disk, which can narrow the foramen and pinch nerve roots. These degenerative changes are called arthritis or spondylosis — normal changes occurring in nearly half of middle-aged and older people, though not all develop symptoms.

### Herniated Disk
A disk herniates when its jelly-like center pushes through the outer ring. If severely worn or injured, the nucleus may squeeze completely through, bulging toward the spinal canal and compressing the nerve root, causing pain and weakness. Herniated disks often occur with lifting, pulling, bending, or twisting movements.

## Symptoms
Pain typically starts at the neck and travels down the arm in the area served by the damaged nerve, described as "burning or sharp" neuropathic pain. Neck movements like extending, straining, or turning the head may increase pain.

Additional symptoms include:
- Tingling or "pins and needles" in fingers or hand (paresthesia)
- Weakness in arm, shoulder, or hand muscles
- Loss of sensation or numbness

Some patients report pain decreases when placing hands on top of their head, temporarily relieving nerve root pressure.

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
Your doctor will:
- Discuss your medical history and general health
- Ask about your symptoms
- Examine your neck, shoulder, arms, and hands for muscle weakness, sensation loss, or reflex changes
- Possibly request specific neck and arm movements to recreate or relieve symptoms

### Tests
- **X-rays**: Show bone alignment in your neck, reveal foramen narrowing, and display disk damage.
- **Computerized Tomography (CT) scans**: More detailed than plain X-rays, these help determine if bone spurs have developed near the foramen.
- **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans**: Create better images of soft tissues like muscle, fat, blood vessels, nerves, and fluids. Neck MRIs can show whether nerve compression results from bulging or herniated disks and reveal spinal cord or nerve root damage.
- **Electromyography (EMG)**: Measures electrical impulses in muscles at rest and during contractions. Combined with nerve conduction studies, these "electrodiagnostic tests" help determine if symptoms stem from spinal nerve root pressure and distinguish cervical radiculopathy from other diseases.

## Treatment
Most patients with cervical radiculopathy improve over time without treatment. Pain may resolve in days or weeks for some, longer for others. Improvement is common even when symptoms return later.

However, when cervical radiculopathy doesn't improve, evaluation and treatment become necessary.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial treatment is nonsurgical, including:

- **Soft cervical collar**: A padded ring wrapping around the neck, held with Velcro. It allows neck muscles to rest and limits motion, decreasing nerve root pinching. Should only be worn briefly, as prolonged use may weaken neck muscles.
- **Physical therapy**: Specific exercises relieve pain, strengthen neck muscles, and improve range of motion. Traction (gentle spine-aligned pulling) can stretch neck joints and muscles.
- **Medications**:
  - **Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)**: Aspirin, ibuprofen, and naproxen may provide relief for nerve irritation or inflammation-related pain
  - **Oral corticosteroids**: Short courses may relieve pain by reducing swelling and inflammation around the nerve
- **Steroid injection**: Steroids injected near the affected nerve reduce inflammation in specific areas. Injections can be placed between the laminae (epidural injection), in the foramen (selective nerve injection), or into the facet joint. While not relieving structural pressure from narrow foramens or herniated disks, they may lessen swelling and relieve pain long enough for nerve recovery.
- **Narcotics**: Reserved for severe pain unrelieved by other options. These medications, like opioids, are prescribed for limited periods only due to side effects and addiction potential. They should be used only as directed and discontinued once pain improves.

### Surgical Treatment
If nonsurgical treatment doesn't relieve symptoms after adequate time, surgery may be recommended. Several procedures exist for treating cervical radiculopathy, with the choice depending on factors like your specific symptoms and the involved nerve root's location.

## Cervical Radiculopathy "Mimickers"
Other diseases can cause similar symptoms, complicating diagnosis. Your symptoms, physical exam, and tests help determine pain's precise origin.

Conditions that may mimic cervical radiculopathy include:
- Shoulder conditions: AC joint arthritis, scapular dyskinesis, thoracic outlet syndrome, SC joint arthritis, suprascapular nerve compression
- Heart disease
- Elbow inflammation
- Nerve impingement elsewhere: ulnar nerve entrapment at the elbow (cubital tunnel syndrome), ulnar tunnel syndrome of the wrist, carpal tunnel syndrome
