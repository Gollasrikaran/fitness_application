# Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis is a long-term condition that causes pain, swelling and stiffness in the joints. It usually affects the hands, feet and wrists, and symptoms can flare up unpredictably. Some people also experience tiredness and weight loss.

## Symptoms

The main symptoms of rheumatoid arthritis are joint pain, swelling and stiffness.

- **Pain** - a throbbing and aching pain, often worse in the mornings and after a period of inactivity
- **Stiffness** - reduced mobility in the affected joints; morning stiffness from rheumatoid arthritis often lasts longer than the 30 minutes typical of osteoarthritis
- **Swelling, warmth and redness** - the lining of joints affected by rheumatoid arthritis becomes inflamed, causing them to swell and feel hot; firm swellings called rheumatoid nodules may develop under the skin around joints

General symptoms can include:
- tiredness and reduced energy
- a high temperature
- sweating
- a poor appetite
- weight loss

Inflammation can also affect other parts of the body, for example causing dry eyes if the eyes are affected, or chest pain if the heart or lungs are affected.

Symptoms may develop gradually over weeks, or progress rapidly over just a few days, and vary considerably between individuals.

## When to see a GP

See a GP if you have persistent pain, swelling or stiffness in your joints, even if a diagnosis hasn't been made. Early diagnosis and treatment helps prevent joint damage and reduces the risk of the condition getting worse.

## Causes

Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease, where the immune system mistakenly attacks the cells that line joints, causing them to become swollen, stiff and painful. Over time this can damage the joint itself, as well as nearby cartilage and bone.

Risk factors include:
- being female
- having a family history of rheumatoid arthritis
- smoking

The exact trigger for the immune system attack is not known.

## Treatment

There's no cure for rheumatoid arthritis, but early treatment and support - including medicine, lifestyle changes, supportive treatments and surgery - can reduce the risk of joint damage and help you stay as active as possible.

### Disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs)
DMARDs ease symptoms and slow the progression of the condition by blocking the effects of the chemicals released when the immune system attacks joints. A combination of DMARDs is usually given as initial treatment. Methotrexate is usually the first medicine given, often with another DMARD and a short course of steroids. Side effects can include feeling sick, loss of appetite, a sore mouth, diarrhoea, headaches and hair loss. Regular blood tests are needed to monitor effects on blood cells and the liver. It can take a few months to notice a DMARD working, so it's important to keep taking the medicine.

### Biological treatments
Biological medicines (such as adalimumab, etanercept and infliximab) are usually taken in combination with methotrexate or another DMARD, and are usually only used if DMARDs have not been effective. They're given by injection and work by stopping particular chemicals in the blood from activating the immune system. Side effects can include skin reactions at the injection site, infections, feeling sick, a high temperature and headaches.

### JAK inhibitors
A newer type of medicine available on the NHS for adults with moderate to severe rheumatoid arthritis, usually offered when DMARDs or biologicals have not worked or could not be used. Usually used in combination with methotrexate.

### Painkillers
- Paracetamol, or paracetamol combined with codeine, may help with pain but does not treat the underlying inflammation.
- NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, naproxen or diclofenac) help relieve pain while also reducing inflammation. NSAIDs can increase the risk of serious stomach problems such as internal bleeding, so you may also be given a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) to take alongside.
- Steroids can reduce pain, stiffness and inflammation, given as tablets, or as injections into a joint or muscle. They provide short-term relief, but long-term use carries risks including weight gain, osteoporosis, easy bruising, muscle weakness and thinning of the skin.

### Supportive treatments
- **Physiotherapy** can help improve fitness and muscle strength and make joints more flexible, including heat/ice packs and TENS.
- **Occupational therapy** provides training and advice to help protect your joints, including splints or other aids.
- **Podiatry** can help with foot problems, including joint support or shoe insoles to ease pain.

### Surgery
If medicine isn't preventing damage, surgery may be used to restore the ability to use a joint, reduce pain, or improve appearance. Options include carpal tunnel release, tendon repair, removal of inflamed tissue, arthroscopy, or joint replacement (which typically involves several days in hospital followed by months of rehabilitation; prosthetic joints usually last 10 to 20 years).

### Diet
There's no strong evidence that specific diets help, though some people find their symptoms worsen after certain foods. A Mediterranean-style diet - based on vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts, beans, cereals, grains, fish and unsaturated fats - is often recommended. Supplements such as calcium, vitamin D and folic acid may help prevent side effects of some medicines.

## Complications

Possible complications of rheumatoid arthritis include carpal tunnel syndrome, inflammation of other parts of the body (such as the lungs, heart or eyes), and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Proper management of the condition can help reduce the risk of these complications.
