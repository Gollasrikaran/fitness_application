# Achilles Tendinitis

## Overview
Achilles tendinitis is a common condition marked by irritation and swelling of the large tendon that runs down the back of the lower leg. The Achilles tendon is the largest tendon in the body, connecting the calf muscles to the heel bone (calcaneus). This tendon makes walking, running, climbing stairs, jumping, and standing on tiptoe possible. Despite its ability to withstand large forces during athletic activity, the Achilles tendon commonly develops tendinitis due to overuse.

## Description
Tendinitis refers to acute inflammation of a tendon, a normal response of the body to injury, typically showing up as swelling, pain, or irritation. The term "tendinopathy" describes a condition where a tendon has undergone microscopic breakdown from prolonged, cumulative damage. The terms tendinitis, tendinosis, and tendinopathy essentially describe similar underlying processes.

Achilles tendinitis is different from an Achilles rupture, in which the tendon tears away from the heel bone or splits lengthwise. Ruptures typically result from a sudden injury. This article focuses on tendinitis rather than rupture.

### Two Types

**Noninsertional.** This type affects the middle portion of the tendon (above where it attaches to the heel). Fibers in this area gradually break down, developing microscopic tears that lead to swelling and thickening. Younger, physically active people, especially runners, are more commonly affected.

**Insertional.** This type involves the lower part of the tendon, where it attaches to the heel bone. Damaged fibers may gradually calcify (harden). Bone spurs frequently form at the back of the heel with the insertional type. While it occurs across all activity levels, runners are still the most commonly affected group. Tight calf muscles frequently contribute to insertional tendinitis by creating excess stress at the attachment site.

## Cause
Achilles tendinitis is usually not linked to a specific injury. Instead, repeated stress on the tendon causes the condition, often from placing too much demand on the body without enough time to adapt. Contributing factors include:

- **A sudden increase in activity.** Dramatically increasing running distance without allowing the body to adjust creates irritation and inflammation.
- **Changes in footwear.** Switching shoes can increase strain on the Achilles tendon.
- **Tight calf muscles.** Restricted calf muscles pass on more stress, particularly at the point where the tendon attaches to the heel.
- **Haglund's deformity.** An abnormal bony enlargement at the back of the heel can irritate the tendon, causing inflammation and pain.

## Symptoms
Common symptoms include:

- Pain and stiffness along the Achilles tendon, especially in the morning
- Pain in the tendon or back of the heel that worsens with activity
- Severe pain the day after exercising
- Thickening of the tendon
- Bone spur formation (particularly with insertional cases)
- Persistent swelling that gets worse throughout the day with activity
- Heel pain aggravated by contact with the shoe

A sudden popping sound in the calf or heel can indicate a tendon rupture and warrants immediate medical evaluation.

## Doctor Examination
After discussing symptoms, the doctor will check for:

- Swelling along the tendon or back of the heel
- Bone spurs or pain in the lower part of the tendon at the heel (insertional presentation)
- Pain in the middle of the tendon (noninsertional presentation)
- Heel discomfort during calf stretching
- Limited ankle motion, specifically reduced ability to point the toes downward

## Tests
**X-rays.** X-rays clearly show bone and can reveal a heel spur at the back of the heel, characteristic of insertional tendinitis. Severe noninsertional cases may show calcification within the middle part of the tendon.

**MRI.** An MRI is not needed to confirm the diagnosis, but it becomes important for surgical planning if conservative treatment does not work. MRI provides detailed information about the extent of damage, which helps guide the choice of surgical approach.

**Ultrasound.** Many doctors are increasingly using ultrasound because it is faster and less expensive than MRI and can be performed in the office. However, results are less consistent than MRI because accuracy depends heavily on the skill of the person performing it.

## Nonsurgical Treatment
Most cases of Achilles tendinitis respond well to conservative treatment, though symptom improvement typically takes several months. Pain may persist beyond three months even with early treatment.

**Rest.** Reducing or stopping activities that worsen pain is the foundation of treatment. Replacing high-impact exercise (like running) with low-impact alternatives reduces stress on the Achilles tendon. Cross-training options such as cycling, elliptical machines, and swimming allow continued activity while reducing strain. Doctors may recommend an ankle brace or boot to support this transition.

**Ice.** Applying ice to the most painful area of the tendon is helpful and can be done repeatedly throughout the day. Sessions should last about 20 minutes at most, ending earlier if the skin becomes numb. A helpful trick is freezing water in a foam cup, then peeling back the rim to create a custom-shaped ice pack, as it can conform to the shape of the tendon with repeated use.

**NSAIDs.** Anti-inflammatory medications such as ibuprofen and naproxen reduce pain and swelling, making it easier to participate in physical therapy. However, these drugs do not reduce thickening in a degenerated tendon. Extended use beyond one month should be discussed with a primary care physician.

**Physical therapy.** Therapeutic exercise is particularly beneficial. Calf stretching involves leaning against a wall with one knee straight and heel on the ground, stepping the other leg forward with the knee bent, then pushing the hips toward the wall in a controlled way. Hold the stretch for 30 seconds and repeat 10 times per foot, aiming to feel it in the calf and heel cord without causing pain in the Achilles tendon itself.

Eccentric strengthening is particularly beneficial for noninsertional cases. Eccentric contraction means the muscle tightens while it lengthens. These exercises should initially be done under the supervision of a physical therapist, since performing them incorrectly risks damaging the tendon. Some discomfort may occur but should not become unbearable:

- **Bilateral heel drops.** Stand at the edge of a stair with only the front of the foot on the step, allowing the heels to move up and down. Keep balance and hold a rail for safety. Raise the heels, then slowly lower them as far down as possible. Repeat 20 times slowly and deliberately; moving too quickly risks injuring the tendon. Difficulty can be increased with hand-held weights as pain improves.
- **Single-leg heel drops.** Similar to the bilateral technique, but with all the weight on one leg; this should only be attempted after the bilateral version has been mastered.

(Note: heel drop exercises are for noninsertional cases only; they should be avoided with insertional tendinitis.)

**Cortisone injections.** Steroid injections into the Achilles tendon are not recommended, because they can lead to rupture. Research shows they significantly weaken tendon integrity.

**Platelet-rich plasma (PRP).** Other injectable substances, including PRP, have been studied for tendinitis. Several studies show pain improvement with PRP, but there isn't yet enough evidence to confirm its true effectiveness.

**Night splinting.** A removable brace that holds the foot with the toes pointed upward during sleep can provide substantial morning pain relief upon waking. This position keeps the calf flexible and reduces tension on the Achilles tendon.

**Supportive footwear and orthotics.** Insertional tendinitis often improves with specific shoes and orthotic devices. Softer shoes or those open at the heel reduce irritation of the tendon. Heel lifts are another option that decreases strain on the tendon. Severe pain may call for temporary use of a walking boot, allowing the tendon to rest before starting therapy. Extended use of a boot risks weakening the calf muscle.

**Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT).** This treatment applies low- or high-energy shockwaves to stimulate healing of damaged tendon tissue. High-energy versions can be completed in a single session, requiring local or general anesthesia. Low-energy versions are spread across three to four sessions and don't require anesthesia. Recent studies show improvements in pain and function, particularly when combined with eccentric exercises. More research is needed before it becomes a routine recommendation. ESWT has minimal risk and negligible complications, making it worth considering before surgery.

## Surgical Treatment
Surgery is only considered when pain persists beyond six months despite conservative treatment. The specific surgical approach depends on the location and extent of the tendinitis.

**Debridement.** This procedure involves removing ("cleaning up") damaged tissue. For insertional cases, removing the bone spur at the heel along with the damaged tendon tissue commonly produces good outcomes. After the unhealthy tissue is removed, the remaining tendon is reattached to the heel bone using metal or plastic anchors and strong sutures.

After debridement, most patients can walk within two weeks using a removable boot or cast, though the timeline varies with the severity of the tendon damage.

When more than half of the Achilles tendon needs to be removed, the remaining segments are not strong enough on their own. A tendon transfer becomes necessary to restore the ability to push off the ground. The flexor hallucis longus tendon (which controls downward flexion of the big toe) is the most common option for a transfer. Movement of the big toe remains intact afterward.

**Gastrocnemius recession.** This surgery lengthens the calf muscle. Because tightness in the calf increases stress on the Achilles tendon, this procedure benefits patients who continue to have limited ankle flexibility despite consistent stretching.

The procedure lengthens one of the two muscles that make up the calf, improving the ankle's range of motion. It can be done through a traditional open incision or minimally invasive approaches using an endoscope (a camera-containing instrument). Complication rates are low but can include nerve injury.

**Minimally invasive surgery.** Minimally invasive approaches are becoming more popular for various musculoskeletal conditions, including Achilles tendinitis. Techniques use small incisions that accommodate a camera and instruments to visualize and remove damaged, inflamed tendon tissue. Small openings also allow placement of anchors into the heel bone to secure the tendon in position.

Other minimally invasive surgeries use small holes to release portions of the tendon and muscle from the bone surface. These methods may become more common, but there is currently limited data on their effectiveness. They may also be less effective in patients with substantial tendon damage.

**Outcomes.** Achilles tendinitis surgery generally produces excellent results. Research on tendon debridement reports that about 75% of patients return to their pre-surgery activity level, with 90% patient satisfaction. Gastrocnemius recession similarly shows favorable outcomes, with substantial pain reduction and functional improvement after surgery.

The extent of tendon damage is the main factor determining recovery. More extensive tendon involvement is associated with a longer recovery and a lower chance of returning to sports.

Physical therapy is a critical part of recovery. Many patients need about 12 months of rehabilitation to reach maximum improvement.

Between 20% and 30% of patients experience some lingering pain after surgery, which is the most common complication. Wound infections can also develop and can be difficult to manage given the location of the surgery.
