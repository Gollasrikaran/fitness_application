# Elbow (Olecranon) Bursitis

## Overview
Elbow bursitis affects the olecranon bursa, a thin, fluid-filled sac that is located at the boney tip of the elbow. The body contains many bursae that serve as cushions between bones and soft tissues, containing lubricating fluid. When the olecranon bursa becomes irritated or inflamed, fluid accumulates and bursitis develops.

## Causes
**Trauma:** A forceful blow to the elbow tip can trigger excessive fluid production and swelling.

**Prolonged Pressure:** Extended leaning on the elbow against hard surfaces (tables, armrests) causes gradual swelling over months. Occupations like plumbing or HVAC work, requiring prolonged kneeling and elbow pressure, increase vulnerability. Athletic activities such as extended plank holds may also trigger this condition.

**Infection:** Breaks in the skin from insect bites, scrapes, or puncture wounds allow bacteria entry into the bursa sac, producing fluid, redness, swelling, and pain. Untreated infections may develop into pus. Occasionally, infection occurs without obvious skin injury.

**Medical Conditions:** Rheumatoid arthritis and gout are associated with elbow bursitis development.

## Symptoms
**Swelling:** Initial symptom, though loose elbow skin may delay detection of minor swelling.

**Pain:** As swelling increases, bursal stretching causes pain that worsens with direct pressure or bending. Swelling may restrict elbow motion.

**Redness and Warmth:** Infected bursae display red and warm skin. Untreated infection risks spreading throughout the arm or into the bloodstream. Infected bursae may spontaneously drain pus.

## Doctor Examination
After reviewing symptoms and history, physicians examine the affected arm and elbow.

**X-rays:** Doctors may order imaging to identify foreign bodies or bone spurs, which are often found on the tip of the elbow bone (ulna) in patients who have had repeated instances of elbow bursitis.

**Fluid Testing:** Doctors may extract bursal fluid samples via needle to determine infection or gout involvement.

## Nonsurgical Treatment
**For Infected Bursas:** Doctors may aspirate (withdraw) bursal fluid using a needle, typically performed in-office settings. This relieves symptoms and provides laboratory samples for bacterial identification and antibiotic sensitivity testing. Doctors often prescribe broad-spectrum antibiotics preemptively before specific infection identification.

**For Non-Infected Bursas:**
- Elbow pads for cushioning
- Activity modifications avoiding direct elbow pressure
- Splinting to maintain straight elbow position
- Oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs like ibuprofen for swelling reduction

If symptoms persist beyond 3-6 weeks, doctors may recommend bursal aspiration followed by corticosteroid injection. While some patients experience significant relief, some patients do not have any relief of symptoms with corticosteroid injections.

## Surgical Treatment
**For Infected Bursas:** When antibiotics fail or symptoms persist, complete bursa removal becomes necessary, potentially combined with continued oral or intravenous antibiotics. The bursa usually grows back as a non-inflamed, normally functioning bursa over a period of several months.

**For Non-Infected Bursas:** Surgery removes the bursa when conservative treatments fail, typically as outpatient procedures without disrupting muscles, ligaments, or joints.

**Recovery:** Post-surgical splinting protects skin. Casts or prolonged immobilization typically aren't necessary. Specific range-of-motion exercises begin within days. Skin healing occurs within 12-16 days, with full elbow use permitted after 3-4 weeks. Extended padding or protection may be needed for several months to prevent reinjury.
