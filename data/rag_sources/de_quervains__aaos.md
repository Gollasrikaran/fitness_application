# De Quervain's Tenosynovitis

## Overview
De Quervain's tenosynovitis is a painful condition affecting thumb tendons. The condition occurs when tendons that control thumb movement become irritated inside the narrow tunnel they pass through to get from the wrist to the thumb.

Pain and tenderness typically develop along the thumb side of the wrist, especially when:
- Moving the thumb
- Making a fist
- Grasping or gripping something
- Turning the wrist
- Lifting objects with arms extended forward and thumbs pointing upward

## Anatomy
Tendons are rope-like structures connecting muscle to bone, enabling movement. Two muscles have tendons running together through a narrow tunnel on the thumb side of the wrist:

- **Abductor pollicis longus (APL)**: helps move the thumb away from the hand
- **Extensor pollicis brevis (EPB)**: helps straighten the thumb

These forearm muscles have tendons wrapped in synovium, a slippery soft tissue layer that provides nutrients and allows smooth gliding. The tendons and sheaths sit within a protective tunnel keeping them close to bone as they cross from wrist to hand.

In De Quervain's tenosynovitis, these tendons become irritated and the sheath thickens, causing reduced space. The tendons no longer glide freely, resulting in increased friction and pain during thumb and wrist movements.

## Causes
The exact cause remains unclear, though wrist and thumb movements like typing, texting, or lifting can worsen symptoms. Potential risk factors include:

- Association with pregnancy and postpartum period (symptoms often appear 4-6 weeks after delivery)
- Increased susceptibility in people with rheumatoid arthritis
- Most common in people aged 40-50
- Affects more females than males

## Symptoms
Signs of De Quervain's tenosynovitis include:

- Pain on the thumb side of the wrist (main symptom), appearing gradually or suddenly, starting in the wrist and potentially traveling up the forearm; worsens with forceful grasping, lifting, or wrist twisting
- Swelling over the thumb side of the wrist and/or fluid-filled cyst in this area
- Catching or snapping sensation when moving the thumb
- Pain and swelling that make thumb and wrist movement difficult

## Diagnosis
Diagnostic approach includes:

- Medical history review covering symptoms, activities, and past hand or wrist injuries
- Physical examination with palpation along the thumb side of the wrist for pain and swelling
- Finkelstein/Eichhoff test: placing the thumb in the palm, grasping it with other fingers, and bending the wrist toward the little finger; pain indicates possible De Quervain's tenosynovitis
- X-rays and ultrasounds usually unnecessary unless diagnosis is unclear or other conditions need ruling out

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
- **Activity modification**: Avoiding pain-causing and swelling-inducing activities may allow symptoms to resolve independently
- **Splinting**: A removable splint keeping the wrist straight and thumb in a comfortable position may improve pain, particularly when worn at night
- **Icing**: Cold gel packs or ice applied to the painful, swollen area every 4-6 hours for 15 minutes (avoid direct skin contact)
- **Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)**: Oral or topical ibuprofen and naproxen may reduce swelling and relieve pain
- **Corticosteroid injections**: Can effectively address the condition by reducing swelling and relieving pain

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery may be recommended if symptoms are severe or persist beyond 6 weeks of nonsurgical treatment. The surgical goal is releasing the tendon sheath to create more room for irritated tendons, relieving symptoms without affecting hand or wrist function.

During surgery:
- The surgeon identifies and protects nearby nerves
- The sheath is released in a way avoiding tendon instability
- Excess tissue (tenosynovium) around tendons is removed if present
- The incision is closed and bandaging (sometimes with splinting) is applied

## Recovery
Most patients experience excellent outcomes with symptom relief through nonsurgical and/or surgical treatment:

- Some achieve relief through splinting or avoiding side-to-side wrist motions
- Many experience relief following corticosteroid injections
- Most surgical patients improve after the procedure
- Surgical complications are rare
- Patients with diabetes may have less successful injection outcomes and higher surgical complication risks

Normal hand use typically resumes once comfort and strength return. Success depends on understanding the diagnosis, following the treatment plan, and modifying or avoiding pain-worsening activities.
