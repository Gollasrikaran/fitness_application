# Morton's Neuroma

## Overview
People who feel like they are walking on a stone and have persistent discomfort in the forefoot may be dealing with Morton's neuroma. Despite the name, it is not truly a tumor but rather a thickening of the tissue surrounding one of the small nerves leading to the toes. This tissue thickening produces pain, tingling, or forefoot discomfort that can limit participation in various activities.

## Anatomy of the Forefoot
The forefoot contains 14 phalanges (toe bones) and five metatarsal bones connecting the forefoot to the midfoot. Between the metatarsal bases lie four web spaces:

- The first web space is between the big toe and second toe
- The second web space lies between the second and third toes
- The third web space lies between the third and fourth toes
- The fourth web space lies between the fourth and fifth toes

Sensation in the toes comes from the medial and lateral plantar nerves, which run along the sole of the foot and branch toward the toes. These branching nerves at the web-space level are called common digital plantar nerves. The transverse metatarsal ligaments connect the rounded ends of the metatarsals, and the common digital plantar nerves pass beneath these ligaments in the web space.

## Description
Other names for this condition include interdigital neuroma, degenerative compression neuropathy, and plantar neuroma. It occurs about eight times more often in women than men, typically in people between the ages of 30 and 60, though it can occur outside that range.

The third web space (between the third and fourth toes) is the most common location, typically developing after irritation, trauma, or excessive pressure. The second web space, between the second and third toes, is occasionally affected but less commonly. The primary symptom is pain or discomfort in the ball of the foot.

## Cause
Research suggests the underlying mechanism likely involves entrapment or compression of the common digital plantar nerve. Compression of the nerve within the web space may cause swelling and abnormal tissue growth around the nerve, though the exact cause is not fully understood.

## Symptoms
Visible signs like a lump or skin discoloration typically do not accompany Morton's neuroma. Common symptoms include:

- Persistent burning or sharp pain in the forefoot that may extend into the toes, especially with weight-bearing activities like walking or running (night pain is uncommon)
- A sensation of a pebble or marble under the ball of the foot while walking
- Pain typically in the third web space, occasionally in the second web space
- Possible numbness or unpleasant tingling in the toes
- Increased pain with activities requiring forefoot push-off, such as sprinting
- Aggravation from footwear: high heels (which position the foot similarly to sprinting) and tight, narrow shoes (which compress the toe bones and pinch the nerve)

## Diagnosis

### Medical History and Physical Examination
The doctor will ask about your overall health, medical history, and symptoms (location, nature, severity, duration), the specific activities that trigger pain, and what relieves it. The physical examination includes:

- Checking the skin of the foot and ankle for signs of excessive pressure
- Inspecting the toes and foot for any deformity that could contribute to forefoot pain
- Applying pressure to the web spaces to identify exactly where the pain is located
- Checking the range of motion of the toes and other joints to look for arthritis or inflammation
- Squeezing the foot to check for a "Mulder's click," which is sometimes present with Morton's neuroma
- Compressing the web space to try to reproduce the neuroma pain
- Injecting local anesthetic into the affected web space — pain relief afterward supports the diagnosis and also provides symptom relief

### Imaging Tests
X-rays may be used to rule out a stress fracture or arthritis in the toe joints. Ultrasound is the preferred imaging method for identifying a Morton's neuroma and can also help guide injections. MRI is generally not needed but may be considered in certain situations.

## Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial management typically includes one or more of the following:

- **Footwear changes.** Replacing high heels and tight, narrow shoes with wider shoes that have a roomier toe box, lower heels, and soft soles allows the metatarsal bones to spread and reduces pressure on the nerve.
- **Orthotics.** Custom shoe inserts and metatarsal pads or bars redistribute pressure in the forefoot and help separate the metatarsal bones, reducing pressure and irritation on the neuroma.
- **NSAIDs.** Ibuprofen or naproxen can reduce associated inflammation.
- **Corticosteroid injections.** One or more corticosteroid injections can reduce nerve swelling and inflammation, given in an orthopaedic office or by a specialist using ultrasound guidance.
- **Nerve-targeting procedures.** Minimally invasive options that disrupt nerve signaling include alcohol injections (chemically damaging the nerve), radiofrequency ablation (using heat to create a controlled nerve lesion), and cryoablation (using cold to temporarily disrupt nerve function, although long-term outcomes need further study).
- **Alternative therapies.** Second-line options such as extracorporeal shockwave therapy and platelet-rich plasma injections have limited research support and are typically used alongside other treatments.

Studies show that many patients achieve long-term relief from a combination of shoe changes, orthotics, anti-inflammatory medication, or corticosteroid injections.

## Surgical Treatment
When nonsurgical treatment fails after 3 to 6 months or symptoms come back, surgery may be recommended. Several surgical techniques produce similar success rates. The surgeon accesses the nerve through an incision on the top or bottom of the foot over the web space, then chooses between:

- **Decompression.** Releasing the surrounding tissue to relieve pressure on the nerve. This may preserve sensation but has slightly less predictable outcomes than removing the nerve. This approach has gained favor among surgeons since it preserves nerve function and limits post-surgical sensation problems.
- **Neurectomy.** Removing the diseased portion of the nerve. This provides high rates of pain relief but carries risks including permanent numbness and the formation of a "stump neuroma" — a new neuroma forming from the remaining end of the nerve.

Increasingly, surgeons use minimally invasive dorsal approaches with smaller incisions, which may reduce soft-tissue disruption and speed recovery. The specific approach depends on multiple factors that should be discussed with the surgeon.

## Recovery After Surgery
Post-operative protocols vary by surgeon, but generally:

- Patients may walk in a stiff-soled post-operative shoe, though weight-bearing may be limited to the heel if the incision was made on the bottom of the foot
- High-impact activities (running, jumping) and submerging the foot in water should be avoided until the surgical wound has fully healed — typically at least 2 to 3 weeks after surgery
- Return to normal footwear commonly occurs by about four weeks after the procedure

Surgery is the most reliable treatment, with numerous studies reporting 80% to 95% long-term success rates.

## Treatment Outcomes
- Conservative measures (shoe changes, activity modification, orthotics) provide relief in roughly 50% of patients
- Corticosteroid injections produce short-term relief in about 50% to 70% of patients, though long-term relief is less common
- Chemical and radiofrequency ablation achieve good success, with 70% to 80% of patients improving
- Neurectomy or decompression has the highest success rate, with most studies reporting 80% to 95% success

Possible complications after otherwise successful treatment include:

- Ongoing mechanical overload after decompression, causing incomplete relief and continuing symptoms
- A risk of permanent numbness after neurectomy
- A risk of stump neuroma formation, which can produce similar symptoms without being a true recurrence

About 5% to 20% of patients experience recurrence of symptoms requiring further treatment. Repeat surgery for recurrence achieves similar success rates (80% to 95%) as initial surgery.
