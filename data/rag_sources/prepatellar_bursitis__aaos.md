# Prepatellar (Kneecap) Bursitis

## Overview
Bursae are small, fluid-filled sacs located throughout the body, including around the shoulder, elbow, hip, knee, and heel. They function as cushions to help reduce friction between bones and soft tissues.

Prepatellar bursitis involves inflammation of the bursa at the patella (front of the kneecap). The condition develops when the bursa becomes irritated and produces too much fluid, which causes it to swell and put pressure on the nearby parts of the knee.

## Cause
Prepatellar bursitis frequently results from pressure due to constant kneeling. Workers in plumbing, roofing, carpet laying, coal mining, and gardening face elevated risk.

A direct blow to the knee's front can also trigger the condition. Athletes in contact sports — football, wrestling, basketball — face greater risk.

Individuals with rheumatoid arthritis or gout are more vulnerable. Additionally, bacteria may get inside the bursa sac and cause an infection if skin injuries like insect bites, scrapes, or puncture wounds occur. This infectious form is less common, but more serious and must be treated more urgently.

## Symptoms
- Pain with activity, typically not occurring at night
- Rapid swelling on the kneecap's front
- Tenderness and warmth to the touch
- Infection-related bursitis may produce fluid, redness, fever, and chills

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
Your physician will inquire about symptom severity, symptom duration, and risk factors. They will specifically ask about infection signs like fever or chills, since prepatellar bursitis caused by an infection requires a different treatment plan.

During examination, your doctor will inspect the affected knee, comparing it to the healthy one, assessing tenderness, range of motion, and pain-related movement restrictions.

### Tests
**X-rays:** These provide clear pictures of dense structures like bone and help rule out fractures or other bone problems.

**Other imaging tests:** While the diagnosis of bursitis is usually made on physical examination, doctors may order CT or MRI scans to check for additional soft tissue injuries.

**Aspiration:** If infection is suspected, they may use a needle to drain fluid from the bursa and send this sample to the lab for analysis.

## Treatment
Nonsurgical treatment is typically effective for non-infected inflammation:

- **Activity modification:** Avoid aggravating activities and substitute low-impact exercise like cycling or elliptical machines until bursitis resolves.
- **Ice:** Apply ice 3 or 4 times a day for 20 minutes at a time.
- **Elevation:** Raise the affected leg except when walking.
- **NSAIDs:** Medications like naproxen and ibuprofen relieve pain and reduce inflammation. Acetaminophen serves as an alternative if NSAIDs are contraindicated.

If conservative measures fail, your doctor may drain (aspirate) the bursa with a needle, then inject the bursa with a corticosteroid. This anti-inflammatory injection is stronger than oral medications.

Infectious bursitis requires antibiotics initially, with surgical drainage if antibiotics prove insufficient. Chronic swelling causing disability may warrant surgical bursa removal, after which the knee should regain its flexibility in a few days, and you can resume normal activities in a few weeks.

## Prevention
- Wear kneepads during knee work or contact sports
- Rest knees regularly with stretching breaks and activity rotation
- Apply ice and elevate knees after workouts
