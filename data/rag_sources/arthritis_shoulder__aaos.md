# Arthritis of the Shoulder

## Overview
Arthritis involves inflammation of one or more joints, ultimately causing cartilage loss that enables smooth movement. Shoulder arthritis creates pain and stiffness, making everyday tasks difficult. While cartilage cannot reliably regrow, most people with arthritis are able to manage pain and stay active through various treatment approaches.

## Anatomy
The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint comprising three bones:
- The humerus (upper arm bone)
- The scapula (shoulder blade)
- The clavicle (collarbone)

The humeral head fits into the glenoid socket in the shoulder blade. Muscles and tendons called the rotator cuff maintain proper arm positioning.

Two joints may be affected by arthritis:
- The acromioclavicular (AC) joint where the clavicle meets the acromion
- The glenohumeral joint where the humerus head fits into the scapula

## Description: Five Major Types
- **Osteoarthritis** ("wear-and-tear" arthritis) destroys articular cartilage, causing bones to rub together painfully. It typically affects people over 50, more commonly in the AC joint than the glenohumeral joint.
- **Rheumatoid Arthritis** is a chronic autoimmune disease affecting multiple joints symmetrically. The synovium lining swells, causing pain and stiffness while damaging cartilage and softening bone.
- **Posttraumatic Arthritis** develops following injuries like fractures or dislocations, affecting both the AC and glenohumeral joints.
- **Rotator Cuff Tear Arthropathy** occurs after large, long-standing rotator cuff tears, allowing the humerus to move upward and damage bone surfaces.
- **Avascular Necrosis** results from disrupted blood supply to the humeral head. Bone cells die without adequate circulation, gradually collapsing and damaging cartilage. Causes include high-dose steroids, heavy alcohol consumption, sickle cell disease, traumatic injury, or idiopathic origin.

## Symptoms
- **Pain** is the most common symptom, worsening with activity and over time. Location varies by affected joint: glenohumeral pain centers in the shoulder's side or back and may intensify with weather changes; AC joint pain focuses on the shoulder's top. Rheumatoid arthritis may cause shoulder-wide pain. Night pain is common, disrupting sleep.
- **Limited Range of Motion** makes lifting and reaching difficult.
- **Crepitus** (grinding, clicking, or snapping sounds) may occur during movement, sometimes painfully and audibly to others.

## Doctor Examination
### Medical History and Physical Examination
The doctor assesses muscle weakness, tenderness, passive and active range of motion, injury signs, previous surgeries, joint involvement patterns, crepitus, and pain with pressure.

### X-Rays
X-rays reveal:
- Narrowed joint space
- Bone changes
- Bone spurs (osteophytes)

Some patients show X-ray arthritis without corresponding symptoms; surrounding muscle soreness may be responsible. Doctors may inject local anesthetic with or without cortisone to confirm whether arthritis causes the pain.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial approaches include:
- Rest or activity modifications
- Physical therapy for range of motion, strength, and function
- NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen) for inflammation and pain — take with food and consult your doctor if you have ulcer history or take blood thinners
- Corticosteroid injections for dramatic but often temporary relief
- Moist heat for temporary relief
- Ice application for 20-30 minutes, 2-3 times daily (avoiding direct skin contact)
- Disease-modifying drugs like methotrexate for rheumatoid arthritis
- Dietary supplements such as glucosamine and chondroitin sulfate (noting limited scientific evidence and that they are not FDA-regulated)
- Joint lubricants (ineffective for shoulder arthritis, not recommended)

### Surgical Treatment
- **Arthroscopy** treats mild glenohumeral arthritis using a small camera and miniature instruments through small incisions. The surgeon can remove loose cartilage, clean inflammation, and shave bone spurs. Pain relief is often temporary and not recommended for advanced arthritis.
- **Shoulder Joint Replacement (Arthroplasty)** for advanced glenohumeral joint arthritis includes:
  - Hemiarthroplasty: Replacing only the humeral head
  - Total Shoulder Arthroplasty: Replacing both the humeral head and glenoid with a plastic cup and metal ball
  - Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty: Positioning components opposite conventional replacement, relying on the deltoid muscle rather than the rotator cuff — better for cuff tear arthropathy
- **Distal Clavicle Resection/Excision** (most common AC joint surgery) removes bone from the collarbone's end, allowing scar tissue to fill the space.

## Recovery
Surgical outcomes are generally effective. Recovery time depends on surgery type and factors including comorbidities, smoking status, and depression.

- **Pain Management**: Post-operative pain is normal. Doctors prescribe medications including opioids, NSAIDs, and local anesthetics, using combinations to minimize opioid needs. Use opioids only as directed, minimally, briefly, and discontinue when pain improves.
- **Complications** may include infection, excessive bleeding, blood clots, or blood vessel/nerve damage.

## Future Developments
Research continues on arthritis causes, new rheumatoid arthritis medications, shoulder replacement designs, and biologic materials for resurfacing arthritic shoulders.
