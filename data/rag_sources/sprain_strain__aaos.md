# Sprains, Strains & Other Soft-Tissue Injuries

## Overview
Soft-tissue injuries affect muscles, tendons, and ligaments, and typically occur during sports and exercise. Common examples include sprains, strains, and contusions, as well as tendinitis and bursitis, and even with appropriate treatment these injuries may require a long time to heal.

## Cause: Acute vs. Overuse Injuries
Soft-tissue injuries fall into two basic categories:

**Acute injuries** are caused by a sudden trauma, such as a fall, twist, or blow to the body. Examples include sprains, strains, and contusions.

**Overuse injuries** occur gradually over time when an athletic or other activity is repeated so often that the soft tissues do not have enough time to recover. Tendinitis and bursitis are examples of overuse injuries.

## RICE Protocol for Acute Injuries
Initial treatment for most acute soft-tissue injuries follows the RICE protocol:
- **Rest**: Take a break from the activity that caused the injury. Your doctor may recommend that you use crutches to avoid putting weight on an injured leg.
- **Ice**: Use cold packs for 20 minutes out of 1 hour, several times a day. Do not apply ice directly to the skin.
- **Compression**: To help stop swelling, wear an elastic compression bandage.
- **Elevation**: To reduce swelling, elevate the injury higher than your heart while resting.

## Sprains
A sprain is a stretch and/or tear of a ligament, a strong band of connective tissue that connects the end of one bone with another. Ligaments stabilize and support the body's joints. The most vulnerable areas for sprains are the ankles, knees, and wrists -- for example, ankle sprains from the foot turning inward, knee sprains from a sudden twist, and wrist sprains from a fall onto an outstretched hand.

Sprains are classified by severity:
- **Grade 1 (mild)**: Slight stretching and some damage to the fibers of the ligament.
- **Grade 2 (moderate)**: Partial tearing of the ligament, with abnormal looseness (laxity) in the joint.
- **Grade 3 (severe)**: Complete tear of the ligament, causing significant instability.

Common symptoms across all grades include pain, bruising, swelling, and inflammation.

**Treatment** begins with the RICE protocol and physical therapy. Moderate sprains often require bracing (for example, CAM walking boots for ankle sprains, or hinged knee braces). Severe sprains may require surgery to repair torn ligaments.

## Strains
A strain is an injury to a muscle and/or tendon. Tendons are fibrous cords of tissue that attach muscles to bone. Common locations for strains include the back, neck, and legs (particularly the hamstring).

A strain may involve a simple stretch of a muscle or tendon, or it may involve a partial or complete tear. Symptoms include pain, muscle spasm, muscle weakness, swelling, inflammation, and cramping.

Sports at risk for strains include contact sports (soccer, football, hockey, boxing, wrestling) and quick-start sports (basketball, sprinting), which can cause hamstring strains; running-heavy sports (football, soccer, tennis, basketball), which can cause calf strains; gymnastics, tennis, rowing, and golf, which can cause hand strains through extensive gripping; and racquet, throwing, and contact sports, which can cause elbow strains.

**Treatment** is the same as for sprains: rest, ice, compression, and elevation, followed by exercises to relieve pain and restore mobility. Surgery may be needed for severe tears.

## Contusions (Bruises)
Contusions occur when a direct blow (or repeated blows) by a blunt object strikes part of the body, crushing underlying muscle fibers and connective tissue without breaking the skin. Discoloration results from blood pooling around the injury.

Most contusions respond well to the RICE protocol. Quadriceps contusions specifically benefit from a compression wrap and temporary immobilization of the thigh with the knee in a bent position. Persistent symptoms warrant medical evaluation to prevent permanent damage.

## Tendinitis
Tendinitis is an inflammation or irritation of a tendon or the covering of a tendon (called a sheath). It results from a series of small stresses that repeatedly aggravate the tendon, with symptoms of swelling and pain that worsens with activity.

Sports with high risk for tendinitis include overhead sports (baseball, softball, swimming, tennis, volleyball, golf), which affect the shoulders and elbows, and running and jumping sports (soccer, basketball, running, aerobic dancing), which affect the knees and ankles.

**Treatment** includes rest to eliminate stress, anti-inflammatory medication, steroid injections, splinting, and corrective exercises. Persistent inflammation may cause significant damage to the tendon, which may require surgery.

## Bursitis
Bursae are small, jelly-like sacs located throughout the body, including around the shoulder, elbow, hip, knee, and heel. They contain fluid and act as cushions to reduce friction. Bursitis results when repeated small stresses and overuse cause the bursa to swell, and it often accompanies tendinitis.

**Treatment**: Activity changes and anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen typically provide relief. If conservative measures fail, doctors may remove fluid and inject corticosteroids -- the steroid medication is an anti-inflammatory drug that is stronger than the medication that can be taken by mouth. Surgery is rarely needed unless infection occurs; removal may be performed via standard incision (open procedure) or as an arthroscopic procedure with small incisions.

## Prevention
Injuries often occur when people suddenly increase the duration, intensity, or frequency of their activities. Many soft-tissue injuries can be prevented through proper conditioning and training. Specific prevention strategies include:

- **Equipment**: Replace athletic shoes as they wear out, and wear comfortable, loose-fitting clothes that let you move freely.
- **Fitness balance**: Develop a balanced fitness program that incorporates cardiovascular exercise, strength training, and flexibility. Add activities and new exercises cautiously, no more than one or two new activities per workout.
- **Warm-up**: Warm up to prepare for exercise, even before stretching. Warming up increases your heart and blood flow rates and loosens up muscles, tendons, ligaments, and joints.
- **Stretching**: Begin stretches slowly and carefully until reaching a point of muscle tension. Hold each stretch for 10 to 20 seconds, then slowly release. Never stretch to the point of pain, always maintain control, and never bounce on a muscle that is fully stretched.
- **Hydration**: Drink 1 pint of water 15 minutes before you start exercising and another pint after you cool down. Have a drink of water every 20 minutes or so while you exercise.
- **Cool-down**: Make cooling down the final phase of your exercise routine. It should take twice as long as your warm-up, for at least 10 minutes before you stop completely.
- **Rest and pacing**: Schedule regular days off from vigorous exercise and rest when tired. Fatigue and pain are good reasons not to exercise. Avoid being a "weekend warrior"; aim for at least 30 minutes of moderate physical activity every day, and resume sports slowly after extended breaks.
