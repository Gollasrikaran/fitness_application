## What is Carpal Tunnel Syndrome?

Carpal tunnel syndrome occurs when the median nerve, which runs from the forearm into the palm of the hand, becomes pressed or squeezed at the wrist. The condition involves a narrow passageway containing both nerve and tendons. Thickening from irritated tendons or other swelling narrows this tunnel, compressing the nerve.

Symptoms typically develop gradually, beginning with numbness and tingling in the hand and wrist. As the condition progresses, grasping objects becomes increasingly difficult. Women experience carpal tunnel syndrome three times more frequently than men.

Contributing factors include trauma or wrist injury, thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and pregnancy-related fluid retention. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial to prevent permanent nerve damage.

## What is the Outlook for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome?

The condition responds well to treatment, though outcomes vary. Notably, less than half of individuals report their hand(s) feeling completely normal following surgery, with residual numbness or weakness remaining common after surgical intervention.

Prevention strategies at work include stretching exercises, frequent rest breaks, wrist splints, proper posture, and wearing fingerless gloves for temperature regulation.

## What are the Treatments for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome?

Initial management typically involves immobilizing the wrist with a splint, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs to reduce swelling, and corticosteroid injections. For more severe cases, surgical intervention may be recommended when conservative approaches prove insufficient.
