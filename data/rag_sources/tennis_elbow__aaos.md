# Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis)

## Overview
Tennis elbow, or lateral epicondylitis, is a painful condition of the elbow caused by overuse, involving degeneration or microtearing of the tendons that join the forearm muscles on the outside of the elbow. Treatment for tennis elbow in most cases involves a team approach with primary care doctors, physical or occupational therapists, and sometimes surgeons working together.

## Anatomy
The elbow joint is made up of the humerus (upper arm bone) and the radius and ulna (forearm bones). Bony prominences called epicondyles are where forearm muscles begin; the bony prominence on the outside of the elbow is the lateral epicondyle. Tennis elbow involves the muscles and tendons responsible for extending the wrist and fingers, particularly the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon, which connects the ECRB forearm muscle to the lateral epicondyle.

## Cause
- **Overuse**: The ECRB muscle helps stabilize the wrist when the elbow is straight. When weakened from overuse, microscopic tears form in the ECRB tendon where it attaches to the lateral epicondyle. The muscle's anatomical position contributes to wear and tear as the elbow bends and straightens.
- **Activities**: Beyond athletes, many people with tennis elbow have jobs or hobbies requiring repetitive, vigorous forearm use — painters, plumbers, carpenters, auto workers, cooks, and butchers have higher rates.
- **Age**: Most people who get tennis elbow are between 30 and 50, although anyone with risk factors can develop it.
- **Unknown cause**: Lateral epicondylitis can occur without any recognized repetitive injury — this is called idiopathic (of unknown cause).

## Symptoms
Symptoms develop gradually; in most cases pain begins as mild and slowly worsens over weeks and months. Common signs include pain or burning on the outer elbow, weak grip strength, and sometimes pain at night. Symptoms are often worsened by forearm activity such as holding a racquet, turning a wrench, or shaking hands. The dominant arm is typically affected, though both arms can be involved.

## Doctor Examination
The doctor considers how the symptoms developed and any occupational or recreational risk factors, discussing which activities trigger symptoms and where the pain is located. During examination, the doctor applies gentle pressure to the lateral epicondyle to check for pain and tenderness, and may test resistance by having the patient try to straighten the wrist and fingers with the arm fully extended.

Tests that may be used:
- **X-rays**: Rule out elbow arthritis.
- **MRI scans**: Show soft-tissue detail, the extent of tendon damage, or rule out cervical (neck) issues.
- **Electromyography (EMG)**: Rules out nerve compression, since several nerves around the elbow can cause similar symptoms.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Approximately 80 to 95% of patients have success with nonsurgical treatment.

- **Rest**: Stopping or decreasing participation in sports, heavy work, and other symptom-triggering activities for several weeks.
- **Physical or occupational therapy**: Exercises to strengthen the forearm muscles; therapists may also use ultrasound, ice massage, or muscle-stimulating techniques.
- **Brace**: A brace centered over the back of the forearm can rest the muscles and tendons and relieve symptoms.
- **Steroid injections**: Cortisone is a very effective anti-inflammatory medicine, but should be used sparingly for lateral epicondylitis since excessive use can weaken the lateral epicondyle over time.
- **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)**: Blood is drawn and centrifuged to concentrate platelets and their growth factors, which are then injected; results have been mixed, though some studies show promise.
- **Extracorporeal shock wave therapy**: Sound waves create microtrauma intended to promote healing; many doctors consider it experimental.
- **Equipment check**: For racquet sports, ensuring proper equipment fit — stiffer or looser-strung racquets can reduce forearm stress.

### Surgical Treatment
If symptoms do not respond after 6 to 12 months of nonsurgical treatment, surgery may be recommended. Most procedures involve removing diseased tendon and reattaching healthy tendon to bone; the approach depends on the scope of the injury, general health, and personal needs.

- **Open surgery**: The most common approach, involving an incision over the outside of the elbow, usually performed as outpatient surgery.
- **Arthroscopic surgery**: Uses miniature instruments and small incisions, also outpatient.

Surgical risks include infection, nerve and blood vessel damage, prolonged rehabilitation, loss of strength, loss of flexibility, and the possible need for further surgery. After surgery, the arm may be splinted temporarily; sutures and splint are typically removed about a week later, stretching exercises begin after splint removal, light strengthening starts around 2 months after surgery, and return to athletic activity typically occurs 4 to 6 months after surgery. Tennis elbow surgery is considered successful in 80 to 90% of patients, though some loss of strength is not uncommon.
