# Fibromyalgia

Fibromyalgia, also called fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS), is a long-term condition that causes pain all over the body. Around 1 in 20 people may be affected by fibromyalgia to some degree, and it's more common in women than men. It usually develops between the ages of 25 and 55, but can also affect children and older people. Diagnosis can be difficult as there's no specific test for it.

## Symptoms

The main symptom is widespread pain, which can vary in severity, sometimes depending on the weather, how stressed you are, and how physically active you are that day.

- **Widespread pain** - pain felt throughout the body, though it may be worse in particular areas such as the back or neck; described as an ache, a burning sensation, or a sharp, stabbing pain
- **Increased sensitivity to pain** - hyperalgesia (an extreme sensitivity to pain) and allodynia (pain caused by things that should not be painful, such as a light touch); people may also be sensitive to smoke, certain foods, and bright lights
- **Stiffness** - may be most severe when you've been in the same position for a long period, such as when you first wake up in the morning
- **Fatigue** - can range from a mild, tired feeling to the exhaustion often felt during a flu-like illness, and can come on suddenly
- **Poor sleep quality** - the condition can prevent you sleeping deeply enough to feel properly refreshed, even after a full night's sleep
- **Cognitive problems ("fibro-fog")** - problems with memory and concentration, and slowed or muddled speech
- Headaches
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
- Feeling dizzy and clumsy
- Sensitivity to temperature
- Restless legs syndrome
- Tingling, numbness, prickling or burning sensations in the hands and feet
- Painful periods in women
- Anxiety and depression

Symptoms can fluctuate depending on time and circumstances, and can sometimes suddenly improve or get worse.

## Causes

The exact cause of fibromyalgia isn't known, but it's thought to be related to abnormal levels of certain chemicals in the brain and changes in the way the central nervous system processes pain messages carried around the body. It's also thought some people are more likely to develop fibromyalgia because of genes inherited from their parents. In many cases, the condition seems to be triggered by a physically or emotionally stressful event, such as an injury or infection.

## Treatment

Treatment for fibromyalgia aims to ease symptoms and improve quality of life. No single treatment works for all symptoms - a combination is often needed.

- **Exercise** - regular exercise has been shown to help reduce pain and improve overall quality of life; a GP may be able to refer you to a supervised exercise programme
- **Talking therapies:**
  - Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) - learning to accept what is out of your control and commit to making changes that will improve your life
  - Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) - a talking therapy that can help you manage your problems by changing the way you think and behave
- **Medicine** - antidepressants such as amitriptyline, citalopram, duloxetine, fluoxetine, paroxetine or sertraline are sometimes prescribed; these medicines are usually only recommended for treating long-term pain in people aged 18 or over
- **Acupuncture** can reduce pain and improve quality of life in some people with long-term pain, though the benefits typically last only up to 3 months
