## What is Gout?

Gout is a common, painful form of arthritis. It causes swollen, red, hot and stiff joints. The condition develops when uric acid builds up in the body, forming needle-like crystals in joints. It typically first attacks the big toe but can affect ankles, heels, knees, wrists, fingers, and elbows. Early attacks usually improve within days, though over time they become longer and more frequent.

## Who is at Risk?

Risk factors include:
- Genetics: many people with gout have a family history of the disease
- Demographics: more common in men than women; more common in adults than children
- Weight: being overweight increases risk due to excess uric acid production
- Alcohol: drinking too much alcohol can lead to a buildup of uric acid because alcohol interferes with the removal of uric acid from the body
- Diet: high-purine foods like liver, dried beans, peas, and anchovies
- Lead exposure: environmental lead can trigger gout
- Medical conditions: kidney disease, high blood pressure, hypothyroidism, rapid cell turnover conditions
- Medications: diuretics, aspirin, niacin, cyclosporine, levodopa

## Treatments

For acute attacks, doctors typically prescribe high-dose NSAIDs or corticosteroids. Patients often begin to improve within a few hours of treatment, and the attack usually goes away completely within a week or so.

For recurring attacks, medications like allopurinol, febuxostat, or probenecid lower uric acid levels. In severe cases, pegloticase via intravenous infusion may be prescribed.
