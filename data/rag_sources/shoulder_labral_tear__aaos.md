# Shoulder Joint Tear (Glenoid Labrum Tear)

## Overview
Advances in medical technology have enabled doctors to identify and treat injuries previously undetected 30-40 years ago. Orthopedic surgeons now use arthroscopic surgery with miniaturized instruments and cameras to treat a shoulder injury called a glenoid labrum tear, or labral tear.

## Anatomy
The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint comprised of three bones:
- The humerus (upper arm bone)
- The scapula (shoulder blade)
- The clavicle (collarbone)

The humeral head rests in a shallow socket called the glenoid. A soft fibrous tissue rim called the labrum surrounds the socket to stabilize the joint. The rim deepens the socket by up to 50% so that the head of the upper arm bone fits better, and it serves as an attachment site for several ligaments.

## Description
Several types of labral tears exist:
- **SLAP lesion** — A tear of the labrum usually occurring on the upper part of the socket, potentially involving the origin of the long head of the biceps tendon.
- **Bankart lesion** — A tear of the front part of the labrum at the bottom of the socket, typically from anterior shoulder dislocation.
- **Posterior labral tear** — A tear in the back part of the socket, either traumatic or degenerative.

## Cause
Injuries occur from acute trauma or repetitive shoulder motion. Traumatic causes include:
- Falling on an outstretched arm
- Direct blow to the shoulder
- Sudden pulling motion (heavy lifting)
- Violent overhead reaching

Throwing athletes or weightlifters can experience glenoid labrum tears as a result of repetitive shoulder motion.

## Symptoms
Symptoms mirror other shoulder injuries:
- A sense of instability in the shoulder
- Shoulder dislocations
- Pain with overhead activities
- Catching, locking, popping, or grinding
- Occasional night pain or pain with daily activities
- Decreased range of motion
- Loss of strength

## Doctor Examination
Your doctor will:
- Take a history of your injury
- Perform physical tests checking range of motion, stability, and pain
- Request X-rays to eliminate other causes

Since the labrum is soft tissue, X-rays will not show damage to it. Your doctor may order CT or MRI scans with contrast medium, though arthroscopic surgery ultimately confirms the diagnosis.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Until final diagnosis occurs, your physician may prescribe anti-inflammatory medication and rest to relieve symptoms. Rehabilitation exercises to strengthen rotator cuff muscles may be recommended. In many cases, nonsurgical methods are effective in relieving symptoms and healing the injured structures.

### Surgical Treatment
If nonsurgical measures fail or symptoms return, surgery may be recommended. Procedures include traditional open surgery or arthroscopic approaches using small incisions and miniature instruments.

During arthroscopic surgery, the surgeon examines the rim and biceps tendon:
- If injury is confined to the rim without tendon involvement, the surgeon removes the torn flap and corrects associated problems.
- If the tear extends into the biceps tendon or if it's detached, the surgeon repairs or transfers the tendon using absorbable tacks, screws, or sutures. Studies have shown that the various techniques produce the same outcomes; no one technique is better than the others.
- For tears in the bottom half of the socket associated with instability, the surgeon reattaches the labrum and ligaments and tightens the socket tissue by pleating it. This may be done arthroscopically or open.

It should be noted that not all tears in the shoulder can be made better with surgery.

## Rehabilitation
After surgery, you will need to keep your shoulder in a sling for 2 to 6 weeks, depending on your doctor's recommendation. Gentle, passive, pain-free range-of-motion exercises follow. After sling removal, motion, flexibility, and gradual strengthening exercises begin.

Athletes can usually begin doing sport-specific exercises 12 weeks after surgery, although it will be 4 to 6 months before the shoulder is fully healed.
