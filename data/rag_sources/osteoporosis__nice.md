# NICE Guideline NG259: Osteoporosis - Risk Assessment (Recommendations)

Published: 29 July 2026. This guideline replaces the fracture-risk-assessment content of the 2012 NICE guideline on osteoporosis (CG146) and covers assessing fragility fracture risk, bone density assessment, deciding whether pharmacological treatment is appropriate, and follow-up. Note: content sourced from the guideline's separate chapter pages — "Fragility fracture risk assessment" (sections 1.1-1.4), "Identifying vertebral fragility fractures" (section 1.5), "Deciding whether pharmacological treatment is appropriate" (section 1.7), and "Follow-up for people not having treatment" (sections 1.8-1.10).

## 1.1 Risk Factors for Fragility Fractures

"Assess fragility fracture risk in all people aged 50 and over and women who have experienced menopause with either of the following risk factors: a previous fragility fracture; current or frequent use of systemic glucocorticoids (for example, 5 mg or more prednisolone daily or equivalent for over 3 months, or intermittent use of higher doses)" unless this is a replacement dose for adrenal insufficiency.

Consider assessing fragility fracture risk in all men aged 75 and over, all women aged 65 and over, and all people aged 50-74 (and women under 65 who have experienced menopause) with any of: 2 or more falls in the last year, a first-degree relative with hip fracture (particularly under age 80), low BMI (less than 18.5 kg/m2), current smoking, alcohol intake of more than 14 units per week, or other factors associated with increased fracture risk (for example rheumatoid arthritis, other inflammatory arthropathies such as spondyloarthritis, endocrine conditions, malabsorption conditions, chronic kidney disease stage 4-5, prolonged immobility, or medicines such as anticonvulsants, SSRIs, or proton pump inhibitors).

For men and women under 50 who have not experienced menopause, assess fragility fracture risk only if they have had a previous hip or vertebral fragility fracture, or 2 or more major osteoporotic fractures; consider assessment for a previous non-hip non-vertebral fracture, current/frequent glucocorticoid use, or untreated early menopause/premature ovarian insufficiency.

## 1.3 Risk Prediction Tools

"Complete a full clinical risk assessment alongside a risk prediction tool to determine the 10-year risk of major osteoporotic fracture using either FRAX or QFracture for people aged between 40 and 90," or QFracture for people aged 30-39. Use the same risk prediction tool throughout a person's care, since FRAX and QFracture assess risk differently. Take into account that fragility fracture risk is decreased while taking systemic hormone replacement therapy (HRT). Seek advice from a specialist when assessing fragility fracture risk in people aged under 30, or with rare forms of osteoporosis or bone disease (for example suspected idiopathic osteoporosis, pregnancy-associated osteoporosis, osteogenesis imperfecta, osteomalacia or hypophosphatasia).

## 1.4 Bone Density Assessment

"Offer a DXA scan to measure BMD (with or without completing a risk prediction tool) when assessing fragility fracture risk in people aged 30 and over who have had: a previous hip or vertebral fragility fracture; a single major osteoporotic fragility fracture in the last 2 years; or 2 or more fragility fractures." Consider BMD measurement with a DXA scan to help guide treatment decisions for people with a 10-year risk of major osteoporotic fracture of 10% or more. Do a baseline BMD measurement with a DXA scan when starting treatment, unless not tolerated, not technically feasible, or not needed for treatment/monitoring decisions. If there is likely to be a significant delay before a DXA scan, consider fast-tracking within 6 weeks for people likely to need anabolic treatment, or starting antiresorptive treatment while waiting if anabolic treatment is unlikely to be needed.

## 1.5 DXA-Based Vertebral Fracture Assessment (VFA)

Consider doing a VFA when doing a DXA scan for men aged 70 and over, women aged 60 and over, or younger people with a previous major osteoporotic fracture, signs/symptoms of vertebral fracture (for example unexplained back or radiating rib pain, height loss over 4cm, or spinal deformity), suspicion of vertebral fracture from the DXA scan, current/frequent glucocorticoid use, or exceptionally low BMD for age (Z-score less than -2).

## 1.7 Criteria for Pharmacological Treatment

"Make a shared decision with the person about whether to start pharmacological treatment based on the following: 10-year risk of major osteoporotic fracture obtained from FRAX or QFracture (if available); BMD from a DXA scan (if available); the number, timing and skeletal sites of previous fragility fractures (especially hip or vertebral fractures); a clinical assessment of risk factors."

Consider pharmacological treatment for all people aged 50 and over and women who have experienced menopause if they have any of: a previous hip or vertebral fragility fracture; very high-dose systemic glucocorticoids (for example 15 mg or more prednisolone daily or equivalent) for 3 months or more; a BMD T-score of -2.5 or less; or a BMD T-score of -1.5 or less combined with a previous fragility fracture, current/frequent glucocorticoid use, or medicines/factors associated with accelerated bone loss (for example aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation therapy, or untreated primary hyperparathyroidism). The guideline notes that "the World Health Organization use BMD T-score to define osteoporosis and osteopenia (T-score of -2.5 or less for osteoporosis, and between -2.5 and -1 for osteopenia)," while acknowledging there is no single BMD threshold below which treatment becomes effective.

If a DXA scan is not tolerated, not technically feasible, or not needed to inform treatment or monitoring decisions (for example for older people living with frailty), make a shared decision about treatment based on the other listed factors without a DXA scan. Seek specialist advice on treatment options for younger people with fractures or idiopathic osteoporosis, for suspected rare forms of osteoporosis, or for other rare bone diseases associated with fragility fractures.

## 1.8-1.9 Follow-Up for People Not Having Treatment

Advise people who have declined or delayed treatment when and how to re-access the service and to contact their GP if their circumstances change. For people with a 10-year risk of major osteoporotic fracture below 10%, consider reassessing risk using the same tool used at baseline when there is a change in clinical circumstances, or at 5 years if there is no change. For people with a 10-year risk of 10% or more, or who had a DXA scan but whose condition did not meet treatment criteria, consider reassessing BMD with a DXA scan within 2 to 3 years if their condition was close to meeting treatment criteria (especially with risk of accelerated bone loss), or at 5 years otherwise.

## 1.10 Timing of Follow-Up DXA Scans

"Do not routinely repeat a DXA scan within 2 years of the person's most recent DXA scan unless there are exceptional circumstances" (for example a high risk of accelerated bone loss from medicines such as high-dose systemic glucocorticoids), because this time period is generally too short to show a clinically meaningful change in BMD.
