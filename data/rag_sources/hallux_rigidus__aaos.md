# Hallux Rigidus (Stiff Big Toe)

## Overview
Hallux rigidus is a condition in which the joint at the base of the big toe becomes progressively stiff. It is the most common arthritic condition of the foot and can significantly limit comfortable walking. It typically develops in middle-aged adults, most often between ages 30 and 60.

## Anatomy
The big toe has two joints. The metatarsophalangeal joint (MTP) is the larger of the two, formed where the first metatarsal bone (the long bone of the foot) meets the first phalanx (the toe bone). This joint allows the toe to bend and grip the ground while accommodating the flexibility the foot needs during walking.

Beneath the MTP joint sit two small bones called sesamoid bones. These bones help create a stable pivot point that supports the push-off motion during walking.

## Why Hallux Rigidus Develops
Normally, the ends of bones in a joint are covered by a smooth layer of articular cartilage. When this cartilage is damaged, the underlying bone surfaces can start to rub against one another. In response, bony growths (spurs) may form on the top of the base of the big toe. These spurs can limit how far the toe can bend — often not enough for normal walking — which causes pain.

As the condition advances to later stages, the cartilage wears away completely, resulting in bone-on-bone contact and arthritis within the joint.

## Cause
Several factors contribute to the development of hallux rigidus:

- Ordinary wear and tear, or repeated injuries to the big toe, that gradually damage the protective cartilage over time
- Biomechanical foot problems, such as flatfoot or bunions, which place excess stress on the MTP joint and can trigger hallux rigidus
- An inherited predisposition that makes some people more likely to develop the condition

## Symptoms
Symptoms of hallux rigidus vary and may include one or several of the following:

- Pain in the big toe joint during walking or other activities; this is often felt on top of the joint but can also feel like a deeper pain within the joint; many people notice difficulty pushing off normally while walking, or pain spread across the top of the foot
- Swelling and thickening of tissue around the joint, which may require changes in shoe size or style
- A bump on top of the foot resembling a bunion or callus; this may be accompanied by redness
- Reduced ability to bend or straighten the big toe through its normal range

## Diagnosing Hallux Rigidus

### Physical Examination
The doctor examines the foot for visible bone spurs and moves the big toe in different directions to measure how much motion is available and to see where pain occurs. This movement testing helps determine whether the pain is coming from deep within the joint or from the bump on top, which guides treatment recommendations.

### Imaging Tests
**X-rays** show the location and size of any bone spurs, the extent of arthritic damage within the joint, and how much cartilage has been lost.

**CT scan.** Doctors may selectively order a CT scan to evaluate the alignment of the foot's structure.

**MRI scan.** An MRI may be ordered to get a better look at the joint cartilage, fluid within the bone, and the ligaments around the joint. It can also help with surgical planning in certain cases.

### Importance of Early Diagnosis
Recognizing hallux rigidus early makes treatment simpler. If diagnosis is delayed, bone spurs can grow, arthritis can set in, and the joint can deteriorate further, potentially requiring more complex treatment.

## Treatment of a Stiff Big Toe

### Nonsurgical Treatment

**Medications.** Doctors often recommend pain relievers, particularly oral or topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), to reduce pain and control swelling.

**Ice.** Applying an ice pack to the affected area can temporarily reduce inflammation and manage symptoms. Ice should never touch skin directly; use an ice pack or wrap it in a cloth or towel to prevent skin injury.

**Shoe changes.** Choosing shoes with a roomy toe box reduces pressure on top of the toe. High heels should be avoided. Doctors often recommend shoes with stiff soles and a rocker- or roller-shaped bottom. Many patients get relief from regular shoes fitted with stiff carbon-fiber inserts (Morton's extension inserts) or metal plates built into the sole. These modifications support the foot during walking and limit how far the big toe bends, reducing pain and inflammation.

**Injections.** Corticosteroid injections into the MTP joint can serve two purposes:
- They can sometimes help confirm the diagnosis of hallux rigidus
- They can provide pain relief for patients who have tried other nonsurgical options without success but are not good candidates for surgery
- These injections do not treat the underlying problem and carry some risk, though they may provide several months of pain relief

### Surgical Treatment

**Cheilectomy.** This is the typical recommendation for patients with mild to moderate hallux rigidus. The procedure involves removing bone spurs and shaving away a portion of bone from the big toe to create more room for it to bend.

The procedure can be done through either a traditional open incision or minimally invasive techniques, both of which produce good results. Minimally invasive approaches use smaller incisions, which may allow earlier weight-bearing and a faster initial recovery. Open approaches give the surgeon a complete direct view of the joint throughout the procedure.

**Arthrodesis.** Permanently fusing the bones is typically recommended when cartilage damage is severe.

In arthrodesis, the surgeon removes the damaged cartilage and uses pins, screws, or metal plates to hold the joint permanently in place. This allows comfortable walking and may allow low-heeled shoes to be worn. Over time the bones fuse together, eliminating the toe joint entirely. Because the toe loses all bending ability after fusion, this procedure is reserved for more severe cases, but it is more reliable at reducing pain in patients with severe hallux rigidus or when cheilectomy has failed.

**Resection arthroplasty.** Surgeons may choose to remove part of the joint surface in elderly patients with low functional demands. This procedure is used less often in more active patients.

**Arthroplasty (total joint replacement).** Older patients with modest activity demands may be candidates for joint replacement. The surgeon removes the surfaces of the MTP joint and implants an artificial joint, which may relieve pain while preserving some motion.

**Hemiarthroplasty (partial joint replacement).** This procedure replaces only one side of the joint and may benefit patients who still have good cartilage on the other side. It is used less often today than total joint replacement or fusion.

**Note on arthroplasty options.** Arthroplasty and hemiarthroplasty are used less often because of the likelihood of needing revision surgery if the implant loosens. Removing the implant during a later procedure risks significant bone loss and may lead to functional problems.

## Surgical Recovery
**Pain management.** Several types of medication help manage pain after surgery, including opioids, NSAIDs, and local anesthetics. Using the appropriate pain medication improves comfort during recovery.

Opioids can relieve pain but carry a risk of addiction and other dangers, so they should be used only as directed by a physician, typically for a short period at the lowest effective dose. Patients whose pain has not improved substantially several days after surgery should contact their doctor.

**Recovery after cheilectomy.** Most patients experience long-lasting pain relief. However, moving the toe soon after surgery is important to prevent scar tissue from making the toe stiff again. The surgical site and toe may remain swollen for several months, and patients typically wear a wooden-soled sandal for at least two weeks before transitioning to regular shoes with enough room in the toe box to accommodate ongoing swelling.

**Recovery after arthrodesis.** Most patients wear a splint or cast for about the first six weeks after surgery. After the splint or cast is removed, patients use crutches while wearing a boot, stiff-soled shoe, or rocker-soled shoe. Swelling may last three to six months after surgery.
