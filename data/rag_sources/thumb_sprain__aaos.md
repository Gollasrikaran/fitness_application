# Sprained Thumb

## Overview
A sprained thumb occurs when the ligaments supporting the thumb stretch beyond their limits, typically from a strong force bending the thumb backward away from the palm. The most common cause is falling onto an outstretched hand.

Most thumb sprains involve the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL), located on the inside of the joint where the thumb meets the palm. An injury to this ligament can be painful and may make the thumb feel loose or unstable, potentially weakening the ability to pinch or grasp objects between the thumb and index finger.

Treatment usually involves wearing a splint or cast to keep the thumb immobilized while the ligament heals. For more severe sprains or ligament tears, surgery may be needed to restore joint stability.

## Description
Ligaments are strong, fibrous tissues that typically connect bones to other bones. The ligaments in the thumb help keep the bones in proper position while allowing for joint motion.

**Sprain Types:**
- Sprains occur when ligaments stretch beyond their limit, potentially causing small, incomplete tears in the fibers. The ligament remains intact. These are known as partial-thickness tears.
- Ligaments can also be torn completely — either in the middle of the ligament or at the end(s), where it can pull off the bone(s). These are known as full-thickness tears.

### Grades of Thumb Sprains
Sprains are graded based on the degree of ligament injury:

- **Grade 1 sprain (mild).** The ligament is stretched, but not torn.
- **Grade 2 sprain (moderate).** The ligament is partially torn. This type of injury may involve some loss of function.
- **Grade 3 sprain (severe).** The ligament is completely torn in the middle or is pulled off its attachment to the bone. These are significant injuries that require medical or surgical care. If the ligament tears away from the bone, it may take a small chip of the bone with it. This is called an avulsion fracture.

### Injury to the Ulnar Collateral Ligament of the Thumb
The ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) of the thumb is a strong band of tissue running along the inside of the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, which is where the thumb meets the palm. This ligament keeps the thumb stable, especially when pinching and grasping things.

A thumb UCL injury is commonly called "skier's thumb," because falling on the ski slopes with the hand strapped to a ski pole is a common cause of this injury.

The ligament can also be injured more gradually, over time, from repetitive grasping or twisting activities. This type of chronic injury is known as "gamekeeper's thumb" because originally, it was most commonly a work-related injury seen among Scottish gamekeepers (workers who manage wildlife and areas of land used for hunting).

The ulnar collateral ligament can tear in different ways. For example, it may be:

- Pulled off its attachment at the base of the first bone (the proximal phalanx) in the thumb
- Pulled off the metacarpal bone, where it originates
- Torn through its middle, though this is less common

## Cause
Any strong force that bends the thumb backward and away from the palm of the hand can stretch or tear the ulnar collateral ligament. This most often occurs by falling onto an outstretched hand.

Sprained thumbs are also common in skiers and in athletes who participate in sports that involve catching and throwing a ball, such as football, baseball, softball, and basketball.

## Symptoms
- Depending on the severity of the sprain, pain may or may not occur at the time of the injury.
- Bruising, tenderness, and swelling around the base of the thumb, near the palm, may develop.
- If the ulnar collateral ligament is completely torn, the end of the ruptured ligament may cause a lump or swelling on the inside of the thumb. The thumb joint may also feel loose or unstable, with difficulty grasping items between the thumb and index finger.

It is important for a doctor to evaluate even a mild thumb sprain if it does not improve quickly. Proper and prompt diagnosis and treatment of a thumb injury is necessary to avoid long-term complications, including chronic pain, instability, and arthritis.

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
The doctor will:

- Ask how and when the injury occurred
- Ask the patient to describe symptoms
- Carefully examine the thumb and hand. To help determine whether the ulnar collateral ligament is partially or completely torn, the doctor will move the thumb in different positions to test the stability of the MCP joint. If the joint is loose and unstable, it is an indication that the ligament may be completely torn.

### Imaging Tests
**X-rays.** X-rays provide images of dense structures, such as bone. X-rays of the thumb and hand may be ordered to ensure there is no avulsion fracture or any broken bones. An X-ray of the uninjured thumb may also be taken to compare it to the injured thumb.

A special type of X-ray, called a stress X-ray, may also be ordered. During this test, the doctor applies tension to the thumb while it is being X-rayed to learn more about the stability of the MCP joint. If the test causes pain, an injection of a local anesthetic may be given.

**Other imaging tests.** If more information is needed, a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan or an ultrasound may be ordered. These tests can help determine the severity of the injury and inform decisions about treatment and return to activity.

## Treatment
Treatment for a sprained thumb depends on the severity of the injury.

### Home Care
Mild thumb sprains will usually improve with home treatment that includes the RICE protocol:

- **Rest.** Try not to use the hand for at least 48 hours.
- **Ice.** Apply ice immediately after the injury to keep the swelling down. Use cold packs for 20 minutes at a time, several times a day. Do not apply ice directly on the skin.
- **Compression.** Wear an elastic compression bandage to reduce swelling. Do not make the bandage too tight, as this could cut off blood flow to the thumb.
- **Elevation.** As often as possible, rest with the hand raised up higher than the heart.

Additionally, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as aspirin or ibuprofen, can help reduce pain and swelling. If, however, pain and swelling continue for more than 48 hours, see a doctor.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
For a moderate sprain, the doctor will probably immobilize the thumb joint with a bandage, thumb spica cast, or splint until it heals. To ease pain and swelling, a cold pack can be applied to the thumb twice a day for 2 to 3 days after the injury. Do not apply ice directly on the skin.

Depending on the severity of the injury, the splint or cast may need to be worn at all times. It is important to avoid applying any pressure or resistance to the thumb until the doctor says it is OK to do so.

Because immobilization may cause some stiffness in the thumb joint, stretching exercises may be recommended to help regain full range of motion.

### Surgical Treatment
For a severe sprain or tear, surgery may be needed to restore the stability of the thumb joint and help regain function. Surgery involves reconnecting the ligament to the bone and/or repairing the avulsion fracture using a pin, screw, or special bone anchor.

After surgery, a short arm cast or a splint may need to be worn for 6 to 12 weeks to protect the thumb ligament while it heals.

## Outcomes
When diagnosed and treated properly, most thumb sprains will heal well with no complications. However, ignoring a sprained thumb with the hope that it will heal on its own may lead to long-term problems, including:

- Chronic instability
- Weakness
- Arthritis

If these late complications develop, surgery may be needed to rebuild the ligament using tissue from the upper arm. If there is significant arthritis, a joint fusion procedure may be needed to address both the arthritis and the instability of the MCP joint.
