# Shoulder Impingement/Rotator Cuff Tendinitis

## Anatomy
The shoulder comprises three bones: the humerus (upper arm bone), scapula (shoulder blade), and clavicle (collarbone). The rotator cuff tendons attach to the top of the humerus (humeral head) and connect the humerus to the shoulder blade's socket to allow you to move your shoulder and arm.

A lubricating sac called a bursa sits between the rotator cuff and the acromion (bony structure on top of the shoulder blade), enabling smooth movement.

## Description
Rotator cuff pain stems from three primary causes:
- **Tendinitis** — inflammation of the rotator cuff tendons
- **Bursitis** — inflammation of the bursa
- **Impingement** — the space between the top of your shoulder (acromion) and the rotator cuff tendons becomes smaller when you raise your arm

## Cause
High-risk groups include young overhead athletes (swimming, baseball, tennis, volleyball) and workers performing repetitive lifting or overhead tasks (painting, construction). Pain may also develop from minor injuries without obvious causes.

## Symptoms
Early symptoms include mild pain at rest and with use, radiating from the front shoulder to the arm side, and sudden pain during lifting. Athletes may experience pain when throwing or serving.

Progressive symptoms involve severe nighttime pain affecting sleep, strength loss, and functional difficulty with dressing, seatbelts, and personal hygiene. The pain may come on suddenly and the shoulder may be severely tender to the touch.

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
The physician evaluates tenderness through palpation, assesses motion through directional arm movements, tests strength, and checks for other shoulder pathology. They may also examine your neck to make sure that the pain is not caused by a pinched nerve.

### Imaging Tests
**X-rays** typically appear normal but may reveal bone spurs. A special X-ray view, called an outlet view, sometimes will show a small bone spur on the front edge of the acromion.

**MRI and ultrasound** provide superior soft tissue imaging, revealing bursal fluid, inflammation, and potential rotator cuff tears.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
- **Rest** involves activity modification, avoiding overhead movements that trigger symptoms.
- **NSAIDs** reduce pain and swelling through anti-inflammatory action.
- **Physical therapy** emphasizes motion restoration through stretching, progressing to rotator cuff strengthening and posture correction.
- **Steroid injection** offers pain relief when conservative approaches prove insufficient. The steroid is usually injected into the bursa beneath the acromion.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery aims to create additional rotator cuff space through:
- **Bursectomy** — removing inflamed bursal tissue
- **Acromioplasty** — removing bone spurs (subacromial decompression)

**Arthroscopic technique** uses small instruments through minimal incisions. The doctor examines your shoulder through a fiberoptic scope connected to a television camera.

Surgeons may address concurrent conditions including acromioclavicular arthritis, biceps tendinitis, and rotator cuff tears.

**Rehabilitation** typically requires sling immobilization initially, followed by progressive exercises. It typically takes 2 to 4 months to achieve complete relief of pain, but it may take up to 1 year.
