# Biceps Tendinitis

## Overview
Biceps tendinitis involves inflammation or irritation of the upper portion of the biceps tendon, also called the long head of the biceps tendon. The biceps tendon connects the biceps muscle to the shoulder socket. Common symptoms include pain in the front of the shoulder and weakness with movement, which can often be relieved through rest and medication, though severe cases may require surgery.

## Anatomy
The shoulder comprises three bones: the humerus (upper arm bone), scapula (shoulder blade), and clavicle (collarbone).

**Glenoid:** The head of the humerus fits into the rounded socket in the scapula. This socket is called the glenoid.

**Rotator cuff:** A combination of muscles and tendons keep the arm centered in the shoulder socket. These tissues, called the rotator cuff, cover the head of the upper arm bone and attach it to the shoulder blade.

**Biceps tendons:** The biceps muscle is in the front of the upper arm with two tendons:
- The long head attaches to the top of the glenoid
- The short head attaches to a bump on the front of the shoulder blade called the coracoid process

## Description
Biceps tendinitis is inflammation of the long head of the biceps tendon. The condition progresses through stages:
- Early stage: the tendon becomes inflamed and swollen
- Progressive stage: the tendon and its sheath (covering) can become more irritated, which causes it to thicken
- Late stage: the damage to the tendon can result in fraying or a complete tear. A complete tendon tear results in a cosmetic deformity of the arm (a "Popeye" bulge in the upper arm)

Biceps tendinitis usually occurs with other shoulder problems, including:
- Rotator cuff degeneration and/or tearing
- Arthritis of the shoulder joint
- Tears in the glenoid labrum
- Chronic shoulder instability
- Other diseases causing shoulder joint inflammation

## Cause
In most cases, damage to the biceps tendon is due to a lifetime of normal activities. As we age, everyday wear and tear causes our tendons to slowly weaken. This degeneration can be worsened by overuse — repeating the same shoulder motions again and again.

Many jobs and routine chores can cause overuse damage. Sports requiring repetitive overhead motion — including swimming, volleyball, pickleball, tennis, and baseball — increase risk for biceps tendinitis.

## Symptoms
- Pain or tenderness in the front of the shoulder, which worsens with overhead lifting or activity
- Pain with reaching backward, such as putting on a seatbelt or coat
- Pain or achiness that moves down the upper arm bone
- An occasional snapping sound or sensation in the shoulder

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
After discussing symptoms and medical history, the doctor examines the shoulder by:
- Assessing shoulder range of motion, strength, and signs of shoulder instability
- Possibly palpating the front of the shoulder directly over the biceps tendon
- Performing specific physical examination tests to check biceps function

Your doctor may press over the area where the biceps tendon attaches to the shoulder, as patients with tendinitis will have tenderness and possible swelling in this area.

### Imaging Tests
**X-rays:** Although they visualize only bones, X-rays may show other shoulder joint problems.

**Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound:** These imaging techniques can show soft tissues like the biceps tendon in greater detail than X-rays. These tests may show how irritated the tendon is, how much inflammation is present, and whether the tendon is torn.

## Treatment
Your orthopedic surgeon will work carefully to identify any other problems in your shoulder and treat them along with your tendinitis.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Biceps tendinitis is typically first treated with nonsurgical methods. This type of nonsurgical treatment is very effective in most patients.
- **Rest:** The first step toward recovery is to avoid activities that cause pain.
- **Ice:** Apply cold packs for 20 minutes at a time, several times a day, to keep swelling down. Do not apply ice directly to the skin.
- **Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs):** Anti-inflammatory drugs like ibuprofen, aspirin, and naproxen can reduce pain and swelling.
- **Steroid injections:** Steroids such as cortisone are very effective anti-inflammatory medicines. Injecting steroids into the tendon can relieve pain. Given the location of the tendon within its sheath, these injections are often done under image guidance, such as with an ultrasound.
- **Physical therapy:** Specific stretching and strengthening exercises can help restore range of motion and strengthen your shoulder.

### Surgical Treatment
If your condition does not improve with nonsurgical treatment, or improved but then comes back, your doctor may offer surgery as an option. Surgery may also be an option if you have other associated shoulder problems.

Surgery is usually performed arthroscopically. This allows the doctor to assess the condition of the biceps tendon as well as other structures in the shoulder, using a small camera (arthroscope) inserted into the shoulder joint and miniature surgical instruments guided by the monitor images.

- **Repair:** Rarely, the biceps tendon can be repaired where it attaches to the shoulder socket (glenoid). This is the least common treatment method and is usually reserved for young, very active, high-demand patients.
- **Biceps tenodesis:** In some cases, the damaged section of the biceps is removed, and the surgeon reattaches the remaining tendon to the humerus (upper arm bone). Removing the painful part of the biceps usually resolves symptoms and restores normal function. The procedure may be performed arthroscopically or through a small open incision in the front of the shoulder.
- **Tenotomy:** In some cases, the long head of the biceps tendon may be so damaged that it is not possible to repair or tenodese it. The surgeon may simply elect to release the damaged biceps tendon from its attachment to bone. This option is the least invasive but may result in a Popeye bulge in the arm. Patients who have tenotomy usually do well and return to near normal strength. This surgery also has the shortest recovery time.

**Surgical complications:** Overall, complication rates are low. Possible complications include infection, stiffness, and — if tenodesis was performed — possible rupture and/or pain where reattached, or — if tenotomy was performed — possible cramping in the muscle.

**Rehabilitation:** After surgery, the doctor will prescribe a rehabilitation plan based on the procedures performed. Patients may wear a sling for a few weeks. Therapeutic exercises typically start a few weeks after surgery; a physical therapy referral may be given but is not always necessary. Flexibility exercises improve shoulder range of motion, and strengthening exercises are gradually added to the rehabilitation plan.

**Surgical outcome:** Most patients have good results. They typically regain full range of motion and are able to move their arms without pain. People who play very high-demand overhead sports occasionally need to limit these activities after surgery.
