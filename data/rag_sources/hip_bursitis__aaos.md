# Hip Bursitis

## Overview
Bursae are small, fluid-filled sacs located throughout the body between bones and soft tissues, functioning as cushions to reduce friction during joint motion. Hip bursitis involves inflammation of these structures.

## Types of Hip Bursae
Two major bursae in the hip commonly become irritated:
- **Trochanteric bursa**: Covers the bony point of the hip bone (greater trochanter); inflammation is called trochanteric bursitis.
- **Iliopsoas bursa**: Located on the inside/groin side of the hip; when inflamed, causes groin area pain and is less common than trochanteric bursitis.

## Symptoms
The primary symptom is pain at the hip point, typically extending to the outer thigh. Early pain is usually sharp and intense, later becoming an ache across a larger area. Pain worsens at night, when lying on the affected hip, after prolonged sitting, and during prolonged walking, stair climbing, or squatting.

## Risk Factors
Hip bursitis is more common in women and middle-aged or elderly people. Associated risk factors include:
- Repetitive stress injuries from running, stair climbing, bicycling, or prolonged standing
- Hip injuries from falls or extended pressure on one side
- Spine diseases including scoliosis and lumbar arthritis
- Tight iliotibial band
- Leg-length inequality
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Previous hip surgery or prosthetic implants
- Bone spurs or calcium deposits in tendons

## Doctor Examination
Diagnosis involves comprehensive physical examination checking for tenderness over the hip bone point. Additional testing may include X-rays, bone scans, or MRI to rule out other injuries or conditions.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial treatment focuses on lifestyle modifications:
- **Activity modification**: Avoid symptom-worsening activities
- **NSAIDs**: Ibuprofen, naproxen, piroxicam, and celecoxib may relieve pain and control inflammation
- **Assistive devices**: Walking cane or crutches for one week or longer if needed
- **Physical therapy**: Stretching exercises for the IT band, hip strengthening, flexibility work, massage, ice, heat, or ultrasound
- **Steroid injection**: Corticosteroid injection into the bursa provides temporary or permanent relief; repeated injections after several months may help if symptoms return

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is rarely necessary. When nonsurgical treatments fail to provide relief, the bursa may be surgically removed. The hip functions normally without the bursa.

Endoscopic removal is a newer technique using a small 1/4-inch incision and camera guidance to remove the bursa through a minimally invasive approach. Both surgical methods are outpatient procedures.

### Rehabilitation
Post-surgery recovery is brief. Patients typically walk the evening after surgery, with a cane or crutches helpful for a few days. Soreness usually resolves within days.

## Prevention
While hip bursitis cannot always be prevented, these measures help reduce inflammation severity:
- Avoid repetitive hip-stress activities
- Maintain healthy weight
- Use properly fitting shoe inserts for leg-length differences
- Maintain hip muscle strength and flexibility
