# Arthritis of the Wrist

## Overview
Your wrist comprises multiple smaller joints functioning together to enable movement. Healthy wrists have smooth cartilage protecting bone surfaces during motion. Arthritis damages this protective cartilage, causing bones to rub together and creating irreparable joint damage.

Several arthritis types affect the wrist:

- **Osteoarthritis**: Degenerative wear-and-tear arthritis occurring with age
- **Posttraumatic arthritis**: Results from joint injuries like broken bones or torn ligaments
- **Inflammatory arthritis**: Occurs in autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, where the immune system attacks joints

Pain and stiffness characterize arthritis symptoms, complicating daily activities. Most arthritis-related diseases are chronic and long-lasting, potentially causing serious joint damage. While no cure exists, many treatment options help relieve symptoms and slow progression. With proper treatment, many people are able to manage their symptoms and stay active.

## Anatomy
The wrist connects the hand to the forearm, formed by two forearm bones (radius and ulna) and eight small carpal bones arranged in two rows at the hand's base, with four bones per row.

Each bone's joint surface is covered with articular cartilage — a smooth and slick substance that protects and cushions the bones as you move your hand and wrist.

## Description

### Osteoarthritis
Osteoarthritis develops from normal wear-and-tear, particularly in older people and those with family histories of arthritis. It commonly appears after middle age but can occur in younger individuals.

The smooth articular cartilage gradually wears away over time. Because cartilage lacks substantial blood supply, it is unable to heal or regenerate when it becomes injured or worn down.

As cartilage deteriorates, it becomes frayed and rough, reducing protective space between bones. This results in bone-on-bone contact, causing pain and stiffness.

Osteoarthritis can also develop from Kienböck's disease, where disrupted blood supply to the lunate carpal bone causes it to die and collapse, eventually leading to arthritis in surrounding joints.

### Posttraumatic Arthritis
Posttraumatic arthritis develops after injuries like broken wrist bones (scaphoid or distal radius fractures) or torn ligaments. Trauma directly damages cartilage or causes delayed cartilage wear due to altered bone movement patterns.

This arthritis can develop over many years following initial injury. Even with proper treatment, injured joints are more likely to become arthritic over time.

### Inflammatory (or Rheumatoid) Arthritis
Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic disease affecting multiple body joints, often starting in smaller joints like those in hands and wrists. It typically affects the same joints symmetrically on both body sides.

This autoimmune disease means the body's immune system attacks its own tissues. In inflammatory arthritis, the defenses that normally protect the body from infection instead damage normal tissue (such as cartilage and ligaments) and can soften bone.

Inflammatory arthritis frequently affects the radius-ulna joint, causing the ulna to become prominent. This can lead to tendon wear and rupture that straightens fingers, causing additional deformity and functional loss.

The exact cause remains unknown with no clear genetic or environmental factors. However, researchers believe some people have genes making them more susceptible to the disease, though it is not directly inherited.

## Symptoms
Not all arthritis patients experience symptoms, and severity varies greatly among those who do. For some, symptoms fluctuate depending on activity levels and other factors.

Arthritis symptoms may include:

- Pain
- Swelling
- Reduced range of motion or stiffness
- Weakness in the joint

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
Your doctor discusses your overall health and medical history, asking you to describe symptoms. They perform careful hand and wrist examinations, looking for:

- Reduced range of motion
- Areas of pain or tenderness
- Joint instability
- Swelling or appearance changes

During examination, your doctor may evaluate:

- Finger and thumb mobility to determine tendon and joint function
- Nerve function to identify other conditions affecting your wrist, such as carpal tunnel syndrome

### Tests
**X-rays**: X-rays provide detailed images of dense structures, such as bone. Wrist X-rays help doctors determine arthritis location and severity while distinguishing between arthritis types.

**Blood tests**: Your doctor may recommend blood tests to determine arthritis type. With rheumatoid and inflammatory arthritis, blood tests are important for accurate diagnosis. Osteoarthritis shows no blood test abnormalities.

## Treatment
No arthritis cure exists, but several treatments may reduce symptom frequency and relieve pain and functional loss.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial arthritis treatment is typically nonsurgical, designed to minimize symptoms.

- **Activity modification**: Limiting or stopping pain-causing activities is the first symptom relief step.
- **Immobilization**: Wearing a wrist splint for a short time will help support the joint and ease the stress placed on it by frequent use and activities.
- **Medications**: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like aspirin, naproxen, and ibuprofen reduce pain and swelling. Topical NSAIDs apply directly to skin around the joint.
- **Exercise**: Specific exercises improve wrist range of motion and function. Your doctor or physical therapist can develop personalized programs.
- **Steroid injection**: Cortisone is a powerful anti-inflammatory agent injected into arthritic joints. Although cortisone injections provide relief and reduce inflammation, the effects may be temporary.
- **Other therapies**: These include "contrast soaks" — alternating warm and cold water soaks — to reduce swelling.

For inadequately controlled inflammatory arthritis symptoms, doctors may prescribe disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), designed to stop immune system joint attacks. DMARDs have risks and benefits, with rheumatologists directing their use.

### Surgical Treatment
If nonsurgical treatment fails to relieve pain and arthritis significantly affects quality of life, surgery may be recommended.

Surgery aims to relieve pain while preserving or improving hand function by minimizing or eliminating bone-on-bone contact. Several wrist arthritis procedures exist, with doctors discussing which works best for individual cases.

**Proximal row carpectomy**: This procedure removes three carpal bones in the row closest to the forearm, designed to reduce pain while maintaining some wrist motion.

**Fusion**: If motion causes pain, doctors may recommend fusion — essentially a welding process in which the bones are fused together so they heal into a single, solid bone. Doctors remove damaged cartilage, then use pins, plates, or screws to hold the joint permanently. Over time, bones fuse similarly to how fractured bone ends heal.

Partial fusion can address damaged joint surfaces while preserving some wrist motion by fusing only some carpal bones.

For extensive arthritis, complete wrist fusion may be necessary, fusing all carpal bones with the radius. Although all wrist motion is eliminated in a complete fusion, forearm rotation and finger/thumb motion are generally preserved.

**Total wrist replacement (arthroplasty)**: Doctors remove damaged wrist cartilage and bone, replacing them with metal or plastic joint surfaces. This relieves the pain of arthritis while allowing more wrist movement than fusion. Like all joint replacements, total wrist replacements may require future revision.

## Living with Arthritis
Learning you have arthritis can be discouraging, but several strategies help minimize its impact:

- **Talk to your doctor**: If symptoms worsen or coping becomes difficult, ask your doctor to review and potentially modify your treatment plan.
- **Change your activities**: Cut down or eliminate activities aggravating arthritis to minimize symptoms.
- **Try alternative therapies**: Some alternative therapies like acupuncture appear helpful for arthritis pain. Always consult your doctor before trying alternatives, as they may interfere with overall treatment.

## Outcomes
While wrist arthritis cannot be cured, many patients experience significant relief with treatment.

Patients ultimately requiring surgery are often satisfied with procedures, especially regarding pain relief. In many cases, some wrist motion can be preserved after surgery.
