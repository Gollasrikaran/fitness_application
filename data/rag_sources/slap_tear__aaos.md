# SLAP Tears in the Shoulder

## Overview
A SLAP tear involves injury to the labrum, which is the ring of cartilage that surrounds the socket of the shoulder joint.

## Anatomy
The shoulder comprises three bones: the humerus (upper arm bone), scapula (shoulder blade), and clavicle (collarbone). The labrum is a rim of strong, fibrous tissue that:
- Helps deepen the socket and stabilize the shoulder joint
- Serves as an attachment point for shoulder ligaments and a biceps tendon

## Description
SLAP stands for "superior labrum anterior and posterior." The injury affects the top (superior) part of the labrum where the biceps tendon attaches, extending from the front (anterior) to the back (posterior) of this attachment point.

## Cause
Acute SLAP injuries result from trauma like falls, forceful pulling, rapid arm movements above shoulder level, or dislocations. Repetitive overhead athletes (throwers, weightlifters) experience tears from repeated motion. Additionally, tearing or fraying of the superior labrum can be seen as a normal process of aging in patients over 30-40 years old.

## Symptoms
Common symptoms include:
- Locking, popping, catching, or grinding sensations
- Pain with movement or specific positions
- Pain with lifting, especially overhead
- Decreased shoulder strength
- Feeling the shoulder might "pop out of joint"
- Reduced range of motion
- Decreased throw velocity or "dead arm" feeling (for pitchers)

## Doctor Examination
**Medical History:** Doctors discuss symptom onset, specific injuries or aggravating activities, pain location, and previous treatments.

**Physical Examination:** The physician checks range of motion, strength, and stability; performs specific positional tests to reproduce symptoms; and examines the neck to rule out nerve compression.

**Imaging Tests:**
- **X-rays:** Show bone clearly but not soft tissue like the labrum; used to exclude arthritis or fractures
- **MRI scans:** Better visualize soft tissue; an MRI arthrogram uses dye injection for clearer SLAP tear visualization

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Initial treatment typically avoids surgery:
- **NSAIDs:** Anti-inflammatory medications like ibuprofen and naproxen reduce pain and swelling
- **Corticosteroid injections:** May reduce inflammation and aid diagnosis
- **Physical therapy:** Includes flexibility exercises, range-of-motion work stretching the shoulder capsule, and strengthening exercises. Programs typically last 3 to 6 months with a qualified therapist.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery is recommended when nonsurgical methods fail.

**Arthroscopy:** The most common surgical approach, using a small camera, called an arthroscope, inserted into the shoulder joint. Small incisions allow surgeons to view and treat the injury with miniature instruments.

Treatment options vary by tear type:
- Removing torn labrum portions
- Reattaching torn sections with sutures
- Releasing the biceps tendon attachment to relieve symptoms

The surgeon determines the best approach based on tear type, patient age, activity level, and other identified injuries.

**Complications:** Potential risks include infection, excessive bleeding, blood clots, shoulder stiffness, and damage to blood vessels or nerves.

**Rehabilitation:** Post-surgery protection typically involves a sling for 2 to 6 weeks. Physical therapy initially emphasizes flexibility and gentle stretching, progressing to rotator cuff strengthening exercises 6 to 10 weeks after surgery. Throwing athletes generally resume interval throwing 3 to 4 months after surgery.

## Outcomes
Most patients report improved shoulder strength and less pain after surgery. Recovery duration varies individually depending on injury complexity, health conditions, and activity goals. Full recovery may take several months, but following surgeon guidelines and rehabilitation plans ensures successful outcomes.
