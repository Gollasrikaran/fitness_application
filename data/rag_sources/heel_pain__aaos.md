# Heel Pain

## Overview
Walking places substantial cumulative stress on the feet — each mile walked generates roughly 60 tons of pressure on each foot. While the feet are structurally capable of managing significant load, excessive stress can exceed their tolerance, frequently resulting in heel pain, which is the most common problem affecting the foot.

## Anatomy
The foot's skeleton is typically divided into three regions: the rear section (hindfoot), the middle section (midfoot), and the front section (forefoot). The heel bone (calcaneus) is the largest bone in the foot and sits in the hindfoot. Two important soft-tissue attachments connect to the heel:

- The Achilles tendon comes down from the calf muscle and attaches to the back of the heel
- The plantar fascia, a thick fibrous band that runs along the sole of the foot, also attaches to the heel bone

Both attachment points, where soft tissue connects to bone, are prone to inflammation and are common sources of pain.

## Cause
Heel pain has various sources, generally grouped into two main locations:

**Pain under the heel:**
- **Stone bruise.** Directly stepping on a hard object (a rock or stone) can bruise the fat pad under the heel, sometimes causing visible discoloration.
- **Plantar fasciitis.** Excessive running or jumping inflames the fibrous band connecting the heel bone to the base of the toes, and this is often made worse by worn-out athletic shoes.
- **Heel spur.** Untreated plantar fasciitis can lead to a calcium deposit where the fascia attaches to the heel bone; this is a result of the underlying problem rather than the cause of it.
- **Calcaneus stress fracture.** Repeated high-impact activity can gradually damage and eventually fracture the heel bone.
- **Nerve entrapment.** Impact activities can cause swelling and inflammation of nerves under the heel bone.
- **Calcaneus fracture.** Falls from a height or car accidents can fracture the heel bone; these injuries are less common but can be severe, often requiring surgical reconstruction.

**Pain at the back of the heel:**
- **Retrocalcaneal bursitis.** The area where the Achilles tendon attaches to the heel bone becomes inflamed, often from excessive running or friction from footwear.
- **Insertional Achilles tendinosis.** Breakdown of collagen within the tendon at its attachment to the heel causes it to thicken and become painful over time.

Continuing to place stress on an already injured heel worsens existing pain and can lead to further complications.

## Symptoms
Heel pain typically presents as follows:

- Swelling and tenderness commonly accompany the pain
- Plantar fasciitis causes pain under the foot that may start out mild and then intensify when first moving after getting out of bed, exiting a car, or standing up after sitting for a while
- Pain at the back of the heel develops gradually, causing thickened, red, and swollen skin; this pain is worst when resuming activity after rest, and ordinary shoes frequently trigger discomfort
- Some people with pain at the back of the heel develop a tender, warm bump on the back of the heel; people born with a prominent version of this bone shape face a higher risk of heel pain as they get older

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
The doctor will:

- Ask about the nature of the pain and any accompanying symptoms
- Feel and look at the bottom and back of the heel, checking for tenderness and swelling that suggest plantar fasciitis or retrocalcaneal bursitis
- Possibly apply pressure to check for a stress fracture
- Possibly ask you to walk, stand on one leg, or perform other movements to help identify the source of pain
- Assess range of motion and tissue flexibility

### Imaging
- **X-rays** are recommended for prolonged heel pain to identify underlying causes including plantar fasciitis, calcaneus stress fracture, and insertional tendinosis.
- **MRI** is rarely needed at first; it is reserved for cases where conservative treatment fails and X-rays are inconclusive, since it gives a much better view of soft tissue and can definitively diagnose Achilles tendon problems, calcaneus stress fracture, bursitis, and fasciitis.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Conservative management includes:

- **Rest.** Minimizing weight-bearing until symptoms improve; pain from a stone bruise naturally settles down with reduced activity.
- **Ice.** Applying ice to the back of the heel reduces inflammation in 20-minute applications separated by equal rest periods (never applying ice directly to the skin — use a protective barrier).
- **NSAIDs.** Anti-inflammatory medication such as ibuprofen reduces swelling.
- **Taping.** Wrapping the heel can reduce discomfort and support healing in cases of fasciitis.
- **Orthotic inserts.** Heel pads or cups provide support; open-backed shoes benefit people with tendon problems at the back of the heel.
- **Night splinting.** Devices worn overnight significantly reduce pain that occurs first thing in the morning.
- **Rehabilitation exercises.** Programs that emphasize stretching the Achilles tendon and calf are critical for resolving symptoms and preventing recurrence.

Long-standing heel pain requires an extended treatment period — typically several months — often combining multiple interventions.

### Surgical Treatment
Most cases resolve with conservative therapy. Pain that does not respond to months of nonsurgical treatment may warrant surgical consideration, targeting the specific anatomical source of pain (available for insertional tendinosis and plantar fasciitis).

## Conclusion
Heel pain is a frequently encountered foot problem that can arise from multiple causes affecting the back, side, or bottom of the heel. A professional clinical evaluation helps establish the underlying cause and guides individualized management. Most cases improve on their own through avoiding aggravating impact activity and doing targeted stretching exercises, typically without the need for surgery.
