# NICE Guideline NG219: Gout - Diagnosis and Management (Recommendations)

Published: 9 June 2022. This guideline updates and replaces NICE technology appraisal guidance 164, NICE technology appraisal guidance 281, and NICE evidence summary ESNM23.

## 1.1 Diagnosis and Assessment

**Suspicion of gout**: Suspect gout in people presenting with rapid onset (often overnight) of severe pain together with redness and swelling, in 1 or both first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joints, or when tophi are present. Gout should also be considered in other joints showing similar rapid-onset inflammatory symptoms.

**Diagnostic testing**: The primary diagnostic tool is serum urate measurement. "Measure the serum urate level in people with symptoms and signs of gout ... to confirm the clinical diagnosis (serum urate level of 360 micromol/litre [6 mg/dl] or more)."

For uncertain diagnoses, joint aspiration with synovial fluid microscopy, or imaging (X-ray, ultrasound, dual-energy CT), is recommended.

## 1.2 Information and Support
Clinicians should provide tailored education explaining gout's progressive nature, individual risk factors, and treatment options, including that "gout is a lifelong condition that benefits from long-term urate-lowering therapy (ULT) to eliminate urate crystals and prevent flares, shrink tophi and prevent long-term joint damage."

## 1.3 Managing Gout Flares

**Acute treatment**: First-line options include NSAIDs, colchicine, or oral corticosteroids. Intra-articular or intramuscular corticosteroid injections are reserved for cases where NSAIDs and colchicine are unsuitable. IL-1 inhibitors require specialist referral.

**Supportive care**: Patients should be advised that "applying ice packs to the affected joint (cold therapy) in addition to taking prescribed medicine may help alleviate pain."

## 1.4 Diet and Lifestyle
The guideline notes that "there is not enough evidence to show that any specific diet prevents flares or lowers serum urate levels." Patients should follow a healthy, balanced diet instead. Excess body weight, obesity, or excessive alcohol consumption may exacerbate gout flares and symptoms.

## 1.5 Long-Term Management and Urate-Lowering Therapy (ULT)

**Indications for ULT**: Therapy is recommended for patients with multiple flares, chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages 3-5, diuretic use, tophi, or chronic gouty arthritis. Discussion of ULT should occur after any flare, and starting ULT is generally recommended at least 2 to 4 weeks after a gout flare has settled.

**Treat-to-target strategy**: "Start with a low dose of ULT and use monthly serum urate levels to guide dose increases, as tolerated, until the target serum urate level is reached."

**Target levels**: The standard target is "below 360 micromol/litre (6 mg/dl)." A lower target of "below 300 micromol/litre (5 mg/dl)" is considered for patients with tophi or chronic arthritis.

**First-line agents**: Allopurinol or febuxostat are offered as initial treatment. Allopurinol is preferred for patients with major cardiovascular disease.

**Flare prevention during ULT initiation**: Colchicine is the first choice during dose titration, with NSAIDs or corticosteroids as alternatives if colchicine is unsuitable.

## 1.5.15 Monitoring
Annual serum urate monitoring is recommended for patients continuing ULT after reaching their target level.

## 1.6 Referral to Specialist Services
Rheumatology referral should be considered when diagnosis is uncertain, treatment fails, patients have advanced CKD (stages 3b-5), or they have undergone organ transplantation.
