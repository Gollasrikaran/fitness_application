# Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

## Description & Anatomy
Carpal tunnel syndrome is a common condition that causes numbness, tingling, and pain in the hand and forearm. The condition develops when one of the major nerves to the hand -- the median nerve -- is squeezed or compressed as it travels through the wrist.

The carpal tunnel itself is a narrow passageway in the wrist, about an inch wide, with floors and sides formed by small wrist bones. The roof of the tunnel is a strong band of connective tissue called the transverse carpal ligament. Because these boundaries are rigid, the carpal tunnel has little capacity to stretch or increase in size.

The median nerve originates as a group of nerve roots in the neck and travels down the upper arm, across the elbow, and into the forearm, then passes through the carpal tunnel at the wrist on its way to the hand and fingers. It provides sensation to the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and half of the ring finger, and controls the muscles around the base of the thumb. Nine flexor tendons also travel through the tunnel, enabling finger and thumb bending.

Without treatment, in most patients carpal tunnel syndrome gets worse over time, and can lead to permanent dysfunction of the hand, including loss of sensation in the fingers and weakness.

## Cause
Most cases result from a combination of factors. Women and older people show higher incidence rates. Risk factors include:
- **Heredity**: The carpal tunnel may be naturally smaller in some people, or there may be anatomic differences that change the amount of space for the nerve, and this can run in families.
- **Repetitive hand use**: Repeating the same hand and wrist motions or activities over a prolonged period may aggravate the tendons in the wrist, causing swelling.
- **Hand and wrist position**: Extreme flexion or extension of the hand and wrist for a prolonged period increases pressure on the nerve.
- **Pregnancy**: Hormonal changes during pregnancy can cause swelling that results in pressure on the nerve.
- **Health conditions**: Diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and thyroid gland imbalance are associated with carpal tunnel syndrome.

## Symptoms
Symptoms include:
- Numbness, tingling, burning, and pain, primarily in the thumb and index, middle, and ring fingers. This often wakes people up at night.
- Occasional shock-like sensations that radiate to the thumb and index, middle, and ring fingers.
- Pain or tingling that may travel up the forearm toward the shoulder.
- Weakness and clumsiness in the hand -- this may make it difficult to perform fine movements.
- Dropping things, due to weakness, numbness, or a loss of proprioception (joint position sense).

Onset is typically gradual. Many patients find that their symptoms come and go at first. However, as the condition worsens, symptoms may occur more frequently or persist for longer periods of time. Nighttime symptoms are common because many people sleep with their wrists bent. Daytime symptoms often occur when holding something for a prolonged period of time with the wrist bent forward or backward, such as when using a phone, driving, or reading a book.

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
The doctor will review medical history and perform physical tests, which may include:
- Performing "Tinel's sign" by tapping along the median nerve on the palm side of the wrist and hand to see if it causes any tingling into the fingers.
- Bending and holding the wrists in a flexed position to test for numbness or tingling.
- Testing sensitivity in the fingertips and hands by lightly touching them with a special instrument while the eyes are closed.
- Checking for weakness in the muscles around the base of the thumb.
- Looking for atrophy in the muscles around the base of the thumb; in severe cases, these muscles may become visibly smaller.

In many cases, carpal tunnel syndrome can be diagnosed by taking a medical history and performing a physical exam, and no further testing is needed.

### Diagnostic Tests
Electrodiagnostic tests may be ordered to measure how well the median nerve is working and help determine whether there is too much pressure on the nerve. These tests help determine the severity of carpal tunnel syndrome, whether compression occurs in other locations, whether other nerves are affected, and whether additional medical conditions affect the nerves. Types include:
- **Nerve conduction studies (NCS)**: These tests measure the signals travelling in the nerves of the hand and arm and can detect when a nerve is not conducting its signal effectively.
- **Electromyogram (EMG)**: An EMG measures the electrical activity in muscles. EMG results can show whether there is any nerve or muscle damage.

**Ultrasound** uses high-frequency sound waves to help create pictures of bone and tissue, to evaluate the median nerve for signs of compression.

**X-rays** show images of dense structures, such as bone, to help exclude other causes for symptoms, such as arthritis, ligament injury, or a fracture.

**MRI scans** provide better images of the body's soft tissues than X-rays, to help determine other causes for symptoms or to look for abnormal tissues that could be impacting the median nerve.

## Nonsurgical Treatment
For early-stage diagnosis, the symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome can often be relieved without surgery. Options include:
- **Bracing or splinting**: Wearing a brace or splint at night will keep you from bending your wrist while you sleep. Keeping your wrist in a straight or neutral position reduces pressure on the nerve. Splints may also be worn during aggravating daytime activities.
- **NSAIDs**: Anti-inflammatory medications such as ibuprofen and naproxen can help relieve pain and inflammation.
- **Activity changes**: Since symptoms often occur when the hand and wrist are in the same position for too long, modifying activities or workstations can slow or stop progression of the disease.
- **Nerve gliding exercises**: Some patients may benefit from exercises that help the median nerve move more freely within the confines of the carpal tunnel.
- **Steroid injections**: Corticosteroid, or cortisone, is a powerful anti-inflammatory agent that can be injected into the carpal tunnel. These injections often relieve painful symptoms, but typically provide only temporary relief and not long-term benefit.
- **PRP injections**: Derived from the patient's own blood, some studies demonstrate temporary benefit from PRP injection in patients with carpal tunnel syndrome, though there is not likely a long-term benefit.

## Surgical Treatment
Surgery is recommended when nonsurgical treatment does not relieve symptoms or provides only temporary relief. The decision considers symptom severity, physical exam findings, response to non-operative treatment, and testing results. In long-standing cases with constant numbness and wasting of the thumb muscles, surgery may be recommended to prevent irreversible damage.

### Surgical Procedure
The procedure is called "carpal tunnel release." The goal is to relieve pressure on the median nerve by cutting the ligament that forms the roof of the tunnel (transverse carpal ligament). Release of this ligament increases the size of the tunnel and decreases pressure on the median nerve, allowing for appropriate blood flow to the nerve and function.

In most cases, carpal tunnel surgery is done on an outpatient basis. The surgery can be done under general anesthesia, which puts you to sleep, or under local anesthesia, which numbs just the hand and arm.

**Open carpal tunnel release**: The surgeon makes a small incision in the palm of the hand and views the inside of the hand and wrist through this incision, then divides the transverse carpal ligament (the roof of the carpal tunnel). This increases the size of the tunnel and decreases pressure on the median nerve. After surgery, the ligament may gradually grow back together in a lengthened fashion, but there will be more space in the carpal tunnel.

**Endoscopic carpal tunnel release**: The doctor makes one or two smaller skin incisions (called portals) and uses a miniature camera, or endoscope, to see inside the hand and wrist. A special knife is used to divide the transverse carpal ligament, similar to the open carpal tunnel release procedure.

The outcomes of open surgery and endoscopic surgery are similar. There are benefits and potential risks associated with both techniques.

### Recovery From Surgery
Immediately after surgery, you will be encouraged to elevate your hand above your heart and move your fingers to reduce swelling and prevent stiffness.

You should expect some pain, swelling, and stiffness after your procedure. Minor soreness in your palm may last several weeks to several months. This can typically be treated with acetaminophen and/or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Nighttime symptoms improve dramatically for most patients in the first week or so after surgery.

**Grip and pinch strength** usually return by about 2 to 3 months after surgery. If the condition of the median nerve was poor before surgery, grip and pinch strength may not improve for about 6 to 12 months.

**Numbness and tingling** tend to improve over the first several months after surgery. Some patients with mild disease may experience return of sensation right away. Patients with severe disease may not have normal sensation in their fingertips for 6 to 12 months after surgery.

You may have to wear a splint or wrist brace for several weeks after surgery. You will be allowed to use your hand for light activities, taking care to avoid significant discomfort. Driving, self-care activities, and light lifting and gripping may be permitted soon after surgery.

### Complications
Common complications include bleeding, infection, wound healing issues, and nerve aggravation or injury.

### Outcomes
For most patients, surgery will improve the symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome. Recovery, however, may be gradual, and complete recovery may take up to a year. If you have significant pain and weakness for more than 2 months, your doctor may refer you to a hand therapist who can help you maximize your recovery.

Additional conditions like arthritis or tendinitis may slow recovery. In long-standing cases of carpal tunnel syndrome with severe loss of feeling and/or muscle wasting around the base of the thumb, recovery will also be slower, and for these patients a complete recovery may not be possible.

Occasionally, carpal tunnel syndrome can recur, although this is rare. If this happens, additional treatment or surgery may be needed.
