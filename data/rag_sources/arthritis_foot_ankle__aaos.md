# Arthritis of the Foot and Ankle

## Overview
Arthritis is inflammation of one or more joints, and it frequently affects the many small joints of the foot and ankle. More than one hundred distinct forms of arthritis exist, and many of them target the foot and ankle region, making routine walking and recreational activities challenging for those affected.

While arthritis has no cure, numerous treatment approaches can slow disease progression and relieve discomfort. With appropriate management, most people with arthritis are able to control their symptoms, stay physically active, and maintain a good quality of life.

## Anatomy
During standing, walking, and running, the foot and ankle provide support, absorb shock, and help maintain balance. Three bones make up the ankle joint, which allows mostly up-and-down motion. The foot itself contains 28 bones and more than 30 joints, allowing for a wide range of movement.

Many of these joints are covered by articular cartilage — a smooth substance at the ends of bones that lets them glide against each other without friction. A membrane called the synovium surrounds each joint and produces fluid that lubricates the joint and nourishes the cartilage.

Strong bands of fibrous tissue called ligaments connect bones to each other and stabilize the joints. Muscles and tendons provide additional support and generate movement.

## Description: Major Types of Arthritis

### Osteoarthritis
This "wear-and-tear" type of arthritis commonly affects middle-aged people but can develop earlier. The protective cartilage gradually wears down, becoming rough and thin. As cartilage is lost, joint space narrows, sometimes leading to bone rubbing on bone and the formation of painful bony growths called osteophytes (bone spurs).

Contributing factors include advancing age, excess body weight, and a family history of the disease. Symptoms usually develop gradually and worsen over time.

### Rheumatoid Arthritis
This chronic, whole-body condition often begins in the foot and ankle and affects multiple joints throughout the body, frequently in a symmetrical pattern (the same joints on both sides of the body).

Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease — the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissue. Immune cells invade the synovial membrane lining the joint, causing swelling. Over time, the inflamed synovium invades and damages bone, cartilage, ligaments, and tendons, which can lead to severe joint deformity and disability.

Rheumatoid arthritis is not directly inherited, but a genetic predisposition can make certain people more susceptible. A triggering event — an infection or an environmental factor — activates these susceptibility genes. Following this trigger, immune cells produce substances that attack the joints.

### Posttraumatic Arthritis
Injury to the foot or ankle can lead to this form of arthritis. Fractures and dislocations, especially those involving the joint surface, are the most common causes. Like osteoarthritis, cartilage breakdown is the hallmark of this condition, and it can develop years after the original injury.

An injured joint is about seven times more likely to develop arthritis than a joint that has never been injured, regardless of how well the original injury was treated. After trauma, the body may release hormones that promote the death of cartilage cells.

## Symptoms
Symptoms vary depending on which joint is affected. Arthritic joints are frequently painful and inflamed. Pain usually develops gradually, though sudden onset is also possible.

Other symptoms include:

- Pain with movement
- Pain that increases with vigorous activity
- Tenderness when the joint is touched
- Joint swelling, warmth, and redness
- Increased pain and swelling in the morning or after resting
- Difficulty walking because of any of these symptoms

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
When examining the foot and ankle, doctors typically:

- Review the patient's overall medical history and general health
- Ask about current prescription and over-the-counter medications
- Feel the joints for tenderness and swelling
- Ask about symptom details: when they started, exact location (one side or both), pattern (constant or intermittent), timing (worse in morning or evening, with activity), and duration
- Ask about any prior foot and ankle injuries, including when they occurred and how they were treated
- Examine shoes for abnormal wear patterns and check whether they provide adequate support

### Gait Analysis
Observing how a patient walks is an important part of the evaluation. Limited motion and joint pain change the way a person walks, and the pattern of limping can reveal the severity and location of the arthritis.

During gait analysis, doctors:

- Evaluate alignment of the bones while walking
- Measure stride length
- Assess the strength of the ankle and foot muscles

### Diagnostic Testing
**X-rays.** X-rays provide detailed images of bone. Arthritic feet typically show:

- Narrowing of the space between joints (indicating cartilage loss)
- Changes in the bone (including evidence of fracture)
- Formation of bone spurs

Weight-bearing X-rays — taken while standing — are more useful because they more accurately reveal the extent of the arthritis, which joints are involved, and the degree of any deformity. Non-weight-bearing images do not adequately show the severity or nature of the arthritis.

**Additional imaging.** Bone scans, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are occasionally needed to evaluate bone and soft tissue conditions.

**Blood tests.** Laboratory testing can help identify the specific type of arthritis present. Diagnosing rheumatoid arthritis in particular relies heavily on blood test accuracy.

Rheumatologists are often involved in evaluating suspected cases of rheumatoid arthritis. While physical exam findings and test results may suggest the diagnosis, specialists make the definitive determination and consider other, less common inflammatory conditions.

## Nonsurgical Treatment

### Lifestyle Modifications
Adjustments to daily routines can help control symptoms and slow disease progression:

- Limiting activities that make symptoms worse
- Switching from high-impact pursuits (jogging, racquet sports) to gentler alternatives (swimming, cycling) that reduce stress on the foot and ankle
- Losing weight to reduce joint loading, which improves function and decreases pain

### Physical Therapy
Targeted exercises can increase range of motion, improve flexibility, and strengthen the muscles of the foot and ankle. Doctors tailor exercise programs to each patient's needs and activity goals.

While physical therapy often reduces stress on arthritic joints, it can occasionally increase pain by increasing friction between damaged joint surfaces. If exercise worsens symptoms, physicians will discontinue this approach.

### Assistive Devices
Supportive devices, including ankle-foot orthoses, can improve mobility and function.

Shoe modifications such as orthotic inserts or specially made shoes with stiff soles and rocker bottoms reduce stress on the foot and decrease pain. For feet with a deformity, a tilted insert that realigns the foot and ankle may reduce joint strain.

### Medications
**NSAIDs.** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen and naproxen reduce inflammation and help manage pain.

**Steroid injections.** This simple office procedure involves injecting a steroid (such as triamcinolone or methylprednisolone) combined with an anesthetic (such as lidocaine or bupivacaine) into the arthritic joint. While it provides temporary pain relief and reduces inflammation, the effect is limited in duration.

## Surgical Treatment
When nonsurgical treatments fail and arthritis-related disability continues, surgery may be appropriate. The choice of procedure depends on the type of arthritis, its location, and how extensive the disease is. Sometimes more than one surgical approach is needed.

### Arthroscopic Debridement
This procedure, used for early-stage arthritis, removes loose fragments of cartilage, inflamed synovial tissue, and bone spurs around the joint.

During arthroscopy, surgeons insert a small camera (arthroscope) into the joint and use a video monitor to guide surgical instruments. Because the instruments are thin, only small incisions are needed instead of a large surgical opening.

Arthroscopy is most helpful when pain is caused by bone spurs rubbing against each other without significant narrowing of the joint space. However, removing bone spurs may speed up cartilage breakdown by increasing joint motion.

### Arthrodesis (Fusion)
This procedure permanently eliminates motion between the bones of a diseased joint by fusing them into one continuous bone. After removing the damaged cartilage, the surgeon places hardware — pins, plates, screws, or rods — to hold the joint in the desired position while the bones fuse, similar to how a broken bone heals. Eliminating motion eliminates the pain caused by that motion.

Arthrodesis generally produces good results, but complications can include:

- Nonunion (failure of the bones to fuse), which can sometimes lead to hardware breakage. A broken piece of metal is not painful by itself, but nonunion causes renewed pain and swelling. Repeat surgery with bone graft and new hardware may be necessary. Repeat fusion attempts have a lower success rate, making strict adherence to post-operative rehabilitation important.
- A small percentage of patients experience wound healing problems, which are usually managed with local wound care or additional surgery.
- Loss of motion after fusion can cause nearby joints to bear more stress, which may lead to arthritis developing in previously healthy joints years later.

### Total Ankle Replacement (Arthroplasty)
The surgeon removes damaged cartilage and bone and replaces the joint surfaces with an artificial metal or plastic implant that restores normal function.

Although less common than hip or knee replacement, recent improvements in implant design have made this a viable option for many patients.

Ankle replacement is suited for patients with:

- Severe ankle arthritis
- Complete destruction of the joint surfaces
- Pain that substantially interferes with daily functioning

Replacement provides better pain relief and mobility than fusion. Because it preserves joint motion, nearby joints experience less stress, reducing the chance of arthritis developing in those joints later.

Implant components can occasionally loosen or wear out over time. If the implant fails severely, revision surgery — replacing the failed implant with new components — may be needed.

An alternative is removing the implant and fusing the joint. This is technically more difficult than a primary fusion. Once the implant is removed, the resulting bone gap must be filled with graft material to preserve leg length. Because newly grafted bone may be weaker, the risk of nonunion increases, and subsequent fusion is more complicated than an initial procedure.

## Recovery
Surgical treatment typically succeeds in eliminating arthritis pain and restoring the ability to perform daily activities. Full recovery takes four to nine months, depending on the severity of the condition before surgery and how complex the procedure was.

Foot and ankle surgery involves notable discomfort. While some pain is to be expected, advances in pain management have improved doctors' ability to control and minimize it. Pain medication is given immediately after surgery, and the physician may prescribe additional pain relievers for use at home for a limited time.

A cast is typically applied after surgery to restrict foot and ankle movement and prevent the fusion from failing. Keeping the foot elevated above heart level for one to two weeks after surgery helps reduce swelling.

Physical therapy to restore strength and motion in the foot and ankle may be part of ongoing recovery.

Most patients return to their normal daily activities within three to four months, sometimes with the temporary use of supportive footwear or orthotic devices.
