# Ankylosing spondylitis

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a long-term condition in which the spine and other areas of the body become inflamed. It's a type of axial spondyloarthritis and usually first develops in teenagers and young adults. Symptoms tend to develop gradually, usually over several months or years, and may come and go over time. For some people the condition improves with time, but for others it can gradually get worse.

## Symptoms

- Back pain and stiffness that gets better with exercise but does not improve, or gets worse, with rest
- Pain and stiffness that is worse in the morning and at night, sometimes waking you
- Pain in the buttock region
- **Arthritis** - pain on moving the affected joint, tenderness when the joint is examined, and swelling and warmth in the affected area (commonly hips and knees)
- **Enthesitis** - painful inflammation where a bone is joined to a tendon or ligament, commonly at the shin bone, Achilles tendon, heel, and where the ribs attach to the breastbone (which can also cause chest pain and breathing difficulties)
- **Fatigue** - feeling tired and lacking in energy

People with AS may also develop associated conditions such as iritis (inflammation of the eye), psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease.

## When to see a GP

See a GP if you have persistent symptoms of AS. They may examine your back and joints, and if AS is suspected they'll usually refer you to a rheumatologist for further tests, including blood tests and imaging.

## Causes

The exact cause of AS is not known, but there's thought to be a link with a particular gene variant known as HLA-B27.

## Treatment

There's no cure for AS and it's not possible to reverse the damage already caused, but treatment can help relieve symptoms and slow, or possibly stop, progression of the condition.

### Exercise and physiotherapy
Staying active helps improve posture and range of spinal movement, and can prevent your spine becoming stiff. Options include:
- a group exercise programme, where you exercise with others
- an individual exercise programme of exercises to do by yourself
- hydrotherapy - exercise in water, usually a warm, shallow swimming pool

Swimming and sport are also good ways of keeping active, and daily stretching is important.

### Painkillers
- NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac or etoricoxib) are usually the first type of painkiller prescribed.
- Paracetamol rarely causes side effects and can be used in women who are pregnant or breastfeeding.
- Codeine offers stronger pain relief but can cause nausea, vomiting, constipation and drowsiness.

### Biological treatments
- Anti-TNF medicines are given by injection and work by preventing the effects of TNF, a protein that causes inflammation. Treatment usually stops if there's no improvement after 3 months.
- Monoclonal antibodies such as secukinumab and ixekizumab may be offered if NSAIDs or anti-TNF medicine haven't worked.

### JAK inhibitors
A type of medicine taken as tablets, which may be offered to people with AS who have not responded to anti-TNF medicine.

### Corticosteroids
Can be injected directly into a joint, with a recommended limit of 3 injections a year, spaced at least 3 months apart.

### DMARDs
Sulfasalazine and methotrexate may be used to treat inflammation in joints other than the spine.

### Surgery
Most people with AS will not need surgery, but hip replacement or corrective spinal surgery may be considered for severe damage.

## Complications

Long-term complications can include weakening of the bones (which can increase the risk of spinal fractures) and cardiovascular disease. With modern treatments, AS does not normally significantly affect life expectancy.
