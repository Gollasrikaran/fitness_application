# Package of Interventions for Rehabilitation, Module 2: Musculoskeletal Conditions (WHO)

Published: 5 July 2023 (Geneva: World Health Organization; 2023). ISBN 978-92-4-007110-0 (electronic version). Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO. This module is part of WHO's 8-module "Package of interventions for rehabilitation" series and covers six musculoskeletal conditions: low back pain, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcopenia, fractures, and amputation. It is intended as "an indispensable resource for countries when planning for and budgeting the integration of rehabilitation services into their health systems," setting out for each condition the recommended assessments, interventions, required assistive products/equipment/consumables, and the rehabilitation workforce needed to deliver them.

## About Low Back Pain

"Low back pain is a condition that many people experience during their lifetime and is the leading cause of years lived with disability." Prevalence accelerates from about 15 years of age and peaks in older age (80-89 years). Low back pain is defined as primary when persistent or recurrent pain is not reliably attributed to an underlying disease process (such as ankylosing spondylitis) or structural pathology. Most people experience a short-term episode, but in about a third it extends to 12 months or more, or recurs across the life course. It is estimated that in 2019, 568 million people worldwide were living with low back pain and associated problems in functioning that could benefit from rehabilitation. "For people with chronic low back pain, multidisciplinary biopsychosocial rehabilitation, including both physical exercise programmes to restore motor functions, and psychological approaches to enable people with low back pain to cope better with pain, are more effective in improving physical tolerances towards daily activities and general well-being than physical treatment alone."

## About Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis is "a chronic condition and the most common form of arthritis," influenced by an interaction between genetic, biomechanical, metabolic, and biochemical responses. It is characterized by joint pain, stiffness and swelling, most commonly in weight-bearing sites (knees, hips, feet, spine) and sites subject to repetitive stress (hands, shoulders). Risk factors include joint injury, being overweight or obese, being female, and older age. In 2019, an estimated 344 million people worldwide were living with osteoarthritis and associated functioning problems that could benefit from rehabilitation. Rehabilitation targets joint function, pain and swelling, and mobility-related functions such as muscle strength, and promotes self-management skills and psychological health to support continued participation in work and social life.

## About Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis is "an autoimmune inflammatory disease that primarily affects synovial joints," particularly the small joints of the hands, wrists and feet, though any synovial joint may be affected. Systemic inflammation can lead to joint swelling and degradation of articular and periarticular tissues, alongside extra-articular conditions such as cardiovascular disease. Risk factors include age, female gender, smoking and obesity. In 2019, an estimated 13 million people worldwide were living with rheumatoid arthritis and associated functioning problems that could benefit from rehabilitation. "Interventions for rehabilitation address both the symptoms related to the disease, such as pain, stiffness, or fatigue, as well as the factors that impact the disease activity and comorbidities, such as nutrition or lack of physical activity," in addition to helping people develop self-management and coping strategies.

## About Sarcopenia

Sarcopenia is "a progressive and generalized skeletal muscle condition involving the accelerated loss of muscle mass and function." It occurs commonly as an age-related process but can also occur in mid-life in association with other conditions; it is termed "secondary" when causal factors other than ageing (for example inflammatory processes, malignancy or organ failure) are evident. Prevalence is estimated at approximately 6-22% of adults aged 65 and over. Physical activity and nutrition are protective factors for both prevention and management; rehabilitation targets loss of muscle function itself, its consequences (reduced activity, increased fall risk), and the associated risk of secondary conditions such as fall-related fractures.

## About Fractures

A fracture is "a break in the continuity of a bone that can occur in any body part and at any age." The top three anatomical sites by years lived with disability are the lower limb, pelvis, and hip, and falls are the leading cause of fracture-related disability. Fractures may result from physical trauma, overuse, or conditions associated with loss of bone strength such as osteoporosis or cancer. In 2019, an estimated 436 million people worldwide were living with fractures and associated functioning problems that could benefit from rehabilitation. Rehabilitation should start immediately following acute treatment and continue until optimal functioning is achieved, supporting bone healing, preventing deterioration of function, and helping to prevent secondary conditions such as deep venous thromboembolism and pneumonia.

## About Amputation

An amputation is the removal of a limb or body part due to trauma or surgery (for causes such as cancer, diabetes, infection or pain). Symptoms range from physical impairments to phantom limb sensations and pain. In 2019, an estimated 176 million people worldwide were living with amputation and associated functioning problems that could benefit from rehabilitation. Rehabilitation should start in the acute phase and continue until optimal functioning is achieved; preparation for, selection, fitting and training in the use of prosthesis is a core component, alongside psychosocial care supporting return to daily life, work, and community and social participation.

## Common Rehabilitation Intervention Framework

Across all six conditions, the module organizes rehabilitation into a consistent "assessment leads to intervention" structure grouped by functional target area, typically including:

- **Pain management**: assessment of pain, paired with interventions such as oral analgesics/NSAIDs (from WHO's Model List of Essential Medicines), cognitive behavioural therapy, joint mobilization, spinal manipulative therapy (for low back pain), and physical exercise training.
- **Exercise and fitness**: assessment of exercise capacity, paired with fitness/aerobic training using equipment such as cycle ergometers, resistance bands, weights and exercise mats.
- **Activities of daily living (ADL)**: assessment of ADL function, paired with ADL training and provision of assistive products for self-care (adapted eating/drinking products, dressing and toileting aids).
- **Mobility**: assistive product provision and training (canes, crutches, walking frames, wheelchairs, orthoses) and, for conditions such as fractures, gait pattern assessment and physical exercise training.
- **Education and vocation**: vocational assessment paired with vocational counselling, training and support, and workplace modification.
- **Lifestyle modification**: assessment of lifestyle risk factors (including nutritional status) paired with education, advice and support for a healthy lifestyle.
- **Self-management**: education, advice and support for self-management of the health condition, including joint protection principles, energy conservation, pacing, and self-directed exercise.
- **Mental health**: assessment of mental health (depression, anxiety, emotional distress) paired with antidepressants, psychological therapies (including CBT), physical exercise training, and stress management training.
- **Secondary condition prevention** (condition-specific): for example breathing exercises and anticoagulants to prevent pneumonia and deep venous thromboembolism after fracture.

## Workforce

The rehabilitation specialists identified across the musculoskeletal conditions module include: dietitians and nutritionists, nursing professionals, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, psychologists, social work and counselling professionals, specialist medical practitioners/physical and rehabilitation medicine (PRM) physicians, and (for fractures and amputation) prosthetists and orthotists.
