# Frozen Shoulder (Adhesive Capsulitis)

## Overview
Frozen shoulder, also called adhesive capsulitis, causes pain and stiffness in the shoulder. Over time, the shoulder becomes very hard to move. After a period of worsening symptoms, a frozen shoulder tends to get better, although full recovery may take up to 3 years. Physical therapy, with a focus on shoulder flexibility, is the primary treatment recommendation.

Frozen shoulder most commonly affects people between the ages of 40 and 60, and occurs in women more often than men. People with diabetes and thyroid conditions are at an increased risk for developing frozen shoulder, though it can happen to anyone at any time, sometimes after an injury or surgery.

## Anatomy
The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint made up of the humerus (upper arm bone), scapula (shoulder blade), and clavicle (collarbone). The head of the upper arm bone fits into a shallow socket in the shoulder blade. Strong connective tissue called the shoulder capsule surrounds the joint, and synovial fluid lubricates the capsule and joint to help it move easily.

## Description
In frozen shoulder, the shoulder capsule thickens and becomes stiff and tight. Thick bands of tissue — called adhesions — develop, and in many cases there is less synovial fluid in the joint. The hallmark signs are severe pain and being unable to move the shoulder, either on your own or with help. It develops in three stages:

- **Stage 1 — Freezing**: Pain slowly worsens, and as it worsens the shoulder loses range of motion. This stage typically lasts 6 weeks to 9 months.
- **Stage 2 — Frozen**: Pain may actually improve, but stiffness remains. Daily activities may be very difficult during the 4-to-6-month "frozen" stage.
- **Stage 3 — Thawing**: Shoulder motion slowly improves. Complete return to normal or close to normal strength and motion typically takes 6 months to 2 years.

## Cause
The causes of frozen shoulder are not fully understood, and there is no clear connection to arm dominance or occupation. Risk factors include:

- **Diabetes**: Frozen shoulder occurs much more often in people with diabetes, and diabetic patients tend to have greater stiffness that lasts longer before "thawing."
- **Other diseases**: Hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Parkinson's disease, and cardiac disease are associated with frozen shoulder.
- **Immobilization**: Frozen shoulder can develop after a shoulder has been held in one position without moving for a period of time due to surgery, a fracture, or other injury. Moving the shoulder soon after injury or surgery is one measure used to help prevent it.

## Symptoms
Pain from frozen shoulder is usually dull or aching, is typically worse early in the disease and when moving the arm, and is usually located over the outer shoulder and sometimes the upper arm.

## Doctor Examination
After discussing symptoms and medical history, the doctor examines the shoulder by moving it in all directions to check "passive range of motion" (when someone else moves the shoulder) and comparing this to "active range of motion" (moving it yourself). People with frozen shoulder have limited range of motion both actively and passively.

Imaging tests may help rule out other causes of stiffness and pain:
- **X-rays**: May show other problems such as arthritis.
- **MRI and ultrasound**: Not required to diagnose frozen shoulder, but may help identify other soft-tissue injuries such as a rotator cuff tear.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Frozen shoulder generally gets better over time without surgery, although it may take up to 3 years. Most people improve with relatively simple treatments to control pain and restore motion.

- Optimizing other health conditions (for example, improving glucose control in diabetes) can help speed recovery.
- Anti-inflammatory medicines can reduce pain and swelling.
- Steroid injections directly into the shoulder joint are a powerful anti-inflammatory option.
- Hydrodilatation — gently injecting a large volume of sterile fluid into the shoulder joint under imaging guidance to expand and stretch the capsule — may be recommended if other nonsurgical methods do not relieve symptoms.
- Physical therapy with stretching and range-of-motion exercises, sometimes preceded by heat, helps restore motion. Example exercises include:
  - **External rotation — passive stretch**: standing in a doorway, bending the affected elbow to 90 degrees against the door jamb, and rotating the body to stretch, holding 30 seconds.
  - **Forward flexion — supine position**: lying on the back and using the unaffected arm to lift the affected arm overhead until a gentle stretch is felt, holding 15 seconds.
  - **Crossover arm stretch**: gently pulling one arm across the chest just below the chin, holding 30 seconds.

### Surgical Treatment
If symptoms are not relieved by therapy and other conservative methods, surgery may be discussed, typically offered during the "frozen" stage. The goal is to stretch and release the stiffened joint capsule.

- **Manipulation under anesthesia**: The patient is put to sleep and the doctor forces the shoulder to move, causing the capsule and scar tissue to stretch or tear, releasing tightness and increasing range of motion.
- **Shoulder arthroscopy**: Tight portions of the joint capsule are cut using pencil-sized instruments inserted through small incisions.

Manipulation and arthroscopy are often combined for maximum results, and most patients have good outcomes.

### Recovery
Physical therapy after surgery is necessary to maintain the motion achieved, with recovery times ranging from 6 weeks to 3 months. Long-term outcomes are generally good, with most patients having reduced or no pain and improved range of motion, though in some cases some stiffness remains even after several years — diabetic patients especially often retain some stiffness. Frozen shoulder can recur, especially if a contributing factor like diabetes is still present, though this is uncommon.
