# Shin Splints

## Description
Shin splint pain most often occurs on the inside edge of the tibia (shinbone). Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome) is an inflammation of the muscles, tendons, and bone tissue around the tibia, with pain typically occurring along the inner border of the tibia where muscles attach to the bone.

## Cause
In general, shin splints develop when the muscle and bone tissue (periosteum) in the leg become overworked by repetitive activity. They often occur after sudden changes in physical activity, including:

- Changes in frequency, such as increasing the number of exercise days per week
- Changes in duration, such as running longer distances
- Changes in intensity, such as running on hills after training only on flat surfaces

Other contributing factors include having flat feet or abnormally rigid arches, and exercising with improper or worn-out footwear. Runners have the highest risk of developing shin splints, along with dancers and military recruits.

## Symptoms
The most common symptom is pain along the border of the tibia; mild swelling in the area may also occur. Shin splint pain may be sharp and razor-like or dull and throbbing, may occur both during and after exercise, and may be aggravated by touching the sore spot.

## Doctor Examination
After discussing symptoms and medical history, the doctor examines the lower leg. An accurate diagnosis is important, since other problems can affect healing. Additional imaging may be ordered to rule out other causes of shin pain:

- **Stress fracture**: A small crack in the tibia caused by stress and overuse; if shin splints don't respond to treatment, the doctor may check for this. A bone scan and MRI will often show stress fractures in the tibia.
- **Tendinitis**: Inflammation of the tendons that attach muscles to bone, which can be painful like shin splints, especially with a partial tendon tear; an MRI can help diagnose it.
- **Chronic exertional compartment syndrome**: An uncommon condition with symptoms like shin splints, caused by dangerous pressure buildup within the muscles brought on by exercise; pain usually goes away soon after activity stops. Diagnosis involves measuring pressure within the leg compartments before and after exercise.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
- **Rest**: Since shin splints are typically caused by overuse, standard treatment includes several weeks of rest from the causative activity, substituting lower-impact aerobic activity such as swimming, stationary biking, or elliptical training.
- **Ice**: Cold packs for 20 minutes at a time, several times a day, not applied directly to the skin.
- **Compression**: An elastic compression bandage may prevent additional swelling.
- **Flexibility exercises**: Stretching the lower leg muscles may help shins feel better.
- **Supportive shoes**: Good cushioning during daily activities helps reduce stress on the shins.
- **Orthotics**: People with flat feet or recurrent shin splints may benefit from custom-made or off-the-shelf shoe inserts that align and stabilize the foot and ankle, taking stress off the lower leg.
- **Return to exercise**: Shin splints usually resolve with rest and the treatments above. Before returning to exercise, be pain-free for at least 2 weeks, and return at reduced frequency, duration, and intensity compared to before the injury.

Guidelines for returning to exercise: warm up and stretch thoroughly beforehand; increase training slowly; if the same pain returns, stop exercising immediately, ice, and rest for a day or two; then resume training for shorter periods, at lower intensity, with more rest between workouts, increasing even more slowly than before.

## Prevention
- **Wear a proper-fitting athletic shoe**: Determine foot shape using the "wet test" (stepping out of the shower onto a surface that shows the footprint) — a flat foot shows a full footprint impression, while a high arch shows only the ball and heel. Choose shoes matching the foot pattern and designed for the specific sport.
- **Cross train**: Alternate jogging with lower-impact sports like swimming or cycling.
- **Barefoot running**: Some people report this helps resolve shin splints, and some research suggests it spreads impact stress among muscles, though there is no clear evidence it reduces injury risk overall. Any such program should start very gradually with short distances to let muscles and feet adjust; barefoot runners are at increased risk of cuts and bruises, and minimalist "toe" shoes also require a slow adjustment period.

If shin splints do not improve after rest and the treatments described above, see a doctor to determine whether something else is causing the leg pain.
