# Progressive Collapsing Foot Deformity

## Overview
Progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD), previously called adult acquired flatfoot (AAF), is a complex foot and ankle condition marked by flattening of the arch along with additional, less obvious structural changes. It is also referred to as posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD). While several interconnected factors likely contribute, the disorder typically originates from problems with the posterior tibial tendon, a key structure that helps support the arch. When this tendon becomes inflamed or torn, it loses its ability to adequately stabilize the arch, leading to flatfoot.

## Description
This condition covers a spectrum of presentations. It may initially appear as a barely noticeable change with minimal discomfort, but it can progress into a severely painful, functionally limiting, and significantly deformed flatfoot.

In the earlier stages, most people respond well to conservative treatment involving orthotic devices and supportive braces, without needing surgery.

When conservative measures are not enough, surgery can reduce pain and restore function. Surgical approaches vary widely depending on the disease stage and degree of deformity, ranging from relatively simple procedures — such as removing inflamed tissue or repairing a minor tendon tear — to complex reconstructive surgery. Most patients experience some lasting activity limitations even after surgery.

## Anatomy
Several structures work together to maintain the normal arch of the foot, including tendons (which connect muscle to bone) and ligaments (which connect bone to bone). Two structures are particularly important:

**Posterior tibial tendon (PTT).** This tendon connects the tibialis posterior calf muscle to bones along the inner arch of the foot. It runs behind the medial malleolus (the bony bump on the inside of the ankle), where it can sometimes be felt or seen. Its main jobs are to help raise and support the arch during walking. It also helps lift the toes and rotate the foot and ankle inward.

**Spring ligament.** This ligament connects the calcaneus (heel bone) to the navicular bone (a bone on the inner side of the foot) and is an important arch-supporting structure.

It is not entirely clear whether damage to these structures causes flatfoot or results from it, though PTT dysfunction is generally considered a key underlying mechanism.

Occasionally, injury to the spring ligament happens first. When PCFD develops without a preceding spring ligament injury, that ligament typically becomes torn or significantly stretched as the flatfoot progresses.

In addition to the spring ligament, smaller ligaments connecting the bones on the inner side of the foot provide extra arch support. The deltoid ligament — a large, strong ligament connecting the ankle bones to the heel and inner foot bones — becomes increasingly stretched and weakened as the flatfoot worsens, ultimately contributing to a flatfoot deformity that involves the ankle joint itself.

## Cause
There are multiple possible causes of PCFD, and the exact cause likely varies from patient to patient. Potential contributing mechanisms include:

- Midfoot arthritis or weakened ligaments can place excessive stress on the posterior tibial tendon, leading to gradual tearing
- Traumatic events such as falls can cause sudden tearing or inflammation of the PTT
- Overuse can cause tendon tearing; people who take part in demanding sports (basketball, tennis, soccer) may develop tendon tears from repetitive stress, while non-athletic individuals can develop similar problems over time, particularly when regularly wearing unsupportive shoes like sandals
- A strong genetic predisposition likely contributes to PCFD

Regardless of the initial cause:

- Ongoing inflammation and weakness of the PTT reduce its ability to support the arch during walking
- The arch begins to sag, transferring excess force to secondary arch-supporting structures, including the spring ligament
- Continued stretching and weakening of these structures requires the PTT to work even harder, creating a cycle that worsens over time
- Eventually, the tendon and ligaments can tear completely; without their support, the bones of the foot shift substantially, causing pain from bone compression and arthritic changes

The literature notes increased prevalence of PCFD in women and in people over the age of 40. Other contributing factors include excess body weight, diabetes, and high blood pressure.

## Symptoms
- Pain in the arch or along the inner side of the foot and ankle where the PTT is located; swelling may occur along the tendon if it is inflamed or torn; pain often develops before any visible structural changes
- Pain and weakness during activity; standing or walking for extended periods becomes difficult for some people; vigorous or high-impact activities like running are especially challenging; unsupportive footwear worsens symptoms; beyond pain, weakness may show up when trying to lift the toes or stand on one foot
- Outer (lateral) ankle pain; as the arch flattens further, the heel bone shifts increasingly outward relative to the ankle; enough shift allows the heel bone to press against the outer ankle bone, a process called impingement

The most common location for pain follows the path of the posterior tibial tendon, running along the back and inner side of the foot and ankle.

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
The doctor will take a full medical history and ask about your symptoms.

The exam looks for the following signs:

- **Posterior tibial tendon swelling and tenderness.** Tenderness and swelling typically localize behind and below the inner ankle bone; the arch itself is often painful.
- **Changes in foot shape.** The exam requires the patient to stand. Flattening of the arch is usually the first and most noticeable change; as the deformity progresses, additional changes appear, including the heel shifting outward from the ankle and the toes rotating outward relative to the foot and ankle. These changes become visible when viewed from behind. In a healthy foot, the heel lines up straight beneath the calf and only the fourth and fifth toes are visible from behind; a flatfoot shows the heel angled outward relative to the calf and ankle, along with more toes visible from behind — called the "too many toes" sign.
- **Reduced ability to rise on one heel.** Successfully standing on one leg and rising onto the toes requires a functioning posterior tibial tendon; inability to do this suggests tendon damage.
- **Reduced flexibility.** Doctors check how much the foot can move side to side; flexibility affects treatment planning, since rigid and flexible feet require different approaches.
- **Restricted ankle motion.** Upward movement of the ankle (dorsiflexion) is often limited in flatfoot, usually due to tight calf muscles.

### Imaging

**X-rays** provide detailed images of bone that identify arthritic changes and structural misalignments contributing to symptoms. Flatfoot deformity typically shows up on standard X-rays as long as they are taken while the patient is standing. Surgical planning uses measurements taken from X-rays to help determine the best procedure.

**CT scan.** CT functions as a three-dimensional X-ray, providing more detailed bone images that can reveal subtle changes not visible on standard X-rays, helping guide surgical planning for deformity correction. Ideally, a weight-bearing CT is used, although this specialized equipment is not always available.

**MRI** shows soft tissue (tendon, ligament) much better than X-ray or CT and can reveal inflammatory or degenerative changes; it is particularly useful when the diagnosis is uncertain.

**Ultrasound** uses high-frequency sound waves to create images of structures; it can show the posterior tibial tendon but is generally not needed to confirm a flatfoot diagnosis.

## Nonsurgical Treatment
Most symptomatic patients get satisfactory results from appropriate conservative treatment. Pain relief can take more than three months even with early treatment. Longstanding symptoms often require six additional months of treatment before substantial improvement occurs.

### Rest
- Reducing or eliminating activities that make pain worse is the foundation of treatment
- Low-impact exercise substitutes such as cycling, elliptical machines, and swimming place little stress on the foot, allowing most people to stay active comfortably
- Losing weight reduces stress on the arch and its supporting structures

### Ice
Applying a cold pack to the area of the posterior tibial tendon with the most pain for 20-minute intervals, three to four times a day, reduces swelling. Ice should never touch the skin directly. Applying cold directly over the tendon after exercise reduces surrounding inflammation.

### NSAIDs
Anti-inflammatory medications such as ibuprofen or naproxen reduce pain and inflammation and are available over the counter and by prescription (such as meloxicam). Topical anti-inflammatory preparations applied directly to the affected area may provide additional benefit.

Important: while these medications effectively reduce tendon and arthritic inflammation and pain, they do not address the underlying problem or change the shape of the foot.

Discussing potential side effects — particularly cardiovascular, kidney, or gastrointestinal risks — with a doctor is worthwhile before starting these medications.

### Orthotic Devices
Orthotics are shoe inserts designed to support and position the foot, making walking more comfortable, and are the most common conservative treatment for flatfoot.

- Off-the-shelf orthotics are enough for people with mild changes in foot shape; many options on the market target arch support and flatfoot-related pain relief; a doctor's recommendation on specific over-the-counter products can be helpful
- Custom-made orthotics may be needed for moderate-to-severe structural changes; while more expensive, custom versions allow for more precise foot positioning

### Bracing
- Simple lace-up ankle braces available commercially may help mild-to-moderate flatfoot by supporting the ankle and back of the foot, reducing stress on the tendon
- More advanced flatfoot involving stiffness or arthritis may require larger bracing systems (Arizona braces, Richie braces) that can be customized and often let people with significant deformities avoid surgery

### Immobilization
A short leg cast or protective walking boot may be used for six to eight weeks, allowing the tendon to rest and inflammation to settle. Casting causes additional weakening of lower-leg muscles, so it is generally used only when other conservative measures have failed.

Doctors may recommend a period of immobilization in a boot or cast followed by bracing once acute pain and swelling have decreased.

### Physical Therapy
Programs that strengthen the tendon can benefit people with mild posterior tibial tendon disease. Stretching the calf and Achilles tendon to address ankle tightness is an essential part of any flatfoot treatment program.

### Steroid Injection
Cortisone, a powerful anti-inflammatory, may be injected into painful areas. Although widely used for arthritis in other joints throughout the body, cortisone injection into the posterior tibial tendon carries a risk of rupture and is therefore not common; discussing this risk with your doctor is important before pursuing an injection.

## Surgical Treatment
Surgery may be considered when pain persists despite months of appropriate nonsurgical treatment. The choice of procedure depends on how severe the deformity is, whether arthritis is present, and how much motion remains in the foot.

Reconstructive surgery can be quite complex; there are many procedures, and combining several is often necessary to address the different components of PCFD:

### Tenosynovectomy (Cleaning the Posterior Tibial Tendon)
Used for less advanced disease — with tendon pain and swelling but without major changes in foot shape. Surgically, the doctor removes inflamed tissue surrounding the tendon and repairs any minor tears present at the same time. This procedure can be done alone or alongside other procedures; the main risk is continued tendon deterioration and recurring symptoms.

### Gastrocnemius Recession or Achilles Lengthening
These procedures lengthen the calf muscle or Achilles tendon, benefiting people with tightness and limited upward ankle motion. They help prevent the flatfoot from recurring but may cause weakness when pushing off the foot or climbing stairs. Small incisions behind the calf allow the procedure to be performed; complication rates are low but include nerve damage and muscle weakness. These operations are frequently combined with other flatfoot correction procedures.

### Tendon Transfer
For flexible flatfoot, tendon transfer reconstructs the function of a damaged posterior tibial tendon. This involves either removing the diseased tendon and replacing it with another foot tendon, or — when PTT disease is less severe — attaching a transferred tendon to the remaining tendon. Commonly transferred tendons include the flexor digitorum longus (which flexes the smaller toes) and occasionally the flexor hallucis longus (which flexes the big toe); toe motion continues after transfer and gait changes typically go unnoticed.

Although a transferred tendon functionally replaces the damaged PTT, the shape of the foot is not fully normalized. Additional procedures to correct foot shape are usually needed; without correcting the shape, ongoing stress leads to re-tearing.

### Ligament Repair
Arch-supporting ligaments often become torn or, at minimum, stretched and weakened. In flexible arch presentations, the surgeon may repair a ligament (particularly the spring ligament) as part of the surgery.

- If the ligament is substantially healthy, it may be repaired using native tissue alone
- Most cases require additional reinforcing material to reconstruct the ligament, including strong sutures

Similar to tendon transfer, ligament repair typically has to be combined with other procedures because continued stretching and tearing recur without correcting the shape of the foot.

### Osteotomy (Reshaping the Bone)
Osteotomies reshape the bones of a flexible flatfoot, creating a more normal arch. Shape correction is important because it reduces the ongoing stress on the posterior tibial tendon, spring ligament, and other arch-supporting structures.

- A common calcaneal osteotomy involves cutting the heel bone and repositioning it from its outward position back beneath the center of the ankle
- Other calcaneal osteotomy techniques involve lengthening the outer side of the heel to help centralize the toes and correct the "too many toes" deformity
- Additional osteotomies of the foot and toe bones may be necessary to fully restore the arch shape

Some osteotomies require bone grafting material to fill gaps and help the bone heal; the bone fragments are held in place with screws or metal plates.

### Arthrodesis (Bone Fusion)
Stiff flatfoot or significant arthritic changes in key foot joints call for a different approach, since the foot is not flexible enough for successful osteotomy and tendon-transfer methods.

Joint fusion realigns the foot's structure and eliminates arthritis. The cartilage is removed and the bone fragments are fixed in a corrected position; the bones eventually fuse into a single continuous structure, eliminating joint-related pain. Screws and metal plates hold the fragments in place while they fuse.

A rigid flatfoot may require fusing the heel bone and ankle bone in a realigned position; restoring the arch may also require fusing bones in the middle of the foot. Although fusion eliminates motion between the fused bones, people who need this surgery typically already have a rigid deformity with little remaining motion; walking and pain — particularly outer ankle pain from arthritis and impingement — should improve substantially. Motion above and below the ankle typically shows minimal impairment.

With any fusion, complete healing of the bone sometimes fails (nonunion), which may require repeat surgery.

### Ankle-Specific Flatfoot Surgery
Severe, advanced flatfoot occasionally progresses to involve the ankle joint, causing instability or arthritis.

- Simple ankle joint instability without arthritis may prompt repair of the deltoid ligament (the main ligament on the inner side of the ankle)
- Arthritic or stiff ankle joints may need more extensive procedures, including ankle replacement or fusion, to address both deformity and pain

### Complication Considerations
- Incomplete pain relief is the most common post-operative complaint; some people are unsatisfied with the appearance of the foot after surgery, expecting it to look completely "normal." It's important to understand that surgery often focuses on preventing worsening deformity and pain, rather than eliminating all pain and restoring a perfect appearance; discussing realistic expectations with the surgeon beforehand is valuable
- Nonunion (failure of the bone to fuse) can complicate both osteotomies and fusions, sometimes requiring additional procedures to achieve bone healing
- Infection at the surgical site is another possible complication

People with diabetes and those who use tobacco have a higher risk of complications.

### Surgical Outcomes
Most people achieve satisfactory results from surgery; motion capacity and flatfoot severity before surgery are important factors in the outcome — greater severity correlates with a longer recovery and a lower likelihood of returning to sports. Most people need at least six months for healing and substantial pain improvement.

Getting the best surgical outcome requires:

- Following the surgeon's instructions after surgery (critically, avoiding weight-bearing until cleared)
- Reducing stress on the foot (weight loss, avoiding high-impact activity)
- Using supportive footwear
- Reducing risk factors for complications (quitting smoking, managing diabetes)
