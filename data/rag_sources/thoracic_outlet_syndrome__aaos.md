# Thoracic Outlet Syndrome

## Overview
Thoracic outlet syndrome represents a term used for several rare and different conditions that can occur when nerves and/or blood vessels in the thoracic outlet are compressed (squeezed) or irritated.

The thoracic outlet is the anatomical space between the collarbone and first rib. When shoulder and chest muscles lack adequate strength to stabilize the collarbone, it may slip downward and forward, compressing underlying nerves and blood vessels.

## Cause
Thoracic outlet syndrome develops from:
- Injury
- Disease
- Congenital abnormalities (such as an abnormal first rib)

The condition occurs more frequently in women than men. Poor posture and excess weight can exacerbate symptoms. Psychological changes often accompany this syndrome, though causation remains unclear.

## Symptoms
Symptom presentation depends on which nerves or blood vessels experience compression. Nerve compression symptoms are more prevalent and easier to diagnose than vascular compression.

**Nerve Pressure Effects:**
- Vague, aching pain in neck, shoulder, arm, or hand
- Pain, numbness, or tingling inside the forearm and fourth/fifth fingers
- Weakness causing hand clumsiness

**Blood Vessel Pressure Effects:**
- Reduced outflow: arm swelling, redness, discoloration
- Reduced inflow: cool sensation, easy fatigue

Overhead activities worsen compression. Range of motion may become limited.

## Doctor Examination

### Medical History and Physical Examination
Physicians will:
- Review symptom history thoroughly
- Conduct comprehensive physical examination
- Attempt reproducing symptoms through positional examination

### Diagnostic Tests
**Elevated Arm Stress Test:** Patients raise arms overhead and open/close fists for approximately three minutes. Symptom reproduction suggests thoracic outlet syndrome, though false positives occur.

**Imaging Tests:**
- X-rays
- Computed tomography (CT) scans
- Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans
- Ultrasound

**Additional Testing:** Physicians may order blood circulation tests and nerve conduction tests to confirm diagnosis, ruling out similar conditions like cervical nerve compression.

Many institutions employ multidisciplinary teams including orthopaedic surgeons, physiatrists, physical therapists, peripheral nerve surgeons, and vascular surgeons.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
**Physical Therapy:** Exercises strengthen shoulder muscles supporting the collarbone. Postural exercises reduce nerve and vessel pressure by improving posture alignment.

**Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs):** Medications like naproxen and ibuprofen can ease the pain and reduce swelling.

**Weight Loss:** Overweight individuals should consider weight loss programs, as excess weight stresses supportive shoulder musculature.

**Lifestyle Modifications:**
- Alter workstation configuration
- Avoid strenuous activities
- Adjust daily routines that aggravate symptoms

### Surgical Treatment
When conservative approaches prove ineffective, surgery may involve:
- Removing abnormal rib portions
- Releasing muscles connecting neck and chest
- Combining both procedures

## Prevention
Avoid carrying heavy shoulder bags, as this depresses the collarbone and increases thoracic outlet pressure.

Perform these exercises 10 repetitions twice daily:
- **Corner Stretch:** Stand approximately one foot from a corner, hands at shoulder height on walls. Lean forward gently until feeling chest stretch. Hold five seconds.
- **Neck Stretch:** Place left hand on head, right hand behind back. Pull head toward left shoulder until feeling neck stretch. Hold five seconds. Repeat opposite direction.
- **Shoulder Rolls:** Shrug shoulders upward, backward, downward in circular motion while maintaining upright posture.
- **Neck Retraction:** Pull head straight backward while keeping jaw level. Hold five seconds.

Discontinue any exercise causing pain.
