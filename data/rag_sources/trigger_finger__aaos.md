# Trigger Finger

## Description
Trigger finger, also called stenosing tenosynovitis, is a condition affecting the tendons in the fingers or thumbs that prevents them from bending normally. It causes a sensation of catching, locking, or "triggering" of the finger or thumb when trying to bend or straighten it, along with pain and stiffness. It affects approximately 2% of the general population, most commonly the ring finger and thumb, though any finger may be involved. When it affects the thumb it is called "trigger thumb."

## Anatomy
The flexor tendons are cord-like structures connecting the forearm muscles to the finger and thumb bones. When bending the fingers, these tendons on the palm side pull the fingers into a fist. Each tendon passes through tunnel-like structures called pulleys that keep the tendon close to the bone, similar to guides on a fishing rod. The A1 pulley, located in the palm at the base of each finger, is the pulley most involved in trigger finger. The tendon sheath attaches to the finger and thumb bones, keeping the flexor tendon in place during movement.

## What Happens
Trigger finger involves a size mismatch between the finger tendons and the A1 pulley. The tendon may thicken or develop a bump, and the A1 pulley may also thicken and narrow, leaving less room for the tendon to pass through. This prevents the tendon from gliding smoothly, causing catching or locking, often painfully — a thickened nodule on the flexor tendon pops through the A1 pulley during deep finger flexion and then catches against the pulley on attempted extension, making straightening difficult and painful. In some cases the finger locks and remains bent, sometimes requiring manual straightening with the other hand, or in worst cases professional medical help.

## Cause
Most cases have no identifiable cause, with symptoms often beginning without any change in activity. It is more common in:
- Women between the ages of 40 and 60 (rare in children, though congenital trigger thumb can cause a child's thumb to lock in flexion)
- People with medical conditions including diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and amyloidosis
- People who do repetitive gripping or pinching activities for work or recreation
- People with certain differences in tendon structure

## Symptoms
Symptoms typically start slowly and get worse over time, and may include:
- A tender lump at the base of the finger or thumb on the palm side
- A catching, popping, or locking sensation with movement
- Pain below the finger when bending or straightening, which may travel up the finger or down into the palm
- The finger getting stuck in a bent position

Stiffness and locking tend to be worse after periods of inactivity, such as first thing in the morning, and symptoms may improve throughout the day with gentle, consistent hand use.

## Diagnosis
Doctors typically diagnose trigger finger through discussion of symptoms and a hand examination; X-rays or other tests are usually unnecessary. The exam checks the affected digit for tenderness over the flexor tendon sheath in the palm, thickening or swelling of the tendon sheath at the base of the finger or thumb, and a clicking or catching sensation with bending and straightening.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
The goal is to reduce pain and help the tendon glide more freely. Most cases respond well to nonsurgical treatment.

- **Activity modification**: Resting the hand and avoiding repetitive gripping or pinching.
- **Splinting**: A night splint keeping the affected finger or thumb straight during sleep.
- **Anti-inflammatory medications**: NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or naproxen for pain and inflammation, or acetaminophen for those who cannot take NSAIDs.
- **Exercises**: Gentle stretching to decrease stiffness and improve range of motion.
- **Steroid injections**: A corticosteroid injected into the base of the affected finger is another first-line option and often resolves the condition; up to three injections may be given if the first does not help or symptoms return, and surgery is often recommended if injections don't help. Steroid injections are less likely to work in diabetics, people with longstanding triggering, or trigger thumb, and typically raise blood sugar for about 10-14 days in insulin-dependent diabetics, requiring close monitoring.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery may be needed if symptoms are severe or don't improve with other treatments, or if the finger is stuck in a bent position and cannot be straightened.

- **Trigger finger release**: An outpatient procedure done the same day under local numbing (with the patient awake or lightly sedated). The doctor makes a small cut in the palm, or uses a needle tip, to open the A1 pulley and give the tendon more room to move. Opening the A1 pulley does not harm hand function since the other pulleys remain intact. Occasionally part of the tendon is removed if opening the pulley alone doesn't resolve triggering, more common in patients with longstanding symptoms.
- **Complications**: Rare, but may include infection, stiffness, or return of symptoms; some pain and swelling after surgery is normal and should improve.
- **Recovery**: Most patients begin moving the finger immediately after surgery. Keeping the hand raised above the heart helps reduce pain and swelling. The incision usually heals within weeks, though swelling and stiffness may take 4 to 6 months to fully resolve, with full recovery averaging about 6 months. Hand therapy may be recommended if stiffness, swelling, or pain persist.

## Outcomes
Most people with trigger finger improve with appropriate treatment, whether nonsurgical or surgical. Early intervention and following the treatment plan are key to successful recovery.
