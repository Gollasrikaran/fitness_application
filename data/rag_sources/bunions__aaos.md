# Bunions (Hallux Valgus)

## Overview
A bunion is a painful bony bump that forms on the inside of the foot at the base of the big toe. It is associated with hallux valgus, a progressive deformity in which the big toe gradually leans toward the smaller toes while the foot's outer border shifts outward. Pain comes from two sources: friction between the bony bump and footwear, and mechanical changes that alter pressure across the forefoot, which can cause secondary pain in the neighboring toes.

The deformity develops gradually over time. Pressure on the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint initially causes the big toe to drift inward. Over time, the bones, tendons, and ligaments rearrange, producing the characteristic deformity. It often progressively worsens, eventually making it difficult to wear shoes comfortably or walk without discomfort.

Bunions are more common in women than men, and most cases develop during adulthood rather than childhood. In many cases, conservative management — wearing properly sized shoes with a roomy toe box and taking steps to reduce pressure — relieves symptoms. If conservative treatment does not help after a reasonable trial, surgery may be considered to correct both the bump and the underlying hallux valgus deformity.

## Anatomy
The big toe has two joints. The main one, called the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, is where the first metatarsal bone meets the base of the big toe's proximal phalanx. Bunions originate specifically at this MTP joint.

## Description
A bunion forms when the bones of the MTP joint become misaligned: the first metatarsal shifts toward the inner border of the foot, the big toe's bones angle toward the second toe, and the MTP joint enlarges and protrudes on the inner side of the forefoot.

The enlarged joint often becomes inflamed through a combination of abnormal joint mechanics and direct friction from shoes. The name "bunion" comes from a Greek word meaning "turnip," since the bump on the side of the foot commonly appears red and swollen, similar to the vegetable.

### Bunion Progression
Bunions typically start small, but they tend to enlarge over time. Because the MTP joint bends with every step, an enlarging bunion causes progressively more discomfort and difficulty walking.

Severe cases can produce notable cosmetic changes, including the big toe angling completely under or over the second toe. Pressure from the big toe may push the second toe out of its normal position, sometimes causing it to contact the third toe and develop a hammer toe deformity. Friction between the toes can create calluses, adding to the discomfort and difficulty walking.

### Related Foot Complications
An enlarged MTP joint can lead to bursitis — inflammation of the fluid-filled sac that normally cushions the joint. Ongoing pain and arthritic changes can develop if the smooth cartilage covering the joint is damaged by abnormal joint motion.

### Adolescent Bunion
In addition to the typical adult presentation, a variation called "adolescent bunion" mainly affects girls between the ages of 10 and 15. Unlike the adult form, the big toe usually retains its normal motion, though pain may still make wearing shoes difficult.

### Bunionette
A related deformity called a "bunionette" or "tailor's bunion" develops on the outer side of the foot, near the base of the little toe. Though it occurs on the opposite side, it shares the same underlying mechanics as a standard bunion and can also produce a painful bursitis and hard corns or calluses over the bony bump.

## Cause
Several factors contribute to bunion formation:

- **Ill-fitting shoes** — particularly footwear with narrow, pointed toe boxes that force the toes into an abnormal position
- **Inherited foot structure** — some people inherit foot shapes and structural traits that predispose them to bunions; roughly 70% of people with bunions have a family history, suggesting a strong hereditary component, especially in cases that develop early in adolescence
- **Inflammatory conditions**, such as rheumatoid arthritis
- **Neuromuscular disorders**, including Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease or effects of polio

## Symptoms
Beyond the visible bump on the inner side of the foot, symptoms may include:

- Pain and tenderness
- Redness and swelling
- Thickening of skin on the sole of the foot
- Corns or calluses developing over the bump
- Reduced motion or stiffness of the big toe, which can contribute to walking difficulty

## Doctor Examination

### Physical Examination
Doctors take a detailed medical history regarding overall health and symptoms, then carefully examine the foot. In most cases, symptoms and appearance alone are enough to identify a bunion, but X-rays are still typically obtained to confirm the diagnosis.

### X-rays
X-rays show the underlying bone structure, allowing doctors to:

- Evaluate toe alignment and identify any damage to the MTP joint. Because bone alignment shifts between weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing positions, standing X-rays give the most accurate picture of any misalignment
- Determine whether arthritis has developed alongside the bunion
- Establish how severe the deformity is and decide on the best corrective approach

## Nonsurgical Treatment
Many bunions can be managed with conservative treatment. A bunion that is not painful does not need treatment at all. Nonsurgical care cannot reverse an established deformity, but it can meaningfully reduce pain and help prevent it from getting worse.

### Footwear Changes
Pain often improves simply by switching to properly fitted shoes with a roomy toe box that does not compress the toes. Some shoes can be stretched at specific points to relieve pressure over the bunion.

Doctors can offer guidance on appropriate shoe selection and fit for individual needs.

### Padding
Silicone-based cushioning pads worn inside the shoe can reduce pain over the bunion. These are available at pharmacies and online.

It's worth trying a pad for a short period before committing to it for extended use, since the added bulk can occasionally increase pressure and worsen rather than relieve symptoms.

### Orthotics and Other Devices
Pressure can also be reduced with:

- Over-the-counter or custom-made shoe inserts (orthotics)
- Spacers placed between the toes
- Night splints that hold the big toe in a straighter position, which may ease discomfort, although there is no proven lasting corrective effect

### Medications
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, including ibuprofen and naproxen, address pain and inflammatory swelling. Patients whose bunion is complicated by arthritis may need additional medication for symptom management.

## Tips for Proper Shoe Fit
People with bunion symptoms should prioritize proper footwear based on the following general principles:

- Look for shoes with a wide midfoot, a roomy toe box, a supportive sole, and good structural support
- Avoid sharply pointed styles, overly tight shoes, shoes that are too short, and heels higher than about two inches, since these concentrate pressure on the front of the foot

Specific fitting strategies include:

- Ignore the marked size, since sizing varies between brands and styles; go by actual fit
- Choose the shoe that most closely matches the shape of your foot
- Have your feet measured periodically, since foot size changes with age
- Measure both feet, since most people have some asymmetry; fit shoes to the larger foot
- Shop for shoes later in the day, when your feet are largest
- Stand while being fitted and make sure there is 3/8" to 1/2" of space beyond your longest toe
- Make sure the widest part of your foot lines up with the widest part of the shoe
- Never buy uncomfortable shoes expecting them to stretch
- Check that your heel fits snugly with minimal slipping
- Walk around in the shoes before purchasing, on different surfaces, to confirm comfort

## When To Consider Bunion Surgery
Surgery is generally considered when an adequate trial of nonsurgical treatment fails to relieve pain and walking difficulty. The goals of surgery are to realign the bones, tendons, ligaments, and nerves so the big toe returns to a normal position and pain is resolved. Surgery should only be done to treat a painful bunion.

Several surgical approaches are used to correct adult bunions:

- **Open bunion surgery.** The traditional technique involves an incision over the bump, removal of the bony prominence, and realignment of the bone through an osteotomy. Screws or metal plates hold the bone in place while it heals.

- **Minimally invasive surgery (MIS).** An increasingly popular alternative that uses smaller incisions and specialized instruments. As with the traditional approach, the surgeon removes the bony bump and performs an osteotomy to realign the bone, then secures the correction with one or two screws. Some variations use different fixation methods.

MIS approaches may cause less early swelling and allow for a somewhat faster initial recovery, though long-term outcomes are similar to traditional surgery. Whether MIS is appropriate depends on the specific characteristics of the bunion. Surgical recommendations are individualized based on X-rays and physical examination.

Bunion surgery is typically done as an outpatient (same-day) procedure without a hospital stay, though full recovery takes a much longer period.

Surgery for adolescent bunions is generally discouraged, and reserved only for cases with intolerable pain that has not responded to shoe changes or orthotics.

If an adolescent does undergo surgery, particularly before the growth plates have closed, the risk of the bunion recurring is substantially higher. For this reason, most doctors delay surgery until growth is essentially complete, to minimize the chance of recurrence.
