# Rotator Cuff Tears

## Anatomy
The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint made up of the humerus (upper arm bone), scapula (shoulder blade), and clavicle (collarbone). The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles that come together as tendons to form a covering around the head of the humerus, attaching it to the shoulder blade and helping lift and rotate the arm. A lubricating sac called a bursa sits between the rotator cuff and the acromion (the bone on top of the shoulder), allowing the tendons to glide freely; this bursa can also become inflamed and painful when the rotator cuff is injured.

## Description
When one or more rotator cuff tendons is torn, it becomes partially or completely detached from the head of the humerus — most tears occur in the supraspinatus tendon, but other rotator cuff tendons may be involved. Tendons often begin by fraying, and as damage progresses can completely tear, sometimes from lifting a heavy object.

- **Partial tear**: The tendon is not completely detached — the tear goes only partially through the tendon's thickness, but it is thinner than normal.
- **Full-thickness tear**: Part or all of the tendon is detached from the bone. A **full-thickness incomplete tear** detaches only a small part; a **full-thickness complete tear** detaches the tendon completely, leaving essentially a hole in the tendon.

## Cause
There are two main causes: injury (acute) and wear (degeneration).

- **Injury-related (acute) tear**: Falling on an outstretched arm or lifting something too heavy with a jerking motion can tear the rotator cuff, sometimes alongside a broken collarbone, dislocated shoulder, or wrist fracture.
- **Wear-related (degenerative) tear**: Most tears result from gradual wearing down of the tendon that occurs naturally with age and is often relatively painless. Degenerative tears are more common in the dominant arm, and a tear in one shoulder raises the likelihood of a tear in the opposite shoulder, even without pain there.

Contributing factors to degenerative tears:
- **Repetitive stress**: Repeating the same shoulder motions (baseball, tennis, rowing, weightlifting, many jobs and chores) stresses the muscles and tendons.
- **Lack of blood supply**: Blood supply to the rotator cuff tendons decreases with age, impairing the body's natural ability to repair tendon damage.

People over 40 are at greater risk given normal age-related wear and tear. People who do repetitive lifting or overhead work — such as tennis players, baseball pitchers, painters, and carpenters — are also at risk. Most tears in younger people are caused by traumatic injury such as a fall, rather than overuse.

## Symptoms
Common symptoms include:
- Pain at rest and at night, especially lying on the affected shoulder
- Pain when lifting and lowering the arm or with specific movements
- Weakness when lifting or rotating the arm
- Crepitus (a crackling sensation) when moving the shoulder in certain positions

Tears from a sudden event like a fall usually cause intense pain, a snapping sensation, and immediate weakness. Tears that develop slowly from overuse may start as mild pain when lifting the arm overhead and progress to pain at rest that no longer responds to over-the-counter medication, with routine activities like combing hair or reaching behind the back becoming difficult. Some rotator cuff tears are not painful at all but still cause weakness and other symptoms.

## Doctor Examination
The doctor checks for tenderness, measures range of motion, tests arm strength in different positions, checks for other shoulder joint problems, and may examine the neck to rule out a pinched nerve or arthritis.

Imaging tests:
- **X-rays**: Usually normal for rotator cuff pain since X-rays don't show soft tissue, but done to rule out other causes such as arthritis, and may reveal a longstanding degenerative tear.
- **MRI or ultrasound**: Show the soft tissues, including the location and size of a tear. MRI can also indicate how old a tear is based on the quality of the rotator cuff muscles. Ultrasound accuracy depends on the skill of the technician.

## Treatment
The goal of treatment is to reduce pain and restore function. Continuing to use the shoulder despite worsening pain can cause further damage and the tear can get larger over time, so early treatment is recommended. Treatment planning considers age, activity level, general health, and the type of tear.

### Nonsurgical Treatment
In about 80 to 85% of patients, nonsurgical treatment relieves pain and improves function.

- **Rest** and **activity modification**: Limiting overhead activities and avoiding motions that cause pain.
- **Strengthening exercises and physical therapy**: Restore movement, improve flexibility and range of motion, and strengthen supporting muscles.
- **Steroid injection**: A local anesthetic combined with cortisone may help if rest, medication, and physical therapy don't relieve pain; on average, injections relieve pain in about two-thirds of patients for at least 3 months, though repeated long-term injections carry risks.

The main advantage of nonsurgical treatment is avoiding major surgical risks (infection, permanent stiffness, anesthesia complications, lengthy recovery, re-tear risk). The disadvantages are that the tear may increase in size over time and activities may need to be limited.

### Surgical Treatment
Surgery may be recommended if pain does not improve with nonsurgical methods, or if the patient is very active or uses the arms for overhead work or sports. Other indicators for surgery include symptoms lasting 6 to 12 months, a large tear (more than 3 cm) with good-quality surrounding tissue, significant weakness and loss of function, or a tear caused by a recent acute injury. Surgery most often involves re-attaching the torn tendon to the head of the humerus; the surgeon will discuss the best procedure for the individual's needs.
