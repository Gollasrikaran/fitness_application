# NICE Guideline NG65: Spondyloarthritis in Over 16s - Diagnosis and Management (Recommendations)

Published: 28 February 2017. Last updated: 2 June 2017 (with further amendments to pharmacological recommendations in 2023 and 2025). Note: this guideline covers spondyloarthritis broadly, including axial spondyloarthritis (ankylosing spondylitis and non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis) and peripheral spondyloarthritis (psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, enteropathic spondyloarthritis). Ankylosing spondylitis is defined within this guideline as "radiographic axial spondyloarthritis."

## 1.1 Recognition and Referral in Non-Specialist Care Settings

"Do not rule out the possibility that a person has spondyloarthritis solely on the presence or absence of any individual sign, symptom or test result." Signs and symptoms may be musculoskeletal (inflammatory back pain, enthesitis, dactylitis) or extra-articular (uveitis, psoriasis). Be aware that axial spondyloarthritis affects a similar number of women as men, can occur in people who are HLA-B27 negative, and may be present despite no evidence of sacroiliitis on plain film X-ray.

**Referral for suspected axial spondyloarthritis**: If a person has low back pain that started before age 45 and has lasted longer than 3 months, refer to a rheumatologist for spondyloarthritis assessment if 4 or more additional criteria are present (onset before age 35, waking during the second half of the night because of symptoms, buttock pain, improvement with movement, improvement within 48 hours of NSAIDs, a first-degree relative with spondyloarthritis, current or past arthritis, current or past enthesitis, current or past psoriasis). If exactly 3 additional criteria are present, perform an HLA-B27 test and refer if positive.

**Referral for suspected acute anterior uveitis**: Refer people for an immediate (same-day) ophthalmological assessment if they have symptoms of acute anterior uveitis (eye pain, redness, light sensitivity, blurred vision).

## 1.2 Diagnosing Spondyloarthritis in Specialist Care Settings

"Do not rule out a diagnosis of spondyloarthritis solely on the basis of a negative HLA-B27 result," and do not rule it out if CRP and ESR are normal.

**Imaging for suspected axial spondyloarthritis**: Offer plain film X-ray of the sacroiliac joints, unless the person is likely to have an immature skeleton. "Diagnose ankylosing spondylitis if the plain film X-ray shows sacroiliitis meeting the modified New York criteria (bilateral grade 2 to 4 or unilateral grade 3 to 4 sacroiliitis)." If the X-ray does not show this, request unenhanced MRI using an inflammatory back pain protocol; diagnose non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis if the MRI meets ASAS/OMERACT MRI criteria. Do not offer scintigraphy for suspected axial spondyloarthritis.

## 1.3 Information and Support

Provide people with spondyloarthritis and their families/carers with information that is ongoing, relevant to the stage of their condition, and tailored to their needs, covering diagnosis and prognosis, treatment options, self-help, employment rights, and local support groups. Advise about the possibility of flare episodes and extra-articular symptoms, and consider developing a tailored flare management plan covering access to care, self-care (exercises, stretching, joint protection), pain and fatigue management, potential medicine changes, and managing impact on daily life and work.

## 1.4 Pharmacological Management of Spondyloarthritis

**Axial spondyloarthritis**: Offer an NSAID at the lowest effective dose for pain associated with axial spondyloarthritis, with gastroprotective treatment considered; if maximum tolerated dose for 2 to 4 weeks does not provide adequate relief, consider switching to another NSAID. For active ankylosing spondylitis with a BASDAI score of 4 or more and spinal VAS of 4cm or more not controlled by NSAIDs, see NICE technology appraisal guidance on medicines such as secukinumab (TA407), adalimumab (TA383), certolizumab pegol (TA383), etanercept (TA383), infliximab (TA383), and (if TNF-alpha inhibitors are unsuitable) tofacitinib (TA920), bimekizumab (TA918), upadacitinib (TA829), and ixekizumab (TA718).

**Psoriatic arthritis and other peripheral spondyloarthritides**: Consider local corticosteroid injections as monotherapy for non-progressive monoarthritis. Offer standard DMARDs for peripheral polyarthritis, oligoarthritis, or persistent/progressive monoarthritis. Consider NSAIDs as an adjunct to DMARDs at the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time.

**Reactive arthritis**: "Do not offer long-term (4 weeks or longer) treatment with antibiotics solely to manage reactive arthritis caused by a gastrointestinal or genitourinary infection," after treating the initial infection.

## 1.5 Non-Pharmacological Management of Spondyloarthritis

"Refer people with axial spondyloarthritis to a specialist physiotherapist to start an individualised, structured exercise programme," which should include stretching, strengthening and postural exercises, deep breathing, spinal extension, range of motion exercises for the lumbar, thoracic and cervical spine, and aerobic exercise. Consider hydrotherapy as an adjunctive therapy to manage pain and maintain or improve function. Consider referral to a specialist therapist (physiotherapist, occupational therapist, hand therapist, orthotist or podiatrist) for people who have difficulties with everyday activities, to assess needs, advise on physical aids, and arrange periodic reviews.

## 1.6 Surgery for Spondyloarthritis

Do not refer people with axial spondyloarthritis for spinal deformity correction assessment unless the deformity is significantly affecting quality of life and is severe or progressing despite optimal non-surgical management (including physiotherapy). If a suspected spinal fracture presents, refer to a specialist to confirm the fracture and carry out a stability assessment.

## 1.7 Managing Flares

Manage flares in either specialist or primary care depending on the person's needs; seek advice from specialist care as needed, particularly for people with recurrent or persistent flares, those on biological or targeted synthetic DMARDs, or those with comorbidities affecting treatment. Be aware that uveitis can occur during flare episodes.

## 1.8 Long-Term Complications

Take into account the adverse effects associated with NSAIDs and DMARDs when monitoring in primary care. Advise people that there may be a greater risk of skin cancer with TNF-alpha inhibitors, and discuss cardiovascular comorbidity risk factors with all people with spondyloarthritis. "Consider regular osteoporosis assessments (every 2 years) for people with axial spondyloarthritis," noting that spinal DEXA bone mineral density measures may be elevated due to syndesmophytes and ligamentous calcification, so hip measurements may be more reliable. Advise people with axial spondyloarthritis that they may be prone to fractures and should consult a healthcare professional following falls or physical trauma, particularly with increased musculoskeletal pain.

## 1.9 Organisation of Care

Commissioners should ensure local arrangements coordinate care for prescribing and monitoring NSAIDs and DMARDs, managing flares, and ensuring prompt access to specialist rheumatology and other specialist services. Ensure effective communication and coordination between rheumatology and other relevant specialities (dermatology, gastroenterology, ophthalmology), particularly for people already receiving or needing to start DMARDs for another condition.
