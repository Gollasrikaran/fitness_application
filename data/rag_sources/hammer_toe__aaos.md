# Hammer Toe

## Overview
A hammer toe is a deformity of the second, third, or fourth toe, in which the toe is bent at the middle joint, giving it a hammer-like appearance. In its early stages, a hammer toe is flexible and can often be corrected with simple measures, but if left untreated it can become fixed and may require surgery.

## Anatomy
The forefoot has five toes, and each has three joints (the big toe usually has two). In hammer toe, the affected toe bends at the middle joint, known as the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint.

## Cause
Hammer toe results from a muscle imbalance that puts pressure on the toe's tendons and joints. Muscles work in pairs to straighten and bend the toes; when a toe is held bent for a prolonged period, its muscles and joints can tighten irreversibly.

Poorly fitting shoes commonly cause this condition. A narrow toe box forces the smaller toes into a bent position, and the resulting rubbing can produce corns and calluses. Higher heels increase the downward pressure on the foot and increase toe bending. Over time, weakened small foot muscles create a permanent imbalance.

## Symptoms
Hammer toe causes pain during movement and when wearing shoes. Additional symptoms include:

- Swelling or redness
- Inability to straighten the toe
- Difficulty walking
- A corn or callus on top of the middle joint or at the tip of the toe

## Doctor Examination
**Physical examination.** Doctors determine whether the toe joint is flexible or rigid, which guides treatment selection.

**Tests.** X-rays confirm the diagnosis by showing the underlying bone structure. Patients with diabetes or decreased sensation in the foot may need additional testing to identify a neurological cause of the tendon imbalance.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Early-stage, flexible hammer toe typically responds to simple interventions.

**Footwear changes.** Avoid tight, narrow, high-heeled shoes. Proper shoes should be about one-half inch longer than your longest toe and have a soft, roomy toe box. Deep-toe-box shoes or a stretched shoe repair may help accommodate the deformity.

**Exercises.** Stretching and strengthening exercises help restore muscle balance, including manually stretching the toe, picking up small objects with the toes, and "towel curls" (scrunching a towel under the foot using the toes).

**Over-the-counter remedies.** Commercial straps, cushions, and nonmedicated corn pads can provide pain relief. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or numbness in the feet should consult their doctor before self-treating.

### Surgical Treatment
Rigid joints that do not respond to nonsurgical treatment may require surgery, typically performed as an outpatient procedure with local anesthesia.

**Tendon lengthening.** For flexible toe joints, lengthening a tightened tendon corrects the joint imbalance.

**Tendon transfer.** Some flexible-joint cases benefit from transferring a tendon from the bottom of the toe to the top to restore a straighter position.

**Arthrodesis (joint fusion).** For rigid joints, the doctor removes a small portion of bone from the PIP joint and inserts external wires/pins or an internal plate to hold the bones together while they fuse.

## Recovery
Stiffness, swelling, and redness may persist for 4 to 6 weeks after surgery. Weight-bearing is possible right away, but activity should be limited and the foot elevated to aid healing. The fused toe may be slightly longer or shorter than before and will no longer bend at the fused joint.
