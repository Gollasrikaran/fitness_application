# Osteoarthritis of the Elbow

## Overview
Osteoarthritis of the elbow develops when the cartilage surface deteriorates or sustains damage. This commonly results from previous injuries such as elbow dislocation or fracture, though it frequently occurs through normal age-related joint wear from years of activity. While osteoarthritis typically affects weight-bearing joints like hips and knees, the elbow remains relatively resistant due to its well-matched joint surfaces and strong stabilizing ligaments.

## Anatomy
The elbow functions as a hinge joint comprising three bones:
- The humerus (upper arm bone)
- The ulna (forearm bone on the pinky finger side)
- The radius (forearm bone on the thumb side)

The bone surfaces where they meet are covered with articular cartilage, a smooth substance that protects the bones and enables them to move easily. A synovial membrane covers remaining interior surfaces, producing lubricating fluid that reduces friction during arm movement. Muscles, ligaments, and tendons stabilize the entire joint structure.

## Description
Osteoarthritis represents an age-related, wear-and-tear condition typically occurring in people over 50, though younger individuals may develop it. The protective cartilage gradually softens and wears away, causing bones to rub against one another. Over time, this friction creates stiffness and pain. The articular cartilage becomes frayed and rough, narrowing the joint space, and bone spurs may develop.

## Cause
Several factors increase osteoarthritis risk:

**Injury-Related:**
- Previous fractures involving joint surfaces
- Elbow dislocations
- Ligament damage causing joint instability
- Surgical reconstruction attempts that cannot fully restore preinjury conditions

**Activity-Related:**
Professional athletes, particularly baseball pitchers, experience excessive demands on their throwing elbows, potentially causing ligament failure and requiring surgical reconstruction. High-shear forces across the joint accelerate cartilage breakdown.

**Prevention:**
The best way to prevent elbow arthritis is to avoid injury to the joint. Proper treatment of injuries, maintaining muscular strength, correct conditioning, and appropriate technique during heavy work or sports reduce arthritis risk.

## Symptoms
Primary symptoms include:
- Pain
- Loss of range of motion
- Grating or locking sensation in the elbow

**Grating:** Results from lost smooth joint surfaces caused by cartilage damage or wear.

**Locking:** Caused by loose cartilage or bone fragments trapped between moving surfaces.

**Progressive Symptoms:**
- Joint swelling (typically appears later as disease advances)
- Numbness in ring finger and pinky finger during advanced stages

**Nerve Involvement:** The ulnar nerve, located in a tunnel behind the inner elbow side, may experience increased pressure from swelling or limited joint motion, causing tingling sensations.

## Doctor Examination
Physicians typically diagnose elbow osteoarthritis through symptom assessment and standard X-rays revealing arthritic changes. Advanced diagnostic imaging, such as a computed tomography (CT) scan or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan, is typically not needed to diagnose osteoarthritis of the elbow.

**Demographic Patterns:** Elbow osteoarthritis without previous injury occurs more frequently in men than women, typically appearing at ages 40-50 or older, though some patients experience earlier symptoms.

## Treatment

### Nonsurgical Treatment
Early-stage osteoarthritis typically responds to nonsurgical approaches including:
- Oral medications for pain reduction or alleviation
- Physical therapy
- Activity modification
- Corticosteroid injections (temporary relief while symptoms progress)

### Surgical Treatment
When nonsurgical interventions prove insufficient, surgical options become appropriate.

**Arthroscopy:** Arthroscopy, a minimally invasive surgical treatment, can improve motion and decrease pain even with severe wear and bone spurs. Performed as outpatient procedures, arthroscopic surgery involves removing any loose bone/cartilage fragments or inflammatory/degenerative tissue in the joint; smoothing out irregular surfaces; and/or removing bone spurs. The procedure allows reasonably quick recovery due to small incisions.

**Joint Replacement:** When joint surfaces have completely worn away, elbow joint replacement may provide relief. There are long-term functional limits — usually, a patient cannot lift more than 5 to 10 pounds with the treated arm — so the decision to have a total elbow replacement should be made carefully.

For younger or highly active patients unsuitable for replacement, surgeons may release stiffness, smooth joint surfaces, or create new surfaces using the patient's own tissue.

## Research on the Horizon
Emerging approaches include cartilage/bone grafts for specific joint damage areas, aiming to restore smooth surfaces and prevent further deterioration. Advancing understanding of cartilage growth and regeneration may enable replacement of larger damaged areas.
