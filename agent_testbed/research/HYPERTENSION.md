# Hypertension: exercise research

First condition for the next phase (`EXERCISE_FIT_PLAN.md`). Researched
2026-10-28 from published guidelines and meta-analyses (sources at the end).
Part A is what the evidence says. Part B is what we propose for our app; it
needs our decision and a clinician's review before it becomes rules.

Scope reminder: who may exercise at all is decided by a colleague. We decide
what an eligible patient with high BP should do, and how much.

## Part A: what the evidence says

### Which exercise lowers BP, and by how much

Edwards et al. 2023, British Journal of Sports Medicine: 270 randomised trials,
15,827 people. Average drop in resting BP after a training programme:

| Exercise type | Systolic | Diastolic | Rank for systolic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isometric (holding a position, e.g. wall sit, hand grip) | −8.2 | −4.0 | 1st |
| Combined aerobic + strength | −6.0 | −2.5 | 2nd |
| Dynamic resistance (strength with movement) | −4.6 | −3.0 | 3rd |
| Aerobic (walking, running, cycling) | −4.5 | −2.5 | 4th |
| High-intensity intervals | −4.1 | −2.5 | 5th |

- Best single exercises: **wall squat (wall sit)** for systolic, **running** for diastolic.
- For comparison, one BP medicine typically lowers systolic by roughly 9–10 mmHg.
- Older reviews (Pescatello, ACSM 2015) put aerobic training at 5–7 mmHg in people who already have high BP: the effect is larger when BP is higher.
- **One session already helps:** after a single aerobic session BP stays 5–7 mmHg lower for up to 24 hours. Even 10–15 minutes of light to moderate activity works.

Other options:

| Option | Effect | How sure |
|---|---|---|
| Yoga (asana + breathing + relaxation), 34 trials | −6.5 / −2.8 | Low quality studies; programmes with all three parts worked best |
| Slow breathing (≤10 breaths a minute), 17 trials | −5.6 / −3.0 | Mixed; a later review found device-guided breathing didn't help |
| Slow breathing at 6 breaths a minute | −9.7 in people ≥130 systolic | Self-reported, not a trial |

### What the guidelines recommend

| Guideline | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **ESC 2024** (European) | At least 150 min a week of moderate aerobic exercise (or 75 min vigorous), **plus dynamic or isometric resistance training 2–3 times a week**. First major guideline to list isometric training. |
| **ACSM** (US, exercise medicine) | Aerobic on most, preferably all, days: 30–60 min a day, moderate (somewhat hard, can still talk), in bouts of 10+ min. Strength 2–3 days a week: 2–3 sets of 10–12 repetitions, 8–10 exercises for the major muscles; bodyweight counts. Combine both. |
| **ACC/AHA 2017** (US) | Aerobic and dynamic resistance; isometric hand grip (e.g. 3 sessions a week) as an option. |
| **API/ICP 2024** (Indian, BP with type 2 diabetes) | Lifestyle change, with **yoga as an add-on therapy**. No exercise doses given. |
| **WHO 2020** (everyone) | 150–300 min moderate activity a week; strength 2+ days; adults 65+ also balance work 3+ days. |

### Safety: what the sources agree on

- **Don't exercise on a very high reading.** Resting BP at or above about **200/110**: don't start; rest 15 minutes, recheck, and if still high see a doctor. Some cardiac programmes use **180/110**. (Sources differ; the exact cut-off is a clinical decision. Whether the patient may exercise at all is the colleague's part.)
- **Never hold the breath while straining** (the Valsalva effect): it sharply raises BP. Breathe out on effort. This matters most for holds (planks, wall sits, isometric neck holds) and lifting.
- **Avoid sudden vigorous effort**, especially without a warm-up. Warm up and cool down for 5–10 minutes, longer for older adults.
- **Use how hard it feels, not heart rate:** BP medicines like beta-blockers (atenolol, metoprolol…) keep the heart rate low, so heart-rate targets don't work. Use the "talk test" (can talk but not sing) or effort 12–13 out of 20 ("somewhat hard").
- **Dizziness after exercise:** several BP medicines (alpha-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, ARBs) can drop BP after exercise. Cool down gradually, don't stop suddenly, stand up slowly.
- **Isometric exercise is less tested for safety.** Trials are mostly in people with controlled or stage 1 BP, with the hold set from a test (wall sit: 4 × 2 minutes, 2 minutes rest, 3 times a week). BP rises during the hold. Authors say safe stopping rules for isometric exercise still need research. A large home-based trial (ISOFITTER) is under way.

## Part B: what we propose for our app (to decide)

### 1. A weekly plan instead of a list

For an eligible patient with high BP:

| Part | What | How often | How much (start → goal) | How hard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aerobic | Walking (or marching on the spot indoors) | 5 days a week | 10–15 min → 30 min a day | Can talk, not sing |
| Strength | 2–3 bodyweight exercises (e.g. sit-to-stand from a chair, wall push-up, glute bridge) | 2–3 days a week | 1–2 sets of 8–10 → 2–3 sets of 10–12 | Somewhat hard, breathing all the time |
| Breathing | Slow breathing, about 6 breaths a minute | Daily | 5 min → 10–15 min | Relaxed |
| Stretching | Our existing stretches | After walking | As today | Gentle |
| Isometric | Wall sit (only when BP is under control) | 3 days a week | To decide with a clinician | Breathing all the time |

### 2. Rules by BP reading, not just "BP under control"

Proposed bands on the latest reading (to be confirmed by a clinician; the
higher bands overlap the colleague's eligibility work, so agree the line with
them):

| Latest BP | What we'd allow |
|---|---|
| Below 140/90 | The full plan above, including the wall sit |
| 140–159 / 90–99 | Plan above, but no isometric holds, moderate effort only, longer warm-up |
| 160–179 / 100–109 | Walking, breathing and gentle mobility only; doctor to review |
| 180/110 or above | No exercise plan: doctor (eligibility) |

### 3. Exercise properties we need to add

To apply these rules to any exercise: **type** (aerobic, strength, isometric,
stretching, breathing), **intensity**, **holds a position** (isometric),
**strain or breath-holding risk**, **head below the heart**, and **position**
(standing, chair, floor).

### 4. Changes to our current 12 exercises

- **Neck Hold, Glute Bridge, Dead Bug:** for BP, "modify" with "breathe normally,
  don't hold your breath" (done 2026-10-28; a "safe" row's note isn't shown).
- **Running:** stays "modify"; not allowed in the 160+ band.
- **Walking:** the core; give it minutes and days, not just km.

### 5. Exercises to add (candidates)

| Exercise | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Wall sit | Isometric | Strongest single exercise for systolic BP |
| Sit-to-stand from a chair | Strength | Bodyweight, safe for older patients, daily-life movement |
| Wall push-up | Strength | Upper-body strength without the floor |
| Marching on the spot | Aerobic | Walking indoors (heat, rain, safety) |
| Slow breathing (6 a minute) | Breathing | Cheap, no risk, some evidence |

### 6. Using the medicines we already have

The clinic record lists medicine names. We could flag beta-blockers (use the
talk test, not heart rate) and BP medicines that can cause dizziness after
exercise (add "cool down slowly"). Needs a small medicine-name list, reviewed
by a clinician.

### 7. Data to check once the clinic DB is back

- How many patients with high BP have a reading, and how recent it is.
- How the latest readings spread across the bands in point 2.
- How many are on beta-blockers or other BP medicines.
